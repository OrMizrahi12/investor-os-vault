## 1. The neglected niche software vendor (near-ideal customers)

**Setup:** A small vendor of administrative software for local governments and small utilities. Revenue down a third from its peak after a botched takeover fight, lawsuits against its own customers and boardroom feuds. Still about 30% of its niche, three times the next rival; the niche too small for large software houses. Share price down over 90% from its high; losing money; a new chief executive just appointed and losses narrowing.

**Discovery:** a regular fundamentals screen (losses shrinking) plus a news search for new chief executives, and industry executives naming it as the company they would most like to own (provided they could also manage it).

**Research:** Management did not return calls (focused on the turnaround). The company listed coordinators of several user groups (one per segment: a good sign). More than a dozen customer conversations over several weeks, then visits. Users were also the decision makers with budget authority; customers had stayed through the turmoil because the product was indispensable; they knew and approved the new plan because the chief executive had visited them; only marginal customers slated to be dropped were lukewarm. Insiders, returning former executives and lower-level employees were buying shares. Armed with customer feedback (including a weakness a competitor was about to exploit), a message to the chief executive got a call back within the hour; then the finance chief. An independent engineer evaluated the product (stable, not about to become obsolete). Large software houses' salespeople, asked openly, said they would never chase such small customers.

**Why it was cheap:** tiny market value (no trading commissions for brokers) and cash-generating (no stock issuance, so no banking fees): no broker followed it.

**Outcome:** accumulated without competition (only insiders were buying); profits recovered, a dividend followed, a creeping takeover was resisted, and a strategic buyer paid about three times the cost within two and a half years.

**Lessons:** look for user groups first; hear at least three key customers; ask customers about their jobs first; ask about the sales cycle and the vendor's people; keep gossip confidential. Imperfections that kept it from ideal: approvals and meetings (months-long sales cycle) and organizational rather than personal benefit to the buyer.

## 2. The great product sold to the wrong buyer, then the right one

**Setup:** A vendor of client-relationship software for securities salespeople, focused on a vertical too small for the market leader. Strong management in all four key functions (general management, marketing, finance, development), cheap stock, a product users liked.

**Research:** Lunches with front-line salespeople revealed the conflict: the product was sold through retail channels to branch managers, who wanted the firm to own client relationships; the salespeople regarded clients as theirs (their wealth included signing bonuses worth about a year's commissions when they moved firms with their clients), kept their own copies and resisted or sabotaged the system. The vendor thought it sold productivity; it was actually selling control over its users. **Decision: no purchase.** The vendor nearly failed.

**Later:** The vendor stopped selling to managers and sold directly to the highest-earning salespeople: more income, and their client data under their own control. Each buyer was user, approver and payer; the benefit was personal and large relative to a modest annual fee; the field was fiercely competitive. Growth approached 100% a year (it even had to issue shares to fund it, which was fine). A second wave followed when firms had to buy the server-side control module. The vendor then went after the market leader's most prestigious accounts; when a regional office of a national firm adopted it and users revolted against headquarters' order to rip it out, the leader bid roughly twenty times revenue for the vendor. Shares were sold progressively on the way up.

**Lessons:** start with the customer and ask whether the buyer and the user want the same thing; seek companies selling directly to single-person decision makers with personal benefit; keep rejected names on file; keep in weekly touch with users and administrative staff at customers during a big position.

## 3. Revenue that the trucks could not carry

**Setup:** A single-plant maker of paving products. An analyst's production-flow model did not reconcile with reported revenue. The finance chief answered with jargon and complex accounting; competitors reacted with pointed silence. A formal written question got a rambling denial and a reminder to "use our guidance".

**Research:** The analyst sat in a diner opposite the plant for two weeks counting outbound trucks and times; a weigh station gave the maximum load; product weight and price gave revenue per truck. Two vetted students continued the count for the rest of the quarter and checked weekly whether the warehouse was filling or emptying. Even at full loads, reported revenue was impossible.

**Aftermath:** The report described the method, offered the logs and forecast a loss against company guidance of a strong profit. The company hosted other analysts with consultants' industry reports, accounting-compliance opinions and a list of typos, never addressing the counts; analysts at firms with banking ties in the sector defended it. A lawyer's letter demanded retraction or an end to coverage. The analyst dropped coverage under protest, reiterated the sell view and disclosed a personal short. Press, then the securities regulator, took an interest; the finance chief left "for health reasons"; the company wrote off and restated; the shares fell to a quarter of their earlier price.

**Lessons:** count physical units where they settle a question; follow controversy; follow denials and attacks on analysts; follow up immediately when a company pressures an analyst to drop coverage; conversely, an analyst capitulating after a long slide can mark a bottom.

## 4. The celebrated discovery that locals laughed at

**Setup:** A resource company whose shares had risen about a hundredfold on a spectacular discovery abroad, now in the index and held by institutions. Shares started falling with no news; then the company announced the death of its chief geologist in an accident and said operations were unaffected; brokers urged buying.

**Research:** Ratios looked fine (they came from the same reports). Calls to local institutions abroad met refusals and laughter. A visit, and friendly conversation with drivers and laborers near the site, revealed politically connected silent partners, samples doctored before assay, and processing facilities built mainly for show.

**Outcome:** The researcher exited the institutional position at once, told no one but a lawyer (who confirmed that local talk, with no theft, trespass or insider source, was usable), and shorted, adding through a squeeze. The shares went to zero.

**Lessons:** laughter and refusals from locals are data; in scams ask rank-and-file, not supervisors; real risk includes physical danger; keep silent; three tests: true, material, exclusive.

## 5. The loan workout seen through a glass door

**Setup:** A bank's loan-workout department occupied offices across a lobby from an index-fund analyst. Workouts either squeeze borrowers (shares fall) or grant debt relief (shares jump).

**Observation:** One day the visiting executives arrived confident while the bankers looked nervous; they left celebrating while the bankers looked defeated. The visitors' company was identified from a logo on clothing and photos in its annual report. It was a creative-services firm whose value lay entirely in its people: a mass-resignation threat left the bank no leverage, so relief was the likely outcome.

**Outcome:** a quick options trade on the announcement multiplied the stake several times.

**Lessons and caution:** body language in public spaces is information; leverage analysis (who can walk away) predicts negotiation outcomes. But a trade timed to a pending announcement and made on observations near the parties is exactly what draws regulatory inquiry; in some jurisdictions it may be prohibited. Document, and take legal advice before acting on anything like it.

## 6. The public boast confirmed four ways

**Setup:** A media interview implied that people close to a money-losing technology company's chief executive expected a large windfall within a month.

**Confirmation:** industry acquaintances mentioned engineers buying shares; the tone in the café where staff gathered was excited; the normally cash-hungry chief executive declined new investment ("not at these prices, wait a month"); and a creditor had been paid ahead of schedule thanks to prepaid orders for a new product.

**Outcome:** the shares rose about sixfold; selling early captured most of it and avoided the later collapse when management bought an expensive business with no revenue.

**Lessons:** a public clue is a lead, never a trade; confirm through independent, legitimate channels; prefer public counterparty records to asking counterparties' staff.

## 7. The beloved technology leader with fragile debt

**Setup:** A maker of the world's fastest computers: dozens of customers worldwide loved it, the campus was full of happy, brilliant people, the chief executive and his lieutenants bought millions of dollars of stock, and the shares were cheap.

**What was missed:** convertible debt with revenue and cash-flow coverage covenants. One delayed order missed the quarter and breached them; part of the debt had been bought by an aggressive fund that refused the usual waiver and demanded warrants, shifting value to creditors. When investor relations called asking whether the researcher would exchange "his bonds", it was clear they did not know who held them and the refinancing was going badly. Risk controls forced a sale at a one-third loss; the obvious next step, shorting, was missed out of relief.

**Lessons:** read filings even when you know the field best; debt is always repaid by someone; avoid companies at the very top of a technology pyramid in a non-growing niche (customers should be satisfied, not ecstatic); insider buying is not infallible; never go for broke; after a mistake, look for the reverse trade.

## 8. The strike that was about respect

**Setup:** A respected optical-equipment maker, usually too expensive, fell on a strike threat over pay. A major foreign customer had warned that a strike would cost the company its order and some jobs; both sides seemed rational. Conversations at the union hall, with the local weekly's reporter, local business owners and the company's negotiator all pointed to a settlement. Shares bought.

**What happened:** the strike passed overwhelmingly; equipment was damaged; the customer left. The cause: management had refused a long-serving worker a modest personal favor, and an executive's contemptuous remark about workers reached the floor. Money became a matter of respect. The shares fell to about book value; the researcher, stung, did not buy back, and they later rose about sevenfold.

**Lessons:** money is only one of the motives that drive people; knowing them does not make them predictable; trivial slights can trigger large actions; after a mistake, keep the stock on your list; a solid old company with a strong balance sheet is worth researching again once it is ridiculously cheap.

## 9. The commodity balance built by hand

**Setup:** A government exploring for a material it was thought to have in plenty suggested scarcity.

**Research:** A list of every consuming plant worldwide (supply sources, consumption, input cost share, key managers), then every producer; visits and relationships with mid-level staff; regulators and processors; a sourced balance table; random-sample physical audits; tracking of ancillary supplies proportional to consumption; review by independent experts. About seven months.

**Finding:** a structural shortage, made worse by new plants under construction; the input was under a tenth of users' operating costs, so even a doubling or tripling of price would not dent demand.

**Action:** bought producers and kept adding as prices rose; exit planned on the physical balance turning or the theme becoming a mass-media cover story.

## 10. The basket of companies below net cash

**Setup:** Ten technology companies trading below net cash, each burning cash and despised after past disappointments.

**Outcome:** sold after a year at about +35%. One failed (and still returned half its cash); two tripled and one rose fivefold after the sale. The businesses were better than assumed.

**Lessons:** in such baskets, verify cost cuts and management integrity first; stopping the burn can force waiting acquirers to bid; research each business before selling the winners.
