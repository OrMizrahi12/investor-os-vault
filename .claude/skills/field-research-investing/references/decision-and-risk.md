## 1. Evidence grading

For each finding record: source tier (filing/public, firsthand observation, interview, inference), date, how obtained, and the three tests:

| Finding | Tier | True? (how confirmed) | Material? (effect on payments, costs, capital, control) | Exclusive? (is it in the price?) |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Rules:
- A finding from one source is a lead; two independent sources make it usable; a physical observation that matches an interview is strong.
- Information from anyone paid to sell (promoters, investor relations, brokers distributing stock) counts as unconfirmed until verified.
- Exclusivity decays: what customers know today, analysts may know next quarter.

## 2. The decision gate

Proceed to a position only if all hold:
1. The business is worth owning (or, for an event bet, the event is well evidenced and the downside bounded).
2. At least three key customers heard firsthand (more for larger positions), and their testimony supports the thesis.
3. The key findings pass all three tests.
4. The price is cheap relative to the value the evidence implies (use whatever valuation discipline the user follows).
5. The trap checklist (§3) is clean or each item is explicitly accepted.
6. The methods used were lawful and documented.

If any test fails: no position. Staying in cash is a legitimate outcome and keeps money ready for the opportunity that does pass.

## 3. The trap checklist (run before every buy)

- [ ] **Public facts read in detail?** Annual and quarterly reports, notes, debt agreements, proxy. Field knowledge makes people overconfident about the filings.
- [ ] **Debt:** covenants (coverage ratios, minimum revenue or cash flow), maturities, conversion terms, who holds the debt (an aggressive holder can seize the equity in a refinancing). In doubt about a bargain with debt: stay out.
- [ ] **Top of the technology pyramid?** A company serving only the very top of a niche that cannot grow, outspending everyone to stay ahead while cheaper rivals climb up from below, is on a treadmill or headed for obsolescence.
- [ ] **Customers satisfied, or ecstatic?** Ecstatic can mean the company is doing them a favor at shareholders' expense.
- [ ] **Role conflicts:** does the product take power or money from the user to give it to the approver (or vice versa)?
- [ ] **Insider buying under a charismatic leader?** Supporting evidence only.
- [ ] **Human factors:** succession, family members being groomed, management-worker contempt, respect issues that money cannot settle.
- [ ] **Concentration:** a single customer, supplier, product or permit whose loss would be fatal.
- [ ] **Competence:** do you understand the product and the economics?
- [ ] **Legal exposure:** anything that could look like MNPI? Documented?
- [ ] **Your own state:** are you buying after a string of wins, or to recover a loss, or out of pride?

## 4. Position sizing

- Deep knowledge justifies a meaningful position: field research is wasted on token holdings. But never so large that being wrong would be ruinous, especially when you feel certain. People and markets do irrational things.
- Stay liquid: the investor who survives is the one never forced to sell.
- Set a maximum loss at which the position is cut and reviewed (risk control saved large losses even after excellent research).
- For baskets of statistically cheap stocks (for example, below net cash), diversify across many names instead of concentrating.

## 5. Buying

- Under-followed stocks can usually be accumulated without competition; if insiders are also buying, you are buying alongside them.
- Add as evidence strengthens and results confirm the thesis, even if the price has risen a little.
- For a company in a temporary problem, buy in stages as the fix shows in the field.

## 6. Monitoring

- Keep the sources warm: customers, users, suppliers, administrative staff at customers. Regular contact (even weekly for an active situation) brings news you would never see otherwise, often volunteered.
- Watch the field signals you identified (product-and-operations.md §7) and the public warning phrases (idea-generation.md §6).
- Re-run the three tests on the thesis each quarter: is the edge still exclusive, or is it now in the price?
- Expect exclusive information to take a year or two to reach the price; a flat position in the meantime is normal and is not by itself a reason to sell.

## 7. Selling

- **Valuation:** nobody buys the bottom or sells the top. Begin scaling out as the gain grows large and the price stops looking cheap; sell to momentum buyers who value growth more than you do.
- **Evidence turns:** customers cooling, competitors gaining, key people leaving, operational slump signals, debt stress.
- **Crowd arrives:** when the theme becomes a mass-media cover story, screen-only investors are piling in; start trimming.
- **Thesis achieved:** a takeover at a full price, a re-rating to fair value.
- **Do not sell a fine business just because it rose:** for excellent companies, long holding compounds value and defers tax. Selling too early is common; before selling winners in a basket, research their businesses (several may be far better than assumed).
- Selling early can also protect you from management's later mistakes (for example, an expensive acquisition at the top).

## 8. Shorts and options

- Shorting suits terminal problems backed by strong physical evidence (fraud, impossible revenue, covenant-driven transfer to creditors). Losses are theoretically unlimited and squeezes can be violent; size small enough to survive a squeeze and add only on confirmation.
- Before shorting or publishing, check the legal points (legal-ethics-guardrails.md §6).
- Short-dated options amplify gains and losses and draw regulators' attention to well-timed trades. Use them only with documented, clearly legitimate evidence, and accept that the premium can expire worthless.

## 9. Learning from mistakes

- Field research reduces but does not remove error. Typical failure modes: ignoring a public fact, trusting insider buying, overweighting customer enthusiasm, assuming people will act in their financial interest, and letting a strong track record breed overconfidence.
- People act on pride, respect and loyalty as well as money; a trivial slight can trigger a large, costly reaction in a tense situation. Knowing the actors does not let you predict them reliably.
- After a mistake, exit to clear your head, but keep the stock on your list. The blow that hurt you may have made it cheap (for a solid company with a strong balance sheet, think about it again near book value). And when a mistake reveals new information (for example, that creditors will take the equity), consider whether the opposite position is now the right one: do not let relief or embarrassment hide the next opportunity.
- Write a short post-mortem: what was known, what was missed, which check would have caught it. Add that check to the trap list.

## 10. Decision memo

Use `assets/decision_memo_template.md`. It must state: the decision; the evidence table; the story (market belief vs evidence, why the gap exists, what closes it); the trap checklist; sizing and maximum loss; monitoring sources and frequency; selling rules; open questions; legal and documentation notes; and that it is analysis of evidence, not personalized advice.
