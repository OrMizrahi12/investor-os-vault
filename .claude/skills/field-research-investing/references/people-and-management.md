## 1. What you want to know about the people

For each key person: ability, motivation, values, ambitions, alliances and rivalries, likely next moves, and how replaceable they are. For the team: whether it is structured for the tasks at hand and whether its members pull in the same direction. If you were buying the whole company you would insist on this; buying a slice with a meaningful share of your savings deserves the same care.

## 2. Motivation: external incentives

- Salary versus bonus; whether the bonus depends on the person's own results or on company results; option and share grants; vesting; and, most telling, shares bought in the open market with the person's own after-tax money.
- Sources: proxy statements and insider filings (public); compensation norms from recruiters and published salary surveys; investor relations or the executives themselves sometimes answer directly.
- Interpretation:
  - Talented people joining at modest salaries with large option grants are betting on the company: continue researching (it is not by itself a reason to buy). A big shared upside also tends to align otherwise conflicting ambitions.
  - Salary-heavy packages for recruits suggest they expect moderate growth; then look harder at internal politics (for example, a contest for the top job).

## 3. Motivation: internal drives

Often more important and harder to see: what the person values, whom they try to please or emulate, what they would do with sudden wealth (retire, start a company, buy a ranch, a plane, a club membership).

Clues from public professional life: career choices, accomplishments, how they describe their work, their choice of words and metaphors. Clues on a visit: what is displayed in their office (a photo with a happy customer at a product delivery versus a photo beside their yacht), the reception area (product samples and patents suggest the leader serves the company; photos of the leader with celebrities suggest the company serves the leader), trade publications versus society or leisure magazines in the waiting area (unless the business itself serves that clientele), the cars in the executive spaces and in the staff lot (well-kept modest cars versus flashy toys; a tidy, well-paid workforce versus neglect), the state of the grounds and the logo.

Treat each clue as one weak data point. Only conclusions supported by several independent clues count.

## 4. Background and track record (professional only)

- Education, employers, roles, results, departures, board seats, professional associations, published work, patents, speeches, interviews.
- Former colleagues can describe ability and working style; an academic supervisor can say whether a technical leader is exceptional or ordinary; a specialist recruiter can summarize professional reputation for a fee.
- Social and status background matters to how teams gel: a team that mixes people of very different status and outlook (for example, polished recruits from big-city finance joining an heir who runs a family firm in a small town) can be productive or explosive. Ask why each person joined.
- Guardrail: stick to professional information. Do not compile home addresses, family details, private habits or personal-life gossip, and do not repeat gossip you hear. If a personal matter becomes public through court records or the press and materially affects someone's ability to lead, it can be weighed, quietly.

## 5. Hypotheses about people, and testing them

Before meeting someone, write hypotheses about their motives (ambition, money, status, roots, loyalty to a former comrade, wanting to rescue a family firm) and what each would imply. In conversation, test them gently with open questions and observe the reaction, revising as you go. This is the same discipline professionals use when they meet someone new: observe, hypothesize, test, revise.

Example question set for a key executive (adapt freely):
- What do they value, what do they consider unimportant?
- Whose approval do they seek; whom do they model themselves on?
- What is their ambition: a bigger bonus, being the top performer, the top job?
- Who are their allies inside; how long have they known each other?
- How do they view newcomers or rivals? Might they leave? If they did, who would replace them, and could that person keep the customers?

## 6. Group dynamics

- Watch the team in action wherever you can be present (annual meeting, investor day, customer event, a tour): who talks to whom, who is treated with disdain, who leads in practice (not always the chief executive), who generates ideas and who vets them.
- Office layout is a clue to influence and priorities: who sits closest to the chief executive (development, sales, finance, public relations). If the task is innovation but sales and development sit far apart and rarely talk, the odds are worse than the market thinks. A leader surrounded by public-relations staff is a warning sign.
- Employees' views of their leaders, heard informally (for example from groups of engineers at lunch), can be strikingly candid. Near-unanimity across several independent groups is significant, whether or not it is fair: a leader who has lost the support of the people who do the real work rarely lasts.
- Family succession: a family member being groomed for the top regardless of ability can be a reason to pass. Cross-check opinions about that person, since gossip is often biased.

## 7. What a site visit shows about people

During any visit, open all your senses and note details a novelist would use to sketch the characters. Mood of staff met on the way (happy, busy, surly). Cleanliness, maintenance, pride in the premises. Who is in the gym or break room at which hours and what they are reading. Write it all down unfiltered right after leaving, before analyzing.

## 8. Reading congruence in conversation

- Compare channels: do tone, emphasis, pauses, face and posture match the words? Hedged verbs ("we *think*"), a skeptical look, or a colleague's silence can contradict an upbeat statement.
- In groups, watch the listeners: nods, frozen faces, a derisive snort.
- Phone calls carry tone and hesitation; written statements carry only words.
- A persistent feeling that "something is off" is a reason to dig, not to buy.

## 9. Building rapport ethically

Rapport comes from genuine interest, sympathetic listening, finding real common ground (shared professional background, a shared interest learned from public sources) and adapting your manner to the person (some respond to a confident, authoritative style, others to an informal one). People spend much of their lives at work and are seldom asked about it; sincere interest opens most doors.

Limits: be honest about who you are and why you are asking; never pose as a customer, job seeker, journalist or anyone else to extract information; never seek or accept confidential information; never pressure or induce anyone to breach their duties. Befriending people to learn about the businesses you may entrust your savings to is legitimate; deceiving them is not.

## 10. Meeting management (last)

- **Timing:** after customers, competitors, suppliers and the product, when you know nearly as much about the business as they do. A large position requires certainty about the human element.
- **Getting the meeting:** annual meetings and investor events; an introduction through investor relations; or a message offering useful customer feedback (customer-research.md §11). Reciprocity is strong among business people.
- **Questions:**
  - Their own background and experience (people rarely resist talking about themselves).
  - Their personal view of the plan to restore profit or growth: explicitly not a forecast and nothing unpublished.
  - The people they brought in, their expertise, and the main challenges.
  - The plan's weak points as they see them.
  - Whether you may speak with the finance chief or another executive.
- Compliment real achievements honestly. Read the person as you go and adjust your questions.
- Afterwards, rate congruence and update the people map. If management is first-rate and honest, that is often the last check before buying; if anything feels wrong, keep researching or pass.

## 11. Insider buying and selling

- A sustained wave of open-market buying by managers and directors, with their own money, is usually a strong signal, stronger still if lower-level employees and returning former executives are buying too.
- It is not infallible: a charismatic leader can convince a whole team to buy into a doomed strategy. Treat insider buying as supporting evidence, never as the thesis.
- Asking insiders admiringly why they have been buying sometimes produces a detailed answer; today most are cautious, rightly, so do not press.
- Executives exercising options and selling soon after aggressive revenue recognition is a classic red flag.

## 12. Promotion, evasion and other management signals

- **Heavy promotion** of the stock: cool off. The less promotional, the more interesting.
- **Not returning calls** is not automatically bad: they may be focused, negotiating something big, or buying stock and not wanting attention.
- **Your research among customers usually triggers a call from the company** (often to check that you are not preparing a takeover). Silence can mean management is heads-down on a turnaround.
- **A chronically cash-hungry leader who suddenly declines new money** ("not at these prices") is a strong positive clue that should be confirmed elsewhere.
- **Answers that avoid the specific question** (jargon, "focus on our guidance", complex accounting explanations) to a concrete operational question: see verification-and-red-flags.md.
- **Physical and social distance between management and workers** (separate perks, contemptuous remarks) creates labor risk that money cannot always fix (events-and-crises.md §5).

## 13. Debriefing with partners

Discuss findings in a private setting with a trusted partner, spouse, colleague or club members. Questions to work through:
- What are each key person's strengths and weaknesses, based on evidence?
- Is the team organized for what the company has to accomplish now?
- Do they work well together and pull in the same direction? If not, is it bad incentives or clashing ambitions?
- What are the internal tensions, factions and coalitions?
- What would change if a particular person left?

Then load yourself with detail until the people come alive in your mind, the way an actor or novelist absorbs a character; a sense of where the story is heading often follows.
