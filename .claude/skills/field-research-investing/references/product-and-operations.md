## 1. See the thing itself

A product is not its name, brochure, the story investor relations tells, or its row in an analyst's comparison table. Many products are partly intangible (a soft drink sells a lifestyle as much as refreshment; sports shoes sell an urban image), but for research purposes get the physical item, hold it, and where safe take it apart. Then trace how raw materials become the product and how the product becomes a customer's payment. Very few investors ever examine the product of a company whose shares they own.

## 2. Buying a sample: the purchase experience

- Buy it as any customer would: call or visit the sales channel and order one unit. If they sell only in packs, ask for a sample; if refused, buy the pack. The cost is trivial next to a loss on the shares.
- On this call you are simply a customer: keep it short and product-focused, and do not invent a business need you do not have.
- Record (it is real evidence about the sales force): how the salesperson builds rapport, product knowledge, enthusiasm, attempts to sell you more, delivery time, whether they called to check it arrived, how problems were handled.
- The first purchase decides whether a customer keeps paying. Everything the company invested (people, design, plant, culture) is wasted if that experience disappoints. Note your own reactions as well as the salesperson's performance.

## 3. Examining the product

| Look at | What it can tell you |
|---|---|
| Finish and build (casting residue, polish, cleanliness, fit) | Manufacturing care and quality control |
| Documentation (paper quality, writing quality) | Attention to detail; who wrote it |
| Design and aesthetics | Even in technical products, appearance affects demand |
| Comparison with rivals' equivalent units | Relative position; claims to test with customers |
| Carrier (courier vs post), addressed by name or generic | Logistics sophistication |
| Ship-from location, packager or distributor marks | Whether manufacturing or packing is outsourced; where the margin sits (design often captures it, contract fabrication less so) |
| Speed of delivery | Efficient systems, or overflowing inventory because orders are scarce (check which) |
| Serial numbers, inspection stamps, date codes | Production sequence and volume hints over successive purchases |

Write down impressions on the product alone and against competitors.

If you cannot judge technical quality yourself, pay an independent specialist to assess it (stability, fitness for purpose, risk of obsolescence within a year or two), or find a knowledgeable intermediary who can talk to experts for you; many experts answer polite questions without charge. The fee is small next to the position.

## 4. The site tour

- After customers, suppliers and the product, ask investor relations for a tour of where the work is done. With polite persistence, most companies agree.
- Bring the product. Point to each part and ask where it was made or processed. Often an engineer is called in: a new contact, and the route the product takes through the plant.
- Walk the route: where parts are made, where they are joined, where the partial product goes next, who does each stage and where they sit, who or what inspects the finished product and where.
- The same approach works for software, publishing, insurance or design work: sequential steps by skilled people passing intangible pieces to each other.
- Detailed questions about operations make staff suspicious (a competitor's spy? a raider? a foreign copier?). Address it at once: explain that you are an investor checking whether the company and its suppliers can meet growth, and offer references from other companies you have researched who can vouch for your discretion. Sincerity usually shows. Once people see you are genuinely interested in their work, you may have to stop them talking.
- Engineers and technical enthusiasts: resist soaking up fascinating detail that has nothing to do with the investment question.
- Do not research operations you cannot understand. Stopping midway because the operation is too complex is fine; specialize in what you can grasp.
- Observe and note people, mood and surroundings too (people-and-management.md §7). Dump all impressions in writing as soon as you leave.

## 5. The production flow map

Sketch the site on a large sheet: where raw materials and people come in (which door, whose trucks), where they are transformed, by whom, in what sequence, where finished goods are stored and leave. Add what happens under unusual volume: where extra staff would sit, where overflow inventory would go (for example a rented trailer behind a building), where temporary workers would park. Then note which of these are visible from public places (a café across the road, a public hill, a side street).

Blank areas are fine at first; fill them in over time. Even a partial map forces physical thinking. For a company with a manageable number of sites, a flow chart of people and materials is worth the effort; for large diversified companies, map only the part that bears on a decisive question.

## 6. Bottlenecks and ramp-up questions

Do not try to learn the whole process in depth. Understand it in general, then focus on:
- the steps that would **bottleneck** in an unexpected volume surge; and
- the **most expensive** steps, which would be shut first in a sudden slump.

Those are where internal events emit external signals.

Ask, casually and as hypotheticals:
- What would happen if volume rose 25%? 50%? doubled? (Choose levels above management's public guidance, such as the previous cycle's peak.)
- Which extra machines, how much extra space, how many more people? Which positions, from where, how fast?
- How did you cope the last time volume spiked? Who was brought in? What was sent outside?
- What would you shut down first if volume fell sharply?

Example of why this matters: if one inspector-packer can handle up to about 1,000 units a day, current output is 800, and guidance (on which every analyst's forecast and the share price rest) is 900, then the arrival of a second packer means volume has jumped far beyond guidance. If you learned on the tour that the second packer is a retired former employee who returns for overtime when volume spikes, later news that this person is back at work is a meaningful, legitimately obtained signal.

Capacity grows in steps: a station, a shift, a building can handle only so much. Big permanent steps (new plant, acquisition, new lease) are announced and therefore priced in. The opportunity lies in **unexpected** changes that surprise management itself: management first verifies the change, then scrambles (overtime and temporary measures for a surge; cost cuts for a slump), and only then revises guidance. The stopgaps come before the announcement.

Public job postings tend to cover professional roles; hiring for production and field roles is often less visible and more informative.

## 7. Surge signals and slump signals

| Surge (demand beyond expectations) | Slump (demand collapsing) |
|---|---|
| Overtime; a second or third shift | Shifts cut; staff lot emptier |
| Staff lot full late at night; late-night food deliveries | Expensive stations idle; lights off in sections |
| Couriers or trucks waiting on site permanently | Fewer outbound trucks; inbound deliveries thinning |
| Temporary storage (trailers, rented warehouse space) | Inventory piling up in yards or rented space |
| Temporary workers, retired staff called back | Temporary staff released; hiring freeze |
| Work sent to outside overflow vendors | Overflow vendors idle; supplier layoffs |
| Suppliers ramping, adding shifts | Suppliers cutting; their trucks idle |

Observe only from public places or through people speaking voluntarily; paid local observers keep dated logs. Single signals mislead (seasonality, maintenance shutdowns): look for several together and confirm with independent sources.

## 8. Overflow vendors

When internal capacity for a step is maxed and a new machine would take months, work is sent outside: to a commercial service shop nearby, even to a competitor at a higher unit cost. Ask during the tour how past peaks were handled; that reveals the overflow vendors. Then watch for activity at those vendors (dedicated delivery runs, a busy yard), or, if one is listed, research it in its own right. Ask vendors about their overall level of activity, not about a client's confidential orders.

## 9. Intangible products and large companies

When the product is intangible (designs, financial products, media) or the business is a sprawling multi-division group, tracing the production route is rarely feasible except for one decisive fact. Even then, look for physical traces of major change: hiring and departures, office space taken or released, travel and event activity, outsourcing contracts, server or data-center build-outs, and customers' own preparations (training, seating, integration projects).

## 10. Keeping it in proportion

- Detailed operational research is mostly a tool for medium-term trades around sudden changes. Sometimes it identifies an out-of-favor healthy business before its recovery is visible.
- The serious money is usually made by holding excellent businesses for years. For those, spend your time on people questions; check operations occasionally for obvious flaws, the way a good chief executive judges division heads by results without spying on them.
- Use operational detail to time the purchase of a great company in temporary trouble: judgment picks the company; the field signals pick the moment.
