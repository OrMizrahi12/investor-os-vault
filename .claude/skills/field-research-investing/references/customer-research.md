## 1. Why customers come first

Analysts usually start with the company, its product or its financials. Start instead with the people who send the money. They decide whether payments grow, they often know of their own future purchases before the vendor does, and what they tell you is generally outside information about their own intentions rather than inside information about the vendor (confirm the boundary in legal-ethics-guardrails.md). Very few professional investors talk to customers systematically: a fund holding a hundred stocks cannot, and many simply do not.

Ideal benchmark: know at least what a newly appointed head of sales would demand on day one: who the five biggest customers are, which individuals inside them decide to buy, their role and spending authority, how the purchase decision is made (alone or in committee, who sits on it, how often it meets), how the product helps each of them personally, and what they care about. You will not get all of it; aim for it anyway. Gather professional information only (rule 2 in SKILL.md).

## 2. The four customer questions

Answer each at two levels: the customer organization and the individual inside it.

1. **Who are the customers?** The organizations that buy, and the specific people who decide.
2. **What do they really get for the money?** Sometimes not what the vendor thinks: a feature, a career safety net, control over subordinates, status, convenience, a lifestyle.
3. **What exactly is the company selling?** To the individual decision maker and to the employer. These can differ.
4. **Why choose this vendor over the others?** Organizational reasons and personal reasons. Sometimes the true answer is one nobody would write down.

True answers to these four questions explain most of a business. They do not tell you whether the business is worth owning; §4 does.

## 3. The three buying roles

Paying is sensitive, so organizations usually split it among three people:

1. **Users and evaluators**, who recommend buying.
2. **Approvers**, who have the power to decide.
3. **Payers**, the purchasing or finance staff who release the money.

Find all three for each important customer. Track how fast each acts, whether they cooperate, and whether the vendor's salespeople reach them directly or through intermediaries.

## 4. Is the customer worth serving?

Unworthy customers: no individual has decision power; decisions are made by group-think in committees; they pay little and late; they are disloyal and switch at the first opportunity; serving them requires the vendor to fund their bureaucracy (for example, complex systems sold to very large institutions, where committees take more than a year to decide). Companies keep serving such customers out of inertia, habit and the belief that the customer is always right.

Worthy customers have real decision power, pay promptly, value what they buy, and stay. A company serving worthy customers in a niche too small for giants to bother with can be a wonderful business even when badly managed for a while.

## 5. The customer quality ladder (scoring)

Score each major customer relationship 0-2 on each line (0 = no, 1 = partly, 2 = yes):

| Criterion | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| The user, approver and payer are the same person | No internal friction; near-instant decisions |
| The vendor sells directly to that person (no distributor) | No margin shared with middlemen; direct feedback |
| The product serves the buyer's personal interest (income, bonus, career, standing), not only the employer's | The buyer pushes the purchase through |
| The buyer works in an intensely competitive field where the product gives an edge | Peers must follow or fall behind; adoption spreads |
| The buyer's income is high and tied to performance the product improves | Willingness and ability to pay |
| The cost is low relative to the personal benefit | Quick yes |
| Decisions are fast (days or weeks, not quarters) | Revenue arrives sooner; less selling cost |
| Payment is prompt | Cash flow; less working capital |
| Customers stayed through the vendor's bad times because they had no real alternative | Evidence of a franchise |

Interpretation: 15-18 is close to ideal (such businesses can grow explosively); 10-14 is good (a strong niche franchise); below 8, be very cautious. Explain scores with evidence from conversations.

- **Near-ideal:** users recommend quickly, approvers approve quickly, payers pay quickly, all three work closely, and the vendor reaches them directly. Typical of niche software or equipment where the user is also a manager with budget authority.
- **Ideal:** one person is user, approver and payer, reached directly, and the product meets that person's own needs at least as much as the employer's. A sales pitch that, translated plainly, says "this will raise your personal income by a third, and it costs you a small fraction of that" sells itself, especially in fiercely competitive professions.

## 6. The buyer's personal benefit

- When an individual with buying authority benefits personally (higher pay, a bonus, a promotion, a stronger negotiating position, credit with voters or board), they will buy even if part of the value shifts from their employer to the vendor. It is the employer's job to align its staff's interests; when it fails, people take care of their own families first.
- Products like these are often among the quickest-growing and highest-margin products in any market. Few business courses teach this; salespeople know it well.
- Organizational benefit only (a city's better tax tracking) still works, especially if users earn career credit from it, but adoption is slower.

## 7. Conflicts between roles (the hidden failure mode)

A superb product can fail if it is sold to the wrong person. Pattern: software that made front-line sales staff more productive was sold, through retail channels, to their managers. Managers bought it because it gave the firm control over the staff's client relationships. The staff, who regarded the clients as their own and whose wealth depended on being able to take them along when changing employers, quietly refused to use it, kept their own records and even sabotaged the system. The vendor nearly failed. Later it sold directly to the best-paid front-line staff instead, as a tool that raised their income and kept *them* in control of their clients; growth exploded, and the managers were then forced to buy a companion control product, a second wave of growth.

Checks:
- For each customer: what does the user gain or lose? the approver? the payer? the user's boss?
- Does adopting the product transfer power or wealth from one of them to another? Who loses, and can they block or sabotage it?
- Is the vendor selling to the person who benefits most, directly?
- Ask users (not managers) how much they actually use it, and whether colleagues do.

Only conversations with end users reveal this. No report will.

## 8. Sales cycle and payment mechanics

Ask, for each customer:
- How did the product first come to their attention?
- How long from first contact to purchase?
- Who approved it? Who resisted, and why?
- What alternatives were considered, and why was this one chosen? (Ask this last; see §9.)
- What follow-up did the vendor do after the sale?
- How is it paid: a one-time capital purchase (slow, needs higher approval, depreciated) or a pay-as-you-go expense (faster, often within a division head's authority)? How long does payment take after delivery?

## 9. Running customer conversations

(Full question set in interview-guides.md §1.)

- **Finding customers:** user groups (one per market segment is a good sign; coordinators are often listed publicly), customer conferences, reference lists in releases, case studies, procurement records, review sites, and each customer's referrals to peers ("who else uses it, may I call them?").
- **Opening:** say honestly that you are an investor researching a company whose product they use and that their view would help. Many people then ask how they can help; if not, ask whether they mind.
- **First question: their job.** Ask what they do and how the product fits into their work and helps them. Then stop talking. People are rarely asked about their work and will say a great deal. Their answer also tells you whether they are user, approver or payer, whether the benefit is corporate or personal, and whether this customer is worth serving.
- **Clarifiers:** how they came to the job, who they work with, how they heard of the product, who else uses it (names), whether you may contact them, peers elsewhere who use it.
- **The "why did you choose it" question last.** Asked early, it gets the official answer; personal advantages, especially those at the employer's slight expense, surface only after trust.
- **If the phone is not for you:** meet in person, or have a socially gifted helper make the calls. Meeting a few customers in person also shows how the product is used: where users sit, what they do, whom they deal with.
- **Volume:** a strong case usually rests on more than a dozen customer conversations over several weeks, followed by a few visits. Three is the absolute floor.

## 10. What customers reveal beyond the product

Listen for the company's story as customers tell it: why it fell into trouble (founders feuding with the board, a failed takeover, lawsuits against its own customers, loss of focus), what the new leadership is doing, who matters inside the vendor, alliances and conflicts. Useful signals:
- Customers stayed through the turmoil because they had no real alternative: a franchise.
- Customers know and approve the new chief executive's plan because the chief executive visited them personally; the only lukewarm ones are marginal customers the plan intends to drop.
- Former staff returning, and customers celebrating it.
- Lower-level employees buying shares alongside insiders.

Keep all of it confidential; never relay gossip between the company and its customers.

## 11. Using customer knowledge with management

When management will not return calls (common, and not necessarily negative: they may be focused, busy with something big, or buying stock themselves), position yourself where the company's revenue flows. Anyone close to the customer relationship (top salespeople, distributors, key intermediaries) gets management's attention. Once you know the customers well, a message such as "I have spoken with several of your customers; they rate you highly, but a few mentioned a shortcoming a competitor is preparing to exploit" usually gets a prompt call back.

Rules: never harm the company or its customer relationships; share only unresolved issues whose resolution helps both sides, without naming individual sources; behave so that both sides come to see you as a trustworthy, helpful shareholder.

## 12. Warning signs from customers

- Customers *ecstatic* rather than satisfied, in a narrow high-end niche where the vendor must keep spending more to stay on top while cheaper rivals take the lower tiers: the vendor may be doing customers a favor at shareholders' expense.
- Purchases decided by committees of junior staff with no real authority.
- Users who do not actually use the product, keep parallel records, or resent it.
- Customers paying through distributors who could redirect business.
- Defections, or rumors of a key customer pulling out (see interview-guides.md for confirming).
- A key customer skipping the vendor's customer conference it was meant to headline.
