# Legal and ethical guardrails

Not legal advice. Laws differ by country and change; anyone acting on field research should consult a securities lawyer in their jurisdiction, especially before trading on findings that could later look prescient.

## 1. The principle

The edge in this method comes from doing legitimate work others skip: talking openly with people about their own experience, looking at what anyone could see from public places, buying and examining products, counting, reading obscure public records, and assembling these pieces with your own analysis. It never comes from obtaining what someone is obliged to keep confidential, from deception, or from intruding on people's private lives. Ethics and self-interest align here: sources who are treated honestly and discreetly keep talking, and documented, legitimate methods protect the gains.

## 2. Inside information: where the lines are

- **Material non-public information (MNPI):** information a reasonable investor would consider important that has not been disclosed to the market.
- **United States (general outline):** trading on MNPI is illegal when it involves a breach of a duty of trust or confidence: by insiders and temporary insiders (officers, employees, lawyers, bankers, accountants, consultants); by outsiders who misappropriate information entrusted to them (for example by an employer, client or family member who shared it in confidence); and by "tippees" who receive information from someone who breached a duty for a personal benefit (which can include a gift to a friend or relative). Obtaining information by deception can itself create liability.
- **European Union and United Kingdom (general outline):** the prohibition is broader and does not require a breach of duty: anyone who has inside information and knows or ought to know it is inside information must not deal on it. Analyses and conclusions drawn from publicly available information are generally not inside information, but precise information about the issuer or a pending event that you happen to learn may be.
- **The mosaic approach:** combining public information with non-material non-public observations (store traffic, delivery activity, product quality, customer satisfaction) into a conclusion is accepted research practice. The risk rises when one piece is itself material and confidential.
- **Customers' statements about their own plans** are generally their information to share, not the vendor's inside information. They become problematic when a customer reveals confidential details of the vendor's business, or when the customer has agreed to keep the relationship confidential.
- **Issuers may not selectively disclose:** companies are generally barred from giving investors MNPI privately. Never ask management for forecasts, unpublished results or deal information, and if they slip, do not trade until it is public.

## 3. Deals and tender offers

- In the US, once steps toward a tender offer have begun, trading while in possession of material non-public information about it that you know (or have reason to know) came from the bidder, the target or their advisers is prohibited even without any breach of duty.
- Observing in public that well-known deal advisers are meeting the executives of a cheap company, then reasoning that a deal may be coming, is very different from learning details from someone involved. Even so, a well-timed trade in short-dated options will draw surveillance inquiries: document how you reached your conclusion *before* trading and get legal advice.
- Never seek information from staff of law firms, banks, printers, caterers, couriers or other service providers about their clients' deals.

## 4. Honesty: no pretexting

- Always identify yourself truthfully as an investor or as someone researching on an investor's behalf. Do not pose as a customer, supplier, job applicant, journalist, student, event planner or prospective bidder to get information.
- Do not issue fictitious requests for proposals or fake inquiries to competitors to test their interest; ask openly.
- Buying a product sample as a genuine customer is fine (you are a customer); using that cover to extract confidential information is not.
- Lying to obtain information can breach fraud, privacy and securities laws (for example, obtaining customer information from financial institutions under false pretenses is specifically illegal in the US), and it destroys sources' trust.

## 5. No payments or inducements for confidential information

- Do not pay, tip, or give gifts to anyone in exchange for information about a company they work for or serve, or for alerts about its internal events. This can be commercial bribery and can make any trade on the information illegal (the payment itself is a personal benefit to the person breaching confidence).
- Paying people for legitimate work is fine: observers counting trucks from a public road, an engineer evaluating a product you bought, a recruiter summarizing an executive's public professional reputation, a researcher compiling public records.
- Paid experts: use networks with compliance controls; do not consult current employees of the company (or of its key counterparties) about their employer; never ask an expert for confidential information of a current or former employer or client.

## 6. Publishing research and short selling

- Publish only verifiable facts and clearly labeled opinions; describe your method; disclose your position; have a lawyer review before publication. Avoid alleging fraud or motive unless proven.
- False or misleading statements to move a price are market manipulation; so is spreading rumors you know are false.
- Check short-selling rules in your market (borrow/locate requirements, disclosure thresholds for net short positions) and any rules on trading around your own publications.
- Expect legal threats from the company; good documentation is the defense.

## 7. Privacy and surveillance

- Research people's professional roles, records and public statements only. Do not compile home addresses, family details, health, relationships or private habits, and do not photograph, follow or stake out individuals. Persistent following or photographing can amount to stalking or harassment, and in some jurisdictions (for example under EU data-protection law) compiling personal data about individuals needs a lawful basis you are unlikely to have.
- Observing sites from public places (traffic, loading docks, parking lots, buildings) is generally lawful; never trespass, never enter premises under false pretenses, never use devices to intrude (drones over private property, listening devices).
- Recording calls: some places require consent from all parties; know the rule before recording.
- Overheard conversations: general mood (excited, gloomy) is a soft signal; do not deliberately eavesdrop to capture specific confidential details, and do not trade on such details (in some jurisdictions it may be prohibited regardless of how you heard it).
- Keep whatever personal information you do encounter confidential; never use it to embarrass or pressure anyone.

## 8. Documentation

- Log each contact and observation with date, time, place, method and who was present (`assets/interview_log_template.md`).
- Time-stamp photographs (of places, not people) and notes; have a colleague witness and initial notes for important observations.
- Before trading on a surprising finding, write a memo to file (or email to your lawyer) describing how it was obtained.
- Keep a record of questions put to your lawyer and the advice given, with dates.
- Assume a regulator may someday ask you to show exactly how you learned what you knew.

## 9. Compliant alternatives to risky tactics

| Tempting tactic | Problem | Compliant alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Posing as a customer, planner or peer company to ask staff questions | Pretexting; deception | Introduce yourself as an investor; ask about their work and views |
| Fictitious tender to see if a big rival would bid | Deception | Ask the rival's salespeople openly whether they pursue customers of that size |
| Paying a local business to alert you when a company holds a celebration, orders supplies or receives visitors | Payment for information about internal events; possible MNPI chain | Watch public signals yourself (or via paid observers on public property); rely on voluntary conversation |
| Following or photographing executives or deal advisers to see whom they meet | Harassment risk; privacy; deal-information rules | Note chance public observations; research advisers' public deal records; watch sites, not people |
| Building dossiers with home addresses, spouses and children | Privacy harm; legal risk | Professional profiles only |
| Asking a creditor's or bank's staff about a borrower's payments or negotiations | Soliciting confidential information | Use creditors' public filings and statements |
| Getting invited inside to glimpse documents or overhear meetings | Misappropriation; trespass in spirit | Ask for an ordinary tour; observe what is shown to visitors |
| Trading short-dated options right after observing a likely deal meeting | Deal rules; heavy surveillance | Stop; document; get legal advice before any trade |
| Recording conversations secretly | Consent laws | Take notes; record only with consent where required |

## 10. If you stumble onto inside information

1. Stop the conversation's line of questioning; do not ask follow-ups.
2. Write down what was said, by whom, when, and how it came up.
3. Do not trade the company (or its close counterparties) and do not pass the information on.
4. Consult counsel; if you work at a firm, inform compliance at once.
5. Resume only when the information is public or counsel clears you.

## 11. Professionals and firms

Registered professionals must also follow their firm's research, communication and personal-trading policies (restricted lists, blackout windows around research publication, pre-clearance, expert-network procedures, recordkeeping). Integrate legal advice into the research routine; budget for it as a normal cost of this kind of work.
