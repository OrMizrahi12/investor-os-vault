## 1. What a company really is

- Not its charter, filings, logo or price history. A company is a team of people whose work (physical, mental, creative) customers pay for.
- The stock rises over time when the group does work customers value more than alternatives and pays for more of it; it falls when payments shrink or shift elsewhere.
- Therefore every piece of exclusive information you seek is, in the end, about customer payments: their size, timing, durability and direction.
- Three places hold that information: the **people** (inside the company, at its customers, at its suppliers and advisers, and peripheral observers such as competitors' staff, former colleagues of executives, local officials and local business people), the **product** (its own output, the inputs it consumes, rival products), and the **plant and periphery** (sites and everything around them that leaves traces: traffic, parking, storage, permits, hiring, local talk).
- Every company is an ongoing human drama: characters, ambitions, alliances, rivalries, a past and a future. Models that average people into numbers miss the specific drama that decides outcomes.

## 2. Information versus symbols

- Most investors treat "information" as numbers and words collected by others (filings, news, databases, broker reports). These are summaries of reality, like a map is a summary of terrain. Maps have properties the terrain lacks and miss what the terrain has.
- Humans communicate on many channels at once: words, tone, emphasis, pauses, face, posture, what others in the room do while someone speaks. Converting that to text throws most of it away.
- The same sentence ("we think next year will be good") read in a release and heard in person, with stress on "think" and a skeptical glance from a colleague, can lead to opposite conclusions.
- Firsthand, physical facts (what you saw, counted, handled, heard) are hard to obtain, which is exactly why they can be exclusive.
- Price fluctuations describe how a stock travels toward its value; they are not the value. Studying the jitter is descriptive, not useful for choosing what to own.
- Purely numerical models cannot see characters, conflict or execution. When such models fail to predict outcomes, the right conclusion is that they measure the wrong things, not that outcomes are random.

## 3. The three tests of investment information

Use these as a gate on every finding and on every decision.

| Test | Passes when | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| **True** | Confirmed by independent sources or by direct observation | Comes from someone paid to sell (promoters, investor-relations spin, brokers distributing stock), or is unconfirmed rumor |
| **Material** | It changes future customer payments, costs, capital structure or control | It is statistically precise but irrelevant (price-volatility measures, decorative charts) |
| **Exclusive** | Few or no other investors know it yet, so the price does not reflect it | It is in filings, the press, broker notes or widely discussed online |

- Judging materiality requires knowing the industry; learn it by talking to its participants, not only by reading. You do not need encyclopedic depth: knowing meaningfully more than investors who buy the whole index (who know almost nothing about each company) is already an advantage.
- If one test fails, do nothing. Holding cash for months until a qualifying opportunity appears is a sound policy. The worst error is losing money; the next worst is tying money up in mediocre ideas and lacking cash when a real edge appears.

## 4. Where the edge comes from, and who leaves it on the table

- Many institutions invest on public information alone: index funds by design, large funds holding a hundred names they cannot study deeply, and many hedge funds that watch the same terminals and the same consensus estimates. Quantitative models work on the same symbolic data; some earn steady profits until a rare event wipes out years of gains, like underpricing catastrophe insurance.
- Big bets on public facts (for example a macro view anyone could form) carry risk without edge.
- Many large active managers lag the indexes partly because they buy famous, expensive stocks that analysts promote (often while their firms help those companies raise money) and then sell the same stocks after they fall from grace and become cheap.
- Analysts at brokers depend on trading and underwriting revenue and on access to management. They tend to cover large, liquid companies that issue securities, avoid offending management, and soften negative views. Small, cash-rich companies that never need to raise money are under-covered. That neglect is an opportunity: when you find something good there, you face little competition for the shares.
- Exceptional investors can win on public data through superior judgment. For everyone else, gathering exclusive firsthand facts is the practical route.

## 5. Public data: necessary, rarely sufficient

- Study the public record first and in detail (filings, proxy, debt terms, insider transactions, legal proceedings). Ignorance of public facts is a classic way for a well-researched position to fail.
- Then do not act on public data alone. Use it to decide what to investigate and to frame questions.
- A public fact is sometimes the start of an exclusive trail (a magazine remark about a firm's habits, a radio interview, a job ad). The edge comes from the follow-up others do not do.

## 6. Reading people: congruence and gut feeling

- A message is *congruent* when all channels agree (words, tone, face, posture, the reactions of others present) and *incongruent* when they conflict. Everyone can sense incongruence; it often surfaces as a vague unease.
- Treat that unease as data: it summarizes many cues your conscious analysis missed. Investigate it before you invest.
- In groups the channels multiply: watch who nods, who freezes, who smirks, who looks at whom.
- Be wary of any situation where you are pressed to decide quickly on a single channel (a phone pitch, a brochure, a screen) and discouraged from checking in person or asking others. Walk away; there will be other opportunities. For important decisions, add the views of several trusted people to your own.

## 7. Concentration, holding period and taxes

- Better to know nearly everything about a few superb companies than a little about many mediocre ones. Deep knowledge allows a meaningful position and the nerve to hold it through price swings.
- Very few businesses deserve long-term ownership. A lifetime of investing may need only a couple of dozen great decisions.
- The best returns usually come from buying an excellent business during a temporary setback and holding it for years: compounding continues, taxes are deferred, and knowledge deepens.
- Buying very cheap but mediocre assets for a quick re-rating works, but it demands constant fresh ideas and frequent taxable sales.
- Event-driven field research (takeovers, sudden contracts, collapses) can pay well but is a trader's game: more work, higher taxes, more legal care.

## 8. Judgment over technique

- Terrific businesses serve worthwhile customers and are run by capable, honest people. Think like a chief executive who hires the best division heads and judges them by results: if you need many inside observers to keep a business on track, the business or its managers are the problem.
- Spend most research time on people questions (customers, incentives, leadership) and use detailed operational research only to time a purchase or to catch obvious flaws.
- The discipline not to do something merely because you can (a clever trick, a fascinating technical rabbit hole) is part of the craft.
- Stay inside your circle of competence. Stopping a project because the product is beyond your understanding is not a failure.

## 9. Humility and self-management

- A run of successes inflates confidence; that is when simple public facts get skipped.
- People and organizations act on pride, respect, fear and loyalty as well as money. Knowing the actors well still does not let you predict them reliably.
- Never put so much in one position that being wrong would be ruinous, especially when you feel sure.
- Insider buying is usually a good sign, not a guarantee: a charismatic leader can carry a whole team into a bad bet.
- When you exit a mistake, clear your head but keep watching the stock. The lesson you paid for is worth using, including reversing your position if the evidence now points the other way.
- Keep what you learn to yourself. Talking about a discovery hands your edge to others, raises the price you must pay, and burns your sources.

## 10. The owner's mindset

- The aim is to act like a principal (working for yourself and your family, choosing what to pursue) rather than an agent working to someone else's order. Investing well can make work optional.
- Once wealth accumulates, separate ownership, control and benefit (for example through holding structures or trusts set up with a qualified lawyer and tax adviser) to reduce exposure to taxes, claims and sudden pressures. Interestingly, the ideal investee is a company whose *customers* keep those roles in one person's hands: the person who uses the product also decides and pays.
- Wealth also allows giving. One satisfying approach is to look for real needs directly, with the same listening skills, and give anonymously through an intermediary, in addition to established charities.
