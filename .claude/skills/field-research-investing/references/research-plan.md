## 1. Gate the company before spending effort

Answer these first; a "no" means stop or reclassify as an event trade.

1. **Is this business worth doing at all?** Warning signs: chronically low returns on capital, deep cyclicality with no pricing power, no control over costs, heavy fixed obligations (pensions, union rigidity), capital-hungry rivalry where competitors cut prices irrationally. Some whole industries have destroyed capital for decades (airlines are the classic example; so were legacy manufacturers carrying huge pension and labor costs against low-cost foreign rivals); cheapness does not fix that.
2. **Would I be content to own it indefinitely?**
3. **Are its customers worth serving?** (customer-research.md §4)
4. **Do I understand the product and how it is made and sold?** If not, either find a trustworthy expert to bridge the gap or move on.
5. **Is the company researchable?** Smaller, focused companies with one or a few sites, few key products and few key customers are far easier. Large diversified companies can be researched only on one or two decisive questions.
6. **Is there a reason to expect mispricing?** (idea-generation.md §3-4). Without one, even good research rarely pays.

## 2. Public baseline checklist

Do this in detail before any calls. Record source and date for each item.

- **Business:** segments, products, revenue by product and region, top customers and concentration, distribution channels, backlog, seasonality.
- **People:** officers and directors, their ages, backgrounds and tenure; key salespeople, engineers and product managers named in releases; recent departures and arrivals.
- **Incentives:** salary, bonus formula (tied to own performance or company results), option and share grants, open-market purchases and sales by insiders.
- **Capital structure:** every debt instrument, maturity, covenant (coverage ratios, minimum revenue or cash flow), security, conversion terms, and as far as can be learned who holds it. Off-balance-sheet obligations, pensions, leases.
- **Accounting:** revenue recognition, receivables and inventory trends versus sales, capitalized costs, one-off charges that recur, auditor and any changes, restatements.
- **Legal and regulatory:** proceedings, investigations, permits, environmental filings.
- **Ownership:** large holders, activists, creeping accumulation, recent new fund owners or sellers.
- **Coverage:** which analysts follow it, their incentives, rating history, any dropped coverage and why. Asking analysts who used to cover a stock why they stopped often reveals broker economics (too small to trade, no fund-raising ahead) rather than a business problem.
- **Physical footprint:** headquarters, plants, warehouses, distribution hubs, offices, where staff eat and gather, transport routes to major customers.
- **Community:** user groups and their coordinators, customer conferences, trade associations, local chambers of commerce, local press.

*Modern equivalents:* filings databases with full-text search; current-report items for officer changes, auditor changes and non-reliance; insider and ownership filings; debt indentures and credit agreements filed as exhibits; lien and secured-transaction registers; court dockets; municipal permits (building, zoning, discharge) and planning-board minutes; job postings; professional networking profiles; review sites and user forums.

## 3. The research brief (information objectives)

Write it down before contact, the way a commander hands an intelligence chief a list of objectives. Use `assets/research_brief_template.md`.

- **The company's critical tasks now:** the two to five things it must get right in the next one to three years (for example: win back defecting customers, launch the next product version, renegotiate debt, fix a plant).
- **Key questions (5-10):** each tied to a critical task and phrased so a source could answer it. Examples: Is the new chief executive's plan working? Do customers accept it? What are the odds of a return to profit? Is the product transition on schedule? Would a large competitor bother with this niche?
- **Expectation:** answering half the questions is normal. Do not worry if the questions are imperfect: working on any reasonably important question teaches you the business quickly, and the real key questions emerge fast.
- **Kill criteria:** the findings that would end the project (for example: customers would switch easily; the buyer is a committee that takes a year; covenants bind within two quarters).
- **Sources and owners:** who will contact which kind of source; what you will look at physically.
- **Time and money budget:** proportional to the position you might take. Legal advice, travel and calls are normal costs of this work (in some tax systems deductible when documented; check locally). Paying a specialist (an engineer to evaluate a product, a recruiter to brief you on an executive's professional reputation, a lawyer to review your methods) is cheap relative to a large position.

## 4. Mapping the people to reach

List roles, then names where public. Describe unnamed roles precisely ("lead salesperson for product X in the western region", "the person negotiating the debt rescheduling with the lending bank").

- **Inside:** executives and directors; the best salespeople on the top products; engineers working on the next versions of the main or most hoped-for products; anyone attached to a critical task.
- **Customers:** for the customers that bring the most revenue and the most growth potential, the individuals who use, approve and pay (customer-research.md §3). For consumer products, a representative sample of buyers and retailers.
- **Suppliers and service providers:** major input suppliers (especially those for whom the company is a large share of business), contract manufacturers, logistics providers, bankers, auditors, law firms.
- **Peripherals:** competitors' product managers and salespeople, former employees, former colleagues of key executives, academic advisers of key technical staff, industry recruiters, trade-press reporters, local chamber-of-commerce staff, local bankers, commercial real-estate agents, local free-paper reporters, university staff.

## 5. Order of work and time budget

1. Public baseline (days).
2. User groups and customer communities; then customer conversations (weeks). Hear at least three key customers before any conclusion; a dozen or more for a large position.
3. Competitors and customers who switched (in or out).
4. Suppliers and service providers.
5. The product itself (buy and examine).
6. Site visit or tour; production map.
7. Management, last, bringing useful feedback.
8. Synthesis and decision.

A full campaign typically runs from a few weeks to about three months. Run two or three candidates in parallel: expect to buy one, drop another as its flaws appear, and keep the third on a watch list. Keep rejected names on file: companies change (a vendor that sold to the wrong customers can later fix that), and your contacts may bring news unprompted.

## 6. Delegation and helpers

- Not everyone enjoys talking to strangers. Relatives, investment-club members, assistants and junior colleagues who are naturally curious about people can be excellent researchers; the skill is learnable and unrelated to formal education. People who are good at it: salespeople, former brokers used to cold calls, detectives, lawyers skilled at gentle questioning, human-resources staff, psychologists, and anyone genuinely fascinated by others.
- Assistants often get further with their counterparts at a target company than their bosses do.
- Some people prefer the phone (strangers often say more to a voice than to a face, and an obviously large investor in the room can make people cautious); others are better in person. Use each person's strength.
- Hired observers (for counts at public vantage points) must be reliable and paid by the user, keep time-stamped logs, and never trespass or approach employees under a false story. Brief them on the legal guardrails.
- A trusted partner to debrief with is essential. Others spot implications you missed.

## 7. Records and documentation

- Keep a dated log of every contact and observation (`assets/interview_log_template.md`): who (role), how contacted, what was said, verbatim key phrases, manner, what you observed, follow-ups.
- Immediately after a visit, write an unstructured dump of everything you noticed before judging what matters; analyze later, alone and with partners.
- For anything that might later look prescient, document that it was obtained legitimately: time-stamped notes and photographs of public scenes, witnesses where practical, and a note to your lawyer before acting on unusual findings. Work as though you may someday need to demonstrate that every step was clean.
- Store personal and sensitive material securely and never repeat it.

## 8. When to stop

- A kill criterion is met.
- After enough detail, you have a strong intuitive sense that there is "no deal here": disengage. Time already spent is easier to give up than money.
- Something makes you uneasy and further work does not resolve it: decisions not to buy are as valuable as decisions to buy.
- The work requires a method you cannot do lawfully (legal-ethics-guardrails.md).
