## 0. Rules for every conversation

- **Identify yourself truthfully:** an investor (or a researcher working for one) studying the company or industry. Never pose as a customer, supplier, job candidate, journalist or student to get information.
- **Ask about them first.** Their job, how long, what it involves. Then listen. Most people are rarely asked about their work.
- **Open questions, then clarifiers.** Save sensitive questions (why they really bought, who resisted, what went wrong) for late in the conversation.
- **No confidential information.** Do not ask for unpublished financials, forecasts, deal discussions, specific confidential orders, or anything they are bound to keep private. If it is offered, steer away and note it (legal-ethics-guardrails.md §2).
- **Ask for the next person.** "Who else should I talk to? May I mention you?"
- **Phone or in person:** choose what suits you and the source; in person shows more, phone sometimes gets franker answers.
- **Write it down at once** (interview log template). Record manner and congruence, not only content.
- **Keep it confidential.** Never repeat one source's remarks to another in a way that identifies them.

## 1. Customer (user, approver, payer)

Opening: "I'm an investor researching [company]; you use its product, and your experience would really help me understand it. Would you mind a few questions?"

1. What is your role? What does a normal week involve?
2. How does [product] fit into your work? What does it help you do better?
3. How long have you used it? How did you first hear of it?
4. Who else in your organization uses it? Who works with you on it? (names, roles; may I contact them?)
5. Who made the decision to buy? Who signed off on the money? How is it paid (one-time capital purchase or ongoing expense)? How quickly is it paid?
6. How long did the decision take? Were there meetings or committees?
7. Was there resistance? From whom, and why?
8. What did the vendor do after the sale? How is support?
9. How do you see the vendor's people: salesperson, support, leadership? Any changes lately?
10. What would make you buy more? What would make you switch? Is there a real alternative?
11. What do you hear from peers at other organizations? Who else uses it? (referrals)
12. *(Late)* Looking back, what tipped the decision toward this product? Did it make a difference to you personally (workload, recognition, career)?
13. *(Turnaround cases)* Do you know the new leadership's plan? What do you think of it? Did anyone from the company explain it to you?

Score afterward on the customer quality ladder (customer-research.md §5).

## 2. Customer who left, or a competitor's customer

1. Your role and how you use this kind of product.
2. What did you use before, and why did you move? (specific shortcomings)
3. Who drove the switch? How long did it take? What did it cost you to switch?
4. Did the old vendor try to keep you? How?
5. Would anything bring you back?
6. Who else has switched, in either direction?

## 3. Competitor product manager or salesperson

1. Tell me about your product and where it is strongest.
2. Your role: how long, what you are measured on.
3. Who do you meet most often in competitive deals? Where do you win, where do you lose?
4. What does [target]'s product lack, in your view? Where has it let customers down?
5. Have customers moved to you from [target]? Would any be willing to talk to an investor?
6. Would your company pursue customers of [niche size/type]? Why or why not?
7. What changes are you seeing in the market?

Then take specific claims to the target's head of sales, and the answers back to the competitor (suppliers-and-competitors.md §6).

## 4. Supplier or service provider

1. What do you supply to companies in this industry? How is business generally?
2. How are lead times and capacity at the moment? Busier or quieter than last year?
3. What do you see across the industry: who is growing, who is cutting back? (general trends, not a client's confidential orders)
4. If demand jumped, could the industry's suppliers keep up? Where are the bottlenecks?
5. What is the price of [component] for a normal order? (ordinary commercial quote, honestly requested)

For contract manufacturers and overflow vendors: ask about general activity and capacity only.

## 5. Rank-and-file employee or former employee

Use mainly in crises or when management is guarded; approach with care and honesty.
1. How long have you been there, and what do you do?
2. What's it like there these days? Busier, quieter?
3. How do people feel about the leadership? About the new plan?
4. Is the company hiring or letting people go in your area?
5. *(Former employees)* Why did you leave? What would you want an investor to understand?

Never ask about confidential matters (orders, results, deals). Never offer payment for information about the employer. Former employees remain bound by confidentiality agreements; respect them.

## 6. Local and community sources

Chamber of commerce, local bankers, local reporters (especially of free weeklies), university staff, commercial real-estate agents, owners of places staff frequent.
1. What is the company's reputation locally? As an employer?
2. Have you noticed changes: hiring, layoffs, new buildings, empty buildings, traffic?
3. Has the company applied for permits, expansions, incentives? (public records)
4. Who in town knows the company well? (introductions)
5. What do people say about its leaders?

Mentioning that you manage savings for others (if true) often makes people helpful. Pre-research the person you are calling.

## 7. Management (chief executive, finance chief, head of sales)

Before: know the customers, competitors, product and filings. Bring something useful (customer feedback without naming sources).

1. Feedback first: "Several customers I spoke with rate you highly; a few mentioned [shortcoming], and [competitor] seems to be targeting it. Is that on your radar?"
2. Could you tell me about your background and what drew you to this role?
3. What is your plan to restore growth (or profit), in your own words? *(Not a forecast; nothing unpublished.)*
4. Who are the key people you rely on? What do they bring?
5. What are the biggest challenges? Where could the plan go wrong?
6. How do you measure progress internally? *(Generally, not numbers.)*
7. *(Head of sales)* Customers who left cited [drawbacks]; have those been addressed? What about [competitor]'s claims?
8. May I speak with your finance chief / head of operations?

Watch congruence (people-and-management.md §8). Compliment real achievements honestly.

## 8. Investor relations and the site tour

1. Could I see where and how the product is made? (persist politely)
2. *(Product in hand)* Where is each part made? Who assembles it? What happens next? Who inspects it?
3. What is your capacity at each stage? What would you need if volume rose 25%, 50%, doubled? Which machines, how much space, how many people, from where?
4. How did you handle the last peak? Did you send work outside? To whom?
5. If volume dropped sharply, what would you scale back first?
6. Where is finished stock kept? What if the warehouse fills up?

If staff become suspicious of detailed questions, explain your purpose and offer references.

## 9. Industry veteran or expert

1. Which company in this field would you most like to own, for the long run? Why? (If "if I could run it", ask what is wrong with how it is run.)
2. In each stage of the industry's value chain, who is the leader you most admire?
3. What does the market misunderstand about this industry right now?
4. What are the players up to: who is gaining, losing, hiring, leaving?
5. *(Technical expert, possibly paid)* Is this product stable? Could it become obsolete within a year or two? How does it compare with alternatives?

Paid experts: use only those not currently employed by the company or its direct competitors, through channels with compliance controls, and never ask for their employer's or clients' confidential information.

## 10. Debrief checklist

- Facts learned (with source tier) and verbatim key phrases.
- Manner: congruent or incongruent; who reacted how.
- Hypotheses confirmed, refuted, or new.
- New names and introductions.
- Answers to brief questions (update the research brief).
- Legal flags (anything that might be confidential: quarantine it, do not trade, consult counsel).
- Three-test rating of each new finding.
- Next actions.
