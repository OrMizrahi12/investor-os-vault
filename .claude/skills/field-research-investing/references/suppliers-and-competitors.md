## 1. What supplier research is for

Research on a company's own people and its customers explains the routine business: what you need for long-term ownership. Research on suppliers is mainly about **breaks in the routine**: an unexpected surge or collapse in volume, the gain or loss of a big contract, an emergency financing, a takeover. Use it (a) to find good companies whose temporary setback is ending, (b) to foresee disasters to avoid or short, and (c) to warn long-term holders of short-term shocks.

## 2. Product suppliers: inputs that move with output

1. List the product's physical ingredients and packaging. Ask a practitioner if needed (someone who makes a similar product can tell you the bulk inputs and the per-unit packaging).
2. Identify who supplies each ingredient.
3. Bulk inputs stored inside the plant reveal little. Inputs with a **one-to-one relationship to units sold** (boxes, labels, caps, crates, printed inserts, specific components) are the best proxies.
4. Many such items are made by smaller outside suppliers and can be observed or counted off the company's own property: at the supplier's storage yard, at shipping docks, in delivery traffic.
5. Get the unit price as any buyer would (a price list or a normal quote) and combine with volume to estimate revenue (verification-and-red-flags.md).
6. Learn the manufacturing process and its volume bottlenecks; knowing which staff are needed at each stage lets you see when a bottleneck is hit (product-and-operations.md).

Mundane products are often the easiest to track. Be careful at the industry level: it is easy to drift back into theory and desk interpretation; keep tying conclusions to physical evidence.

## 3. Choosing the supplier to watch

The best signals come from suppliers for whom the company is a **large or distinctive share** of business. A packaging maker serving fifty similar producers tells you little about any one of them; a supplier that gets most of its revenue from one customer is effectively a window onto that customer's volume. When the supplier ramps up (new shift, full yard, extra trucks), confirm which customer drove it.

Private suppliers are often more relaxed about discussing their general business than listed ones. Ask about capacity, lead times and how busy they are in general; never ask them to disclose a customer's confidential orders (legal-ethics-guardrails.md).

## 4. Physical signals around suppliers

- **Watch the destination, not the traveler.** It is cheaper and more lawful to observe a place where an event will show up than to follow anyone around.
- **Sometimes watch the supplies, not the supplier.** A stockpile building up at a supplier's warehouse (visible from a public road) can precede a big order or campaign. Periodic drive-bys, or a local paid to note when stock piles up or empties, can be enough.
- Shipping docks and loading bays visible from public places allow counts.
- Municipal records: expansions need permits (building, zoning, effluent discharge) and sometimes local infrastructure upgrades. Planning files occasionally contain a company's own multi-year projections of its needs. Public, and rarely read.

## 5. Service providers, especially "services of sudden change"

- Professionals who serve the company (accountants, lawyers, bankers) are closer to discontinuities than physical-input suppliers, who sit far upstream of the end customer.
- Specialists signal change by their mere involvement: merger-and-acquisition bankers and lawyers, takeover-defense counsel, loan-workout and restructuring teams, special public-relations firms engaged before deals, forensic accountants (possible fraud), bankruptcy lawyers (possible collapse).
- Know who the active deal advisers are in your area and sector: league tables, legal and financial trade press reporting which firm advised which deal, firms' own websites. Sectors have specialists (media, software, regional banks), which keeps the list short.
- Learn to recognize the active dealmakers and the corporate-development officers of companies you follow from public professional sources (firm websites, conference programs, annual reports). If you happen to see, in a public place, a known takeover adviser in conversation with the executives of a cheap company, that observation is legitimate; note time, place and context in a dated memo.
- Guardrails: do not follow, stake out or photograph individuals to track their movements; do not approach advisers' staff for client information; any information that originates with a party to a pending deal is high risk (tender-offer rules can apply even without a breach of duty). See legal-ethics-guardrails.md §2-3.

## 6. Competitors: the triangulation loop

Competitors are both obstacles and excellent sources. Start with the people in charge of competing products.

1. **Find competitors' product managers.** Product announcements and trade-press articles often name them; most have an incentive to talk up their product.
2. **Call as an investor** researching the industry. Ask about their product, then their job, then steer to the comparison: features the target lacks, the target's failures, customers who switched to them, customers the target would rather nobody heard about. Ask whether any of those customers would be willing to talk.
3. **Call the defectors.** Their experience, their reasons, their job and role in the decision.
4. **Take the specifics to the target's head of sales:** "Customers who left mentioned these drawbacks; have they been fixed?" Mention the competitor's claimed advantages. Expect a vigorous answer listing the competitor's weaknesses and customers who moved the other way.
5. **Take that back to the competitor** for a rebuttal, if useful.
6. Examine the products yourself first, so you can judge the claims.

Done tactfully, this yields a sharper picture of strengths, weaknesses and trajectory than any report. For intangible products, focus on attributes and service. Over time you will develop a set of customer-view comparison categories for each industry.

## 7. Competitive-threat checks

- **Would a big player bother?** Ask salespeople at large competitors, openly as an investor, whether they pursue customers of the niche's size and type. If they laugh at the idea and even name the niche leader, the niche is protected. (Do not invent a fictitious tender or pose as a prospective buyer.)
- **Rival hires.** A rival that replaces its head of sales with someone who shares background and networks with your company's key buyers raises the risk of customer losses before the market notices. Ask customers casually what they think of the new person (relationship-mapping.md §5).
- **Attacks on the leader's best accounts.** A small rival winning the most prestigious customers of the market leader can force the leader to buy it at a high price. Track such wins through users and administrative staff at the contested customers.

## 8. Commodity and industry balances

When the thesis concerns a commodity or an industry rather than one company, build the physical balance yourself. Pattern from a successful case (a nuclear fuel), generalized:

1. **Trigger:** an anomaly that contradicts the consensus (a government exploring for a material it was believed to have in abundance suggests scarcity).
2. **Universe list:** every significant consumer (plant by plant: supply source, output, input quantities, input cost as a share of operating cost, key managers by role) and every producer down the chain (mines, processors), current and planned.
3. **Field contact:** visit facilities, build relationships with mid-level staff, meet regulators and processors. Sell-side analysts mostly add producer names and stated expansion plans.
4. **Balance table:** production by source, consumption by user, inventories, planned additions and closures. Record the source of every cell.
5. **Audit by sampling:** pick random entries and verify them physically or through primary sources.
6. **Proxies:** track contracts for ancillary goods and services whose volumes move in proportion to consumption; people are often more relaxed about these than about the core material.
7. **Expert review:** show the finished table to independent experts and ask where it is wrong.
8. **Reciprocity:** by the end you can offer sources useful perspective in return, which keeps doors open.
9. **Decision and exit:** if the balance shows a real shortage, own the best-placed producers, add as evidence strengthens, and keep updating the table. Exit when the physical balance turns, or start trimming when the theme becomes a mass-media cover story and screen-only investors pile in.

A project like this can take months. Prefer company-level research unless the imbalance is large and your evidence is unusually good.

## 9. Price sensitivity of demand (cost-share test)

If the input is a small share of the user's operating cost and the alternative (stopping production) is very expensive, demand barely responds to price: even a doubling or tripling of the input price raises the user's total cost only modestly. Compute it: `python3 scripts/field_math.py costshare --share 0.09 --price-change 1.0` (a 9% cost share and a 100% price rise raise total cost by about 9%). Low cost share + high switching or shutdown cost = room for prices to rise far.
