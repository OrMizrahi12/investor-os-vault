## 1. Sudden declines: terminal or transitory?

- A sharp rise usually has a visible reason (a contract, a bid, a product). A sharp or steady fall with no announced reason is harder for desk investors to read, and their explanations are guesses.
- Two hypotheses: **a lurking terminal problem** (exit or short) or **unjustified fear** (a buying opportunity; unexplained drops that are later reversed are among the best entry points).
- The first bad surprise is often followed by more: one cockroach in the kitchen usually means others.
- Not evidence: a reassuring company release ("operations proceed as planned, no cause for alarm"), brokers' notes calling the drop excessive, and healthy ratios recalculated from the same reported numbers.
- Evidence: what people near the physical operation know and how they behave.

## 2. Who knows when nobody will talk

In a decline, management, investor relations and middle managers become guarded even with friendly investors. The best sources are then:
- **Rank-and-file employees** who see the operation at its rawest: production, shipping, purchasing, returns, support. They often know more than their bosses realize. Many live paycheck to paycheck and track the company's health closely: they know from purchasing whether parts orders are rising, from sales whether orders are coming in, from the returns desk whether a new product is being sent back.
- **Local intermediaries:** local bank managers, business owners, the reporter at the local free weekly (who often knows everything), chamber-of-commerce staff, commercial real-estate agents, drivers and service providers who work around the site.
- **Counterparties' public footprints:** creditors' filings, court records, liens, local permits.

Approach: honest, warm, unhurried conversation, interest in their lives first, no pressure on the sensitive topic, reciprocity and humor. Let them volunteer. Never offer money for information about the company and never ask anyone to breach confidentiality.

## 3. Remote operations and suspected fraud

Pattern (a resource company whose share price had multiplied a hundredfold on a spectacular discovery abroad): the stock began sliding for no stated reason; the company then announced that its chief geologist had died in an accident and that everything was proceeding normally; brokers urged buying; the slide continued. Calls to local institutions in that country produced curt refusals and, tellingly, laughter. A visit to the area and friendly conversation with local people and laborers revealed that politically powerful families were silent partners in the project, that samples were being doctored before going to the assay lab, and that the processing plant under construction was largely for show. The stock went to zero; institutions that had bought it once it entered the index lost billions.

Lessons:
- When locals laugh or refuse to talk about a celebrated operation, the locals know something the market does not.
- In a suspected scam, ask rank-and-file workers, not supervisors: supervisors are climbing the ladder, will say little, and may report your interest to the people running the scam. Workers who are not complicit often speak plainly.
- Check who really owns and controls the operation locally (politically connected partners), and whether insiders or local holders are selling.
- For resource claims, check the chain of custody of samples, the independence of the assay lab, and whether independent drilling or twin holes confirmed results. *(Modern equivalent: technical reports filed under resource-disclosure rules, qualified-person sign-offs, independent assay verification.)*
- Physical danger is real. Do not send the user (or anyone) into unsafe places; once warning signs appear, leave. The investment decision rarely needs more certainty than the first conversations provide.
- Act quietly; tell no one but your lawyer. Document that what you learned came from local public conversation, not from company insiders.
- Short positions in collapsing frauds face violent squeezes on the way down; size so you can hold through them.

## 4. Debt, covenants and workouts

- Debt is permanent in a way revenues and profits are not: it always gets repaid, if not by the borrower then by the lender through a loss. A bargain stock with debt needs an extra check: covenants (coverage ratios, minimum revenue or cash flow), maturities, and who holds the debt.
- A missed quarter can breach covenants. Usually lenders waive breaches for a fee or a higher rate; an aggressive holder (for example a distressed-debt fund that bought part of the issue) may instead demand equity (warrants, conversion), transferring value from shareholders to creditors. If the company's own investor-relations staff do not know who holds the bonds, the refinancing may be going badly.
- **Workouts and haircuts.** When a lender agrees to cut what it is owed, the borrower's shares usually jump (often with the lender taking warrants); when the lender forces repayment or a sale, the shares fall. The lender's leverage is the key variable: if the borrower's value walks out the door with its people (an advertising agency, a creative studio, a professional-services firm), the threat of mass resignation leaves the lender little choice but to concede.
- Observable signals: the body language of the parties in public spaces before and after negotiations (who looks confident, who looks defeated), announcements by lenders, changes in advisers. Anything learned from a lender's or borrower's staff about the negotiation is inside information: do not seek it or trade on it.

## 5. Strikes and labor disputes

- Strikes look like disasters but usually are not, except in very large unionized companies where they reflect chronic mistrust (such companies rarely deserve long-term ownership anyway).
- A dispute about pay alone is generally transitory and is usually settled at the last moment, when each negotiator can show their side there was no better option. A strike threat that knocks down a quality company's shares can be an opportunity.
- But disputes can turn on **respect**, not money. Pattern: a settlement looked certain (a key customer had threatened to pull a large order if work stopped, which put jobs at risk); then management refused a long-serving worker a modest personal request, and an executive's contemptuous remark about workers reached the shop floor. The vote went overwhelmingly for a strike, equipment was damaged, the customer left, and the plant needed months to recover.
- Checks: talk to workers and their local haunts, local press, and the company's negotiators; look for status separation and contempt between management and staff; ask what issues *besides* pay are in the air.
- After such a blow-up, a solid old company with a strong balance sheet may fall to around book value: that is the time to research it again, not before.

## 6. Takeovers, mergers and restructurings

Signals that an extraordinary event may be coming:
- Engagement of specialist advisers (deal bankers and lawyers, takeover-defense counsel, special accountants, crisis public relations). Public sources: league tables, legal trade press, conference appearances.
- Prior working relationships between people at the target and at a likely acquirer (shared former employers, deal histories): most acquisitions grow out of existing personal ties (relationship-mapping.md §5).
- Corporate-development officers of a likely acquirer appearing at the target's events or in its town.
- A cheap stock with a new controlling holder, creeping accumulation or activist filings.
- A company that stops bleeding cash while potential buyers were waiting for it to become desperate (idea-generation.md §5).
- A small rival winning the market leader's best accounts, forcing the leader to consider buying it.

Guardrails specific to deals: information about a pending tender offer that originates with the bidder, the target or their advisers can make trading illegal even without a breach of duty. Observations made in public, combined with public records and your own analysis (the mosaic approach), are the legitimate path. Document how you reached your conclusion before trading, and get legal advice when your evidence would look prescient (legal-ethics-guardrails.md).

## 7. Confirming a public clue

A public remark (an interview, an offhand comment in the media by someone close to the company, a job ad) can hint at something big. Never act on the hint alone; confirm with independent, legitimate checks. Pattern: a media interview suggested that the family of a money-losing company's chief executive expected a large windfall within a month. Confirmation came from four directions:
1. Industry acquaintances mentioned that engineers at the company were buying shares (which would appear in insider filings).
2. In the café where the company's staff gathered, the tone of conversation was excited (words not needed).
3. The chief executive, long hungry for cash, declined an offer of new investment because the price was too low and said to wait a month.
4. The company had paid a creditor ahead of schedule thanks to prepaid orders for a new product.

The shares multiplied several times. Note the legal sensitivity of step 4: asking a counterparty's staff about a company's payments can elicit confidential information. Prefer counterparties' public filings and statements; if someone volunteers confidential detail, do not trade on it before taking legal advice.

## 8. Ears open: everyday clues

Once you know a company's people and routines, small remarks become signals:
- A key customer due to give the keynote at the company's customer conference turns out to be planning a family trip that weekend: possible defection. Verify through other customers and the conference program.
- A person you know is called back to the plant for overtime when volume spikes now says they will miss a regular hobby event because "they need me at work": possible surge.
- A company's celebration habits change visibly around exceptional results.

Each is a lead to confirm legitimately, never a reason to trade by itself. Do not set up paid arrangements with people around the company to report on its internal events.

## 9. A decision tree for crises

1. Is the balance sheet able to survive the worst plausible version? (cash, debt terms, covenants, holders) If no: exit or stay out regardless of story.
2. Is the problem understood? If no: talk to the edges (§2) before acting; do not average down blind.
3. Is it terminal (fraud, obsolescence, lost franchise, hostile creditors) or transitory (fixable mess, pay dispute, cyclical trough)? Need at least two independent confirmations.
4. Terminal: exit; consider a short only with legal review, strict sizing and tolerance for squeezes.
5. Transitory: buy in stages as evidence of the fix accumulates; keep monitoring the sources that told you.
6. Unclear after reasonable effort: stay out. Cash is a position.
