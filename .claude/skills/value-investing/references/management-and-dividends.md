# Management, Shareholder Rights and Dividend Policy

Use this file when judging whether a company's management serves its outside shareholders: dividend payout and retention, stock dividends and splits, buybacks, executive options and pay, proxy statements and governance, activism and takeovers. It draws on the classic 1949 view of the investor as business owner as well as the classic dividend rules.

## 1. The owner's two questions

Buying a stock makes you an owner: the managers work for you, the directors answer to you, the company's cash is yours. The classic view (1949) reduces the owner's job to two questions:
1. **Is management reasonably efficient?** Judge by comparing profitability, size and competitive position with similar companies in the industry.
2. **Are the interests of the average outside shareholder properly recognized?** Judge by capital use: dividends, retention, buybacks, compensation, conflicts of interest.

Why both: a management can run operations efficiently yet fail the owners because its efficiency stops at operations and does not extend to the best use of capital. Managements want as much capital from owners as they can get, to minimize their own financial problems, and will run with more capital than necessary if holders permit (1949). A classic corollary (1934): management should try to prevent both absurdly high and unduly low prices for its securities.

A 1947 survey of about 600 analysts: over 95% agreed shareholders have the right to call for a formal investigation of managers whose leadership does not enhance the stock's value, yet such action was almost unheard of. In theory the shareholders as a class are king; in practice they vote "sheeplike" for management whatever its record (1949).

Secondary companies: a minority share of a secondary company is worth much less than a controlling block, so these relations matter even more there.

## 2. When to challenge management

The classic three-part trigger. Questioning competence is justified when results:
- [ ] (1) are unsatisfactory in themselves,
- [ ] (2) are poorer than those of similarly situated companies, AND
- [ ] (3) have produced an unsatisfactory market price of long duration.

Be generous to managements demonstrably doing a good job; demand clear explanations when results are worse than they should be; support movements to improve or remove clearly unproductive managements.

If management is judged poor (1949):
- [ ] A few substantial holders become convinced change is needed and work for it.
- [ ] Rank-and-file holders read the proxy material from both sides, recognize failure, and demand more than artful platitudes.
- [ ] When results are clearly well below average, bring in outside "business engineers" to assess policies and competence: authorized by proxy vote, chosen by an independent committee of outside shareholders, reporting to shareholders (not the board), paid by the company.

Example of the issue (Pair 5 in case-studies.md): International Harvester did $2.5 billion+ of business yet earned only 5.5% on its stock capital and sold at a 41% discount to book. What is the point of such volume if it cannot justify the owners' capital? Both managers and shareholders must recognize the problem exists. Modern update: in 2001 a majority of Navistar (Harvester's successor) holders voted to remove anti-takeover restrictions and the board simply refused.

## 3. Dividend policy rules

The classic standard: **shareholders should demand either a normal payout, on the order of two-thirds of earnings, or a clear demonstration that reinvested profits have produced a satisfactory increase in per-share earnings.**

Apply it by case:
- [ ] **Recognized growth company:** a low payout is acceptable if the retained-earnings test is met (per-share earnings rose satisfactorily on the reinvested capital). Test: change in EPS over 5-10 years vs cumulative retained earnings per share; the incremental return on retained capital should be at least what owners could earn elsewhere.
- [ ] **Other companies with a low payout:** the low payout is often itself the cause of a below-fair-value price; holders have every right to inquire and probably to complain.
- [ ] **Financially weak company withholding dividends** because it needs earnings plus depreciation to pay debt and rebuild working capital: little to argue, except to criticize management for letting finances deteriorate.
- [ ] **Unprosperous company withholding dividends "to expand":** illogical on its face; require a complete explanation and a convincing defense. There is no a priori reason to expect owners to gain from expansion with their money by a mediocre business under the same management.

The either-or test, as the screening script (scripts/value_screen.py) runs it:
1. Payout ratio (average dividends ÷ average EPS) of about two-thirds or more: pass. No retained-earnings test is needed.
2. Payout below that: compare the 10-year gain in EPS with the cumulative retained EPS over the same years (EPS minus dividends per share, summed). The ratio is the rough return earned on the retained profits.
3. Pass if that return is satisfactory, at least what owners could earn elsewhere on the same money. Flag a low payout without a satisfactory return on retained earnings: that is the case in which holders have every right to inquire and complain.
4. Read the result with the cases above. A financially weak company retaining to repair its balance sheet fails for a different reason (weak finances), not for its dividend policy.

Why dividends matter:
- The profits belong to the shareholders (within prudent management); many holders need the income; dividends are "real money," while retained earnings may or may not later show up as value.
- The market historically favored liberal payers. Studies showed a dollar paid out had as much as four times the price effect of a dollar retained. Before 1950, low-payout utilities sold at low multipliers and proved especially good buys because their dividends were later raised.
- Old pattern: weak companies were forced to retain profits instead of paying the usual 60-75%, and their prices suffered; by 1972 strong growth companies kept payouts low with investors' approval, and for growth favorites the dividend (or its absence) had virtually no price effect.
- The market's emerging rule: non-growth stocks are "income issues" priced mainly on the dividend; recognized rapid-growth stocks are priced on expected growth over about the next decade, dividends largely ignored. Not a clear guide for most stocks: many are intermediate, and the market's view of their growth swings from year to year. Paradox: the market asks less prosperous (slower-growth) companies to pay more, whereas traditionally the more prosperous paid more and raised payments.

Modern update (2003) adds:
- Payout ratios fell from 60-75% in the early 1970s to 35-40% by the end of the 1990s; the stronger the company, the less likely it paid.
- Reject the "Daddy Knows Best" claim that management can reinvest all cash better than owners. Many companies waste cash (failed ventures and acquisitions) or hoard it beyond any use (section 9).
- Evidence for dividends: Arnott and Asness found low current payouts were followed by low future earnings growth, high payouts by high growth; over 10-year periods earnings growth averaged 3.9 points higher when payouts were high. Nissim and Ziv found dividend increases were followed by better returns and higher profitability for at least four years. Even skeptics (Benartzi, Michaely, Thaler) agree dividend increases precede higher returns.
- Conclusion: a dividend does not guarantee good results, but it improves the typical stock's return by taking some cash out of managers' hands before they can squander or hoard it.
- Academic counterview (Miller and Modigliani, 1961): dividends are irrelevant if total return is the same. The classic and modern answer is practical: retained cash is not reliably converted into value.

## 4. Stock dividends vs stock splits

Definitions:
- **Stock split:** a restatement of the share structure (e.g., 2 or 3 for 1) to lower the per-share price; not tied to specific reinvested earnings. Done by a change in par value, or technically via a transfer from earned surplus to capital.
- **Proper stock dividend:** paid to give holders tangible evidence of specific earnings reinvested for their account over a short recent period (no more than the two preceding years). Valued at about market value at declaration, with that amount moved from earned surplus to capital; usually no more than 5%.
- NYSE dividing line (1972): 25%. Distributions of 25% or more need not capitalize their market value. Modern update: NYSE Rule 703 now calls distributions above 25% and below 100% "partial stock splits," which may now require capitalization from retained earnings; virtually all splits today are par-value changes.
- Arbitrary stock dividends unrelated to recent earnings (some banks paid 10%) create confusion; avoid treating them as income.

The classic policy preference:
- [ ] Favor companies with a systematic, clearly announced policy of periodic stock dividends that capitalize all or a stated share of reinvested earnings (Purex and GEICO capitalized 100% of reinvested earnings). Modern update: such policies are now extremely rare; special dividends fell from about half of NYSE stocks (1936, 1950) to under 10% by 1970 and under 5% in the 1990s.
- The classic view rejects the academic claim that stock dividends are mere paper: a periodic 5% stock dividend changes only form (105 shares instead of 100), but holders can sell the new shares to cash in reinvested profits, and the cash rate is more likely to be maintained on 105 shares than raised 5% on 100. Modern update (2003): this argument is obsolete with electronic shares and the probability claim cannot be computed; skip it.
- The classic utility reform: replace liberal cash dividends followed by rights offerings (the company takes the money back by selling new stock) with stock dividends. Holders end in the same position but avoid income tax on dividends they do not really keep; those wanting cash sell the stock dividend as they now sell rights. Taxing earnings not truly received is inequitable. Modern update: rights offerings are now rare outside closed-end funds and insurance/holding companies, and they coerce non-participants through dilution; a cleaner tax fix would make dividends deductible to the corporation.
- [ ] When a company pays a large dividend and then sells new shares to the same holders, treat the net as retained capital, not as payout.

## 5. Buybacks: when they help and when they hurt

Theory: fewer shares raise EPS; a buyback is tax-free to holders who do not sell; if the shares are cheap it is an excellent use of capital. Historically companies bought more when prices were low: in the 12 days after the Oct 19, 1987 crash, 400 companies announced buybacks vs 107 in the earlier, higher-priced part of the year.

Practice since the 1990s:
- Buybacks mostly offset option dilution. In 1998, 63.5% of CFOs said countering option dilution was a major reason. Companies spent 41.8% of net income on buybacks in 2000 vs 4.8% in 1980 (helped by the SEC's 1982 relaxation of repurchase limits).
- Oracle, June 1999-May 2000: issued 101 million shares to senior executives and 26 million to employees for $484 million (average $3.53/share), while spending $5.3 billion (52% of revenue) to repurchase 290.7 million shares at an average $18.26: sell low, buy high. When the stock halved by 2002, buybacks were cut to $2.8 billion because fewer options were exercised.
- Why managers prefer buybacks: (1) the company gets a tax deduction when options are exercised (Oracle $1.69 billion FY2000-2002; Sprint $678 million while insiders took $1.9 billion of option profits in 1999-2000); (2) options gain value with volatility and dividends dampen volatility, so dividends lower the value of managers' own options. Buyback-only companies show profitability at least twice as volatile as dividend payers.

Checks:
- [ ] Net buyback = shares repurchased minus shares issued; judge the change in share count, not the dollars spent.
- [ ] Compare average repurchase price with intrinsic value (and with the average price at which shares were issued to insiders).
- [ ] Do buybacks rise when the stock is high and shrink when it is low? That is value destruction.
- [ ] Treat buybacks that merely offset option issuance as compensation expense, not capital return.

## 6. Executive options and pay

- About two-thirds of executive pay now comes as options and other non-cash awards; thirty years earlier at least two-thirds was cash.
- Options are meant to align managers with long-term owners, but nearly all managers sell shares immediately after exercising (Bogle), which aligns nothing.
- Apple example: Steve Jobs took a $1 salary as interim CEO from 1997; at end-1999 the board gave him a $90 million Gulfstream jet for his unpaid 2.5 years; the next month he dropped "interim" and received options on 20 million shares (he had owned 2 shares). At just 5% annual stock growth through early 2010 (half the market's long-run return) the options would be worth $548.3 million (split-adjusted figures).
- [ ] Vote against any plan that turns more than **3% of shares outstanding** over to managers through option grants. Christopher Davis uses the same ~3% limit.
- [ ] Veto plans not contingent on a fair and enduring measure of superior results, e.g., outperforming the average stock in the industry for at least five years. No CEO should get rich producing poor results for owners.
- [ ] Deduct option cost from owner earnings (ROIC formula in enterprising-selection.md) and add outstanding options to the share count or capitalization (method in convertibles-warrants.md).
- In 2003 options were a tax deduction but not an income-statement expense; the modern update called this ludicrous (see section 10).

## 7. Proxy and governance checklist

The proxy statement discloses the annual-meeting agenda, pay and stock ownership of managers and directors, transactions between insiders and the company, auditor choice and board elections. Read it before and after buying: it is a canary in the coal mine.

- [ ] **Related-party transactions:** do insiders do business with the company or own entities that trade with it? Who approved it?
- [ ] **Incentive conflicts:** does an officer profit when the company gets a worse price? Does the company lend money to insiders' entities?
- [ ] **Pay:** option overhang and new grants vs the 3% ceiling; performance conditions; perks (jets, loans).
- [ ] **Ownership:** do managers and directors own meaningful stock bought with their own cash?
- [ ] **Board quality:** independent directors with real business experience, not cronies; willingness to act on shareholder votes.
- [ ] **Accounting candor:** understandable statements; "nonrecurring" charges that do not recur (enterprising-selection.md, management tests).
- [ ] **Capital use:** payout vs the two-thirds standard or the retained-earnings test; cash hoard vs any plausible need; acquisitions paid with overvalued stock.
- [ ] **Honesty over time:** compare what management said one year with what happened the next.

If something disturbs you: vote against every director; attend the annual meeting and speak; rally other holders. Owning 100 shares is no excuse: if you never read the proxy of a stock you own and it goes bust, you share the blame.

Enron's 1999 proxy, worked through: under "Certain Transactions" it disclosed that the CFO, Andrew Fastow, was managing member of partnerships (LJM1, LJM2) that had bought $170 million of assets from Enron, sometimes with money borrowed from Enron; directors approved; Fastow shared the partnerships' profits.
- Duty: as CFO he must get the highest price for Enron's assets.
- Incentive: a lower price raised his partnerships' profits and his own income.
- Enron even lent money for the purchases.
- Conclusion: a profound, undeniable conflict, and directors who approved it showed judgment that should send your money elsewhere. Lessons: never let detailed number-crunching override common sense; always read the proxy.

Voting facts (modern update, 2002): proxy response rates about 80-88% including broker votes (which default to management), so owners of 12-20% of shares do not vote; individuals own about 40% of U.S. shares and institutions must vote, so roughly a third of individual investors do not vote.

## 8. Activism, takeovers and outside directors

- The classic conclusion after 36 years (1934-70): practically nothing was accomplished by the body of shareholders acting intelligently; annual-meeting appearances are generally futile. The cause was rescued from outside, by takeovers.
- Mechanism: poor management produces a poor price; the low price attracts companies seeking to diversify (or outside groups); bids usually fall within the range of the business's value under reasonably competent management; inert public holders are bailed out.
- Rule: with very few exceptions, poor managements are changed not by public stockholders but by an individual or compact group asserting control. The threat puts boards on notice and has increased changes of company presidents. Limits: change often comes only after long poor results and after enough disappointed holders sell cheaply to let outsiders gain control.
- [ ] Read with an open mind any proxy material from fellow shareholders seeking to fix an obviously unsatisfactory management.
- [ ] Search primary filings for surplus assets the market ignores. When cash or securities clearly exceed operating needs, a shareholder (or a small group) may press management to pay them out. Case, Northern Pipe Line (1925): the shares traded at $65 while the company held at least $80 a share in high-quality bonds, visible only in obscure regulatory reports; a shareholder bought, pressed management to raise the payout, and came away with about $110 a share three years later.
- Modern history: takeovers dried up after 1973 and the 1970s-early 1980s were a low point of U.S. industrial efficiency; T. Boone Pickens' 1984 hostile bid for Gulf Oil and junk-bond-financed raiders then forced efficiency, leaving business leaner (good) and meaner (sometimes not).
- **Independent directors (classic proposal, 1949):** add one or more professional, independent directors with broad business experience, who submit their own annual report directly to shareholders answering: is the business producing the results for outside holders that proper management should deliver, and if not, why and what should be done? The modern update would also have them justify dividend and buyback policy and explain how they concluded management is not overpaid.

## 9. Evidence and cases

No-dividend growth, and how the market's attitude changed:
- Texas Instruments: 5 (1953) to 256 (1960) while EPS rose from 43 cents to $3.91 with no dividend; dividends began in 1962 when EPS had fallen to $2.14 and the stock to a low of 49.
- Superior Oil: 1948 EPS $35.26, dividend $3, high 235; 1953 dividend cut to $1, high 660; 1957 no dividend, price 2,000; 1962 down to 795, earning $49.50 and paying $7.50. Modern update: peaked at $2,165 in 1959 (dividend $4); later acquired by Mobil (1984).
- AT&T at 25x earnings in 1961 still traded on dividend rumors; IBM's yield (0.5% at the 1960 high, 1.5% end-1970) drew little attention. Stock splits were potent market influences for both.

Wasted cash: Priceline wrote off $67 million in 2000 on grocery and gasoline ventures; Amazon destroyed at least $233 million on stakes in Webvan and Ashford.com; the two largest losses then on record, JDS Uniphase ($56 billion, 2001) and AOL Time Warner ($99 billion, 2002), followed decisions to merge using overvalued shares instead of paying dividends.

Hoarded cash (late 2001): Oracle $5 billion; Cisco at least $7.5 billion; Microsoft $38.2 billion, growing by over $2 million an hour, and $43.4 billion by end-2002 (operations efficient, finance not). Microsoft announced a regular dividend in early 2003. Classic rule (1949): while surplus cash stays in the company the outside holder gets little benefit from it.

Mergers: BusinessWeek found 61% of 300+ large mergers in 1995-2001 destroyed value for the acquirer's holders, and stock-financed acquirers trailed rivals by 8%; Fuller, Netter and Stegemoller found buying private companies or subsidiaries helped acquirers while buying whole public companies hurt them.

Dividend-payer decline: only 3.7% of 1999 IPOs paid a dividend vs 72.1% of 1960s IPOs (Fama and French); total real dividends rose but the number of payers shrank by nearly two-thirds.

## 10. Translating to today

- The classic two-thirds payout norm reflects an era of 60-75% payouts; it is a benchmark, not a mandate. Apply the either-or test: high payout OR proof that retained earnings raised per-share earnings at a satisfactory return. Count net buybacks (after option issuance) as part of payout when judging capital return.
- Option expensing: in 2003 options were not charged against reported earnings; since U.S. rules changed in the mid-2000s they are, but analysts' "adjusted" earnings often add stock-based compensation back. Undo that add-back; treat stock compensation as a real cost and check dilution in the share count.
- Rights offerings, systematic stock dividends and bank-style arbitrary stock dividends are now rare; splits are cosmetic. Do not count either as value creation.
- Tax details (dividend double taxation, 2003 proposals, utility rights offerings) are dated; use current law.
- Governance tools have changed (say-on-pay votes, proxy access, majority voting in some markets), but the owner's two questions and the proxy checklist apply unchanged.
