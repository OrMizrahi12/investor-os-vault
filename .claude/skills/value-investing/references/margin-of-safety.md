# Margin of Safety: The Full Treatment

Use this file when testing whether any security (bond, preferred, ordinary stock, growth stock, bargain, special situation, warrant) offers a genuine margin of safety at its price, and when explaining why diversification and refusing to overpay protect against being wrong. ## 1. The concept and its function

- The motto for sound investment, in three words: MARGIN OF SAFETY. It runs through every part of the method, explicitly or not.
- Its function: to make an accurate estimate of the future unnecessary. If the margin is large, it is enough to assume future earnings will not fall far below the past to feel protected against the vicissitudes of time.
- The margin always depends on the price paid: large at one price, small at a higher price, nonexistent at a still higher one.
- It guarantees only better odds of profit than loss, not that loss is impossible; hence it must be paired with diversification (section 10).
- A true margin of safety can be shown by figures, by persuasive reasoning, and by a body of actual experience.

## 2. Test for bonds and preferred stocks

Two equivalent measures; use both.
- [ ] **Earnings over charges:** the past ability to earn well above interest requirements over a period of years. Example standard: a railroad should have earned total fixed charges more than 5 times before income tax, averaged over years, for investment grade. (Full coverage table by industry: security-analysis.md.)
- [ ] **Alternative statement:** the percentage by which revenues or profits could fall before nothing is left after interest. Compute: margin = (earnings available for interest - interest) / earnings available for interest.
- [ ] **Revenue form of the same test:** estimate the revenue decline that would wipe out the balance after interest, using operating leverage (fixed versus variable costs). With revenue R, contribution margin m (1 - variable costs / revenue), earnings before interest E and interest I: revenue decline that leaves nothing after interest = (E - I) / (m x R). Illustrative (not the classic arithmetic): R $1,000 million, m 40%, E $100 million, I $20 million -> (100 - 20) / 400 = a 20% revenue decline, although profits could fall 80% before interest went uncovered. High fixed costs make the revenue cushion much thinner than the profit cushion.
- [ ] **Enterprise value over debt:** compare the fair value of the whole business with its debt (same calculation for a preferred, adding senior claims). Worked example: debt $10 million, business fairly worth $30 million -> value could shrink by two-thirds before bondholders lose. Approximate the cushion with the average market value of the junior stock over a period of years; since average stock prices track average earning power, this measure and coverage usually agree.

Why past record rather than projections: the bond investor does not expect future earnings to equal the past and does not rely mainly on forecasting whether they will be better or worse; he relies on a large margin in the record, so a moderate shortfall cannot hurt him.

- [ ] Test coverage of interest and preferred dividends over several years including a subnormal period (a period like 1970-71).
- [ ] When accepting a small fixed return, demand convincing evidence you are not risking a substantial part of principal (business principle 3; core-principles.md).

## 3. Common stock with a bond-like margin

- A company with only common stock outstanding that sells, in depressed conditions, for less than the bonds that could safely be issued against its property and earning power gives the stock buyer a bond's margin of safety plus all the upside of a common. Only missing: the legal right to force payments, a minor drawback.
- Many strongly financed industrials were in this position at the 1932-33 lows. Such stocks supply an ideal but infrequent combination of safety and profit opportunity.
- Worked example, National Presto 1972: total enterprise value $43 million; recent pretax earnings $16 million. Illustrative check (not the classic arithmetic): if $43 million were bonds at 1972 rates of roughly 7-8%, interest would be about $3-3.5 million, covered about 5x by $16 million pretax, so the company could easily have supported that much debt. The common therefore carried a bond-grade margin.
- [ ] Test: hypothetical debt = market value of the equity; hypothetical interest = that x current high-grade bond yield; pretax earnings / hypothetical interest >= the bond coverage standard? If yes, the stock has a bond-like margin.

## 4. Ordinary common stocks: earnings yield vs bond yield

For a normal stock bought under normal conditions, the margin of safety lies in an expected earning power considerably above the going rate on bonds.
- **Earning power** (the 1934 definition): what the company might be expected to earn year after year if current business conditions continued, meant to cover five years or more. Crude approximation: earnings yield = 1 / (P/E); a P/E of 11 gives about 9%.
- **Worked example (1965):** earning power 9% on price, bond rate 4% -> a margin of 5% a year in the stockholder's favor. Part is paid as dividends (it counts toward his result even if spent); the rest is reinvested for him. Reinvested earnings often fail to add proportionately to earning power and value (one reason the market values paid-out earnings more generously; management-and-dividends.md), but over a whole period surpluses and values grow reasonably together.
- **Ten-year aggregate:** the typical excess of earning power over bond interest over 10 years can total about 50% of the price paid (5 points x 10 years). That is a real margin which, under favorable conditions, prevents or minimizes loss.
- **Diversified application:** with such a margin in each of a diversified list of 20 or more stocks, the probability of a favorable result under fairly normal conditions becomes very large. That is why investing in representative stocks needs no special foresight, provided purchases are made at the average level of the market over a span of years.
- **Dangers:** concentrating purchases at the upper levels of the market, and buying non-representative stocks with above-average risk of shrinking earning power.

Procedure:
- [ ] Estimate earning power from 5+ years of average earnings (defensive-selection.md and security-analysis.md for averaging).
- [ ] Earnings yield = average earnings / price.
- [ ] Margin per year = earnings yield - current high-grade bond yield.
- [ ] Ten-year cumulative margin = margin x 10 (as % of price). The classic adequate case: about 50%.
- [ ] Diversify across 20+ such stocks; buy over time rather than at market peaks.

## 5. The 1972 "no margin" problem

- In 1972 typical earning power was far below 9% of price. Even a defensive investor concentrating on low-multiplier large companies at 12x recent earnings gets an 8.33% earnings yield (about 4% dividend + 4.33% reinvested). Against high-grade bonds then near 7.5%, the 10-year excess over bond interest is too small to be an adequate margin.
- Consequence: real risks exist even in a diversified list of sound stocks. They may be offset by profit possibilities, and the investor may have no choice, because holding only fixed claims in steadily depreciating dollars may be riskier still. Accept, as philosophically as possible, that the old combination of good profit chances and small ultimate risk is no longer available.
- A 1972 lecture made the point: in 1965, the typical stock at 11x earnings yielded about 9% vs 4% on bonds, a margin of over 100%; in 1972 there was no difference between the stock earnings rate and the bond rate, so no margin, even a negative one.
- Modern update (2003): the early-1972 description matched early 2003 almost exactly (market-level.md).

## 6. Growth stocks

- The growth buyer substitutes expected, above-past earnings for the record when computing his margin. In theory carefully estimated future earnings are no less reliable a guide than the bare past; analysis increasingly prefers competent evaluation of the future. So growth investing can supply as dependable a margin as ordinary investment, provided:
  - [ ] the projection is conservative, and
  - [ ] it still shows a satisfactory margin relative to the price paid.
- The danger: the market tends to price favored growth issues above what conservative projections protect.
- Rule: **all estimates, when they differ from past performance, must err at least slightly on the side of understatement**.
- If the average market level of growth stocks is too high to offer an adequate margin, simple diversified buying in the field will not work; special foresight and judgment are needed to overcome the hazards of the customary price level. Valuation formula and growth-multiplier tables: security-analysis.md.

## 7. Bargain issues

- By definition a favorable gap between price and indicated or appraised value. The gap is the margin; it absorbs miscalculation or worse-than-average luck.
- The bargain buyer stresses the investment's ability to withstand adverse developments; usually he has no real enthusiasm for the company's prospects.
- [ ] Avoid a security whose prospects are definitely bad, however low the price.
- [ ] Draw bargains from the many companies, perhaps the majority, whose future looks neither distinctly promising nor unpromising. Bought on a bargain basis, even a moderate fall in earning power need not prevent satisfactory results.
- Thresholds and methods (price <= two-thirds of value; NCAV; secondary companies): enterprising-selection.md.

## 8. Investment vs speculation: the margin as touchstone

- Many authorities deny any useful line between investment and speculation; that skepticism is unnecessary and harmful because it encourages the innate pull toward speculative excitement.
- Speculators usually believe the odds favor them (timing, superior skill, trusted adviser or system), but such claims rest on subjective judgment without supporting evidence or conclusive reasoning. Whoever stakes money on a view that the market is heading up or down is not protected by a margin of safety in any useful sense.
- The investor's margin rests on simple, definite arithmetic from statistical data, supported by practical experience. There is no guarantee this approach keeps working under unknown future conditions, but equally no valid reason for pessimism.
- Rule: to have a true investment there must be a true margin of safety, demonstrable by figures, reasoning and experience. (Definition of investment: core-principles.md.)

## 9. Unconventional investments (enterprising only)

Conventional investments, suitable for a typical portfolio: government securities; high-grade dividend-paying common stocks; state and municipal bonds for those who benefit from tax exemption; first-quality corporate bonds when they yield sufficiently more than savings bonds.

Unconventional investments, enterprising investor only:
- [ ] Undervalued common stocks of secondary companies, bought at **two-thirds or less of indicated value**.
- [ ] Medium-grade corporate bonds and preferreds at depressed prices offering a considerable discount from apparent value.
- [ ] Special situations: always based on thorough analysis promising a larger realization than the price; individual risks are allowed for in the calculations and absorbed by diversification.
- [ ] "Shocker": a diversified group of common-stock option warrants at historically low prices could be a defensible operation if a study of the odds of a future bull market raising their value shows much more to gain than to lose (supported by Hallingby's 1947 Analysts' Journal study). Warrants have largely been superseded by options.

Principle: a sufficiently low price can turn a security of mediocre quality into a sound investment, provided the buyer is informed, experienced and adequately diversified. There are no good or bad stocks, only cheap and expensive ones; the best company is a sell when its price is too high and the worst is worth buying if low enough.

Worked case, real-estate bonds: in the 1920s billions were sold at par as sound investments though many had little margin of value over debt (really speculative). In the 1930s many defaulted and fell, some below 10 cents on the dollar, and the same advisers then rejected them as worthless paper. Yet the ~90% fall made many exceedingly attractive and reasonably safe: true values were four to five times the price. The resulting "speculative profit" was the reward for shrewd investment. The fair-weather investments that ruin naive buyers later give the sophisticated buyer opportunities at nearly his own price. Those who called tech stocks a sure thing in late 1999 called them too risky in 2002; analysts tend to say "strong buy" when prices are high and "sell" after they fall.

## 10. Diversification and the roulette arithmetic

- Margin of safety and diversification are correlative. Even with the odds in your favor, one security may work out badly; as the number of such commitments rises, it becomes more certain that aggregate profits exceed aggregate losses. That is the basis of insurance underwriting. Universal acceptance of diversification is in effect acceptance of the margin-of-safety principle.
- Worked example (American roulette, 38 slots including 0 and 00):
  - A $1 bet on one number pays $35 profit if it wins; odds are 37 to 1 against. Negative margin: diversification is foolish here; betting $1 on all 38 numbers loses $2 a spin (win $35, lose $37).
  - If the payout were $39, betting $1 on every number would win $2 a spin with certainty: a small positive margin makes diversification a sure winner.
  - These are the positions of player and casino. The $2 on $38 staked is a 5.26% house edge; the player should bet as seldom as possible, the casino wants the wheel spinning.
- Rule: maximize the number of holdings each offering better odds of profit than of loss; for most investors diversification is the simplest and cheapest way to widen the margin of safety.
- Classic practice: the net-net partnership held more than 100 issues; defensive investors 10-30 stocks (defensive-selection.md); the 20+ stock rule (section 4).

## 11. Where the big losses come from

- Overpaying for good-quality stocks is a real risk but not the chief one. The chief losses come from buying **low-quality securities at times of favorable business conditions**.
- Buyers treat current good earnings as earning power and prosperity as safety. In such years inferior bonds and preferreds sell near par for a little extra yield or a deceptive conversion privilege, and common stocks of obscure companies are floated far above tangible investment on two or three years of excellent growth.
- These have no adequate margin in any admissible sense. Fair-weather investments bought at fair-weather prices suffer disturbing declines when the horizon clouds over, often sooner, and recovery cannot be counted on because there was never a real margin.
- [ ] Before buying anything in a boom, ask: would its coverage or earning power have passed in a subnormal year? Was it issued, priced or rated on recent peak results?

## 12. The cost of loss and break-even arithmetic

- "Don't lose" (J. K. Klingenstein's summary of how to get rich). The biggest bull-market risk-takers are usually hurt worst in the next bear market, and big losses push people to gamble harder to get back.
- Worked example: a stock you expect to grow 10% a year vs a market growing 5%. You overpay; it loses 50% in year one, then earns 10% a year. Market path: $10,000 x 1.05^n. Your path: $5,000 x 1.10^(n-1). It takes more than 16 years to overtake the market, purely because of the initial overpayment.
- Recovery rule: a loss of L requires a gain of L / (1 - L) to recover (50% loss -> 100% gain; 95% loss -> 1,900% gain).
- Worked example, JDS Uniphase: four quarters to Dec 1999 sales $673 million, loss $313 million, tangible assets $1.5 billion; on March 7, 2000 the stock hit $153.42 (split-adjusted), about $143 billion of market value; end-2002 price $2.47. Years to get back to the purchase price = ln(153.42 / 2.47) / ln(1 + r):

| Annual return | Years to break even |
|---|---|
| 5% | 84.6 |
| 10% | 43.3 |
| 15% | 29.5 |
| 20% | 22.6 |
| 25% | 18.5 |
| 30% | 15.7 |
| 40% | 12.3 |
| 50% | 10.2 |

- Losing some money is inevitable; your responsibility is never to lose most or all of it. Refusing to overpay keeps wealth from vanishing.
- Risk lives in what kind of investor you are; meaning of risk shifts with the mood (in 1999, earning less than the neighbor; in 2003, losing what was left). Decision checklists (well-calibrated confidence, correctly anticipated regret): core-principles.md. Paul Slovic: risk is brewed from probabilities and consequences in equal measure.

## 13. Consequences over probabilities (Pascal's wager)

- Pascal's agnostic must bet his conduct on whether God exists; reason cannot decide the probability, but the consequences are clear and certain. Peter Bernstein's lesson: in decisions under uncertainty, the consequences must dominate the probabilities, because we never know the future.
- You will almost certainly make at least one big mistake in an investing lifetime, and those odds are beyond your control; the cost of being wrong is within it.
- Evidence of the wrong side of the wager: a 1999 Money Magazine online survey of 1,338 Americans found nearly one-tenth had at least 85% of their money in Internet stocks.
- Rule: keep holdings permanently diversified and refuse to throw money at Mr. Market's latest fashion, so no mistake is catastrophic. Then whatever the market does, you can say "This, too, shall pass away" (Lincoln's legend of an Eastern monarch's motto, true in all times and situations).

## 14. GEICO and the one big decision

- The classic partnership's approach (a net-net and bargain fund, 1936-56): safe and careful rather than trying to make all the money in the world; combined profit possibilities with sound values; avoided anything overpriced; sold rather too quickly when issues reached unattractive levels; always 100+ issues; averaged about 20% a year on several million dollars of capital over many years.
- Evidence net of fees: the partnership gained at least 14.7% a year from 1936 to 1956, against 12.2% for the stock market as a whole. The ~20% average appears not to include management fees, so quote the net figure when citing the method's real-money record.
- In 1948 the fund was offered a half interest in a growing company (GEICO) that lacked Wall Street appeal and had been rejected by several important houses. Decisive factor: a price moderate relative to current earnings and asset value. Size: about one-fifth of the fund by one account; $712,500, about 25% of assets, by another.
- The deal nearly failed because the partners insisted the price be 100% covered by asset value; a future gain of $300 million+ turned on about $50,000 of accounting items, and by luck they got what they demanded. The buyers had established they could liquidate GEICO for at least what they paid, so financial risk was small; the courage required was psychological.
- The shares rose to 200 times or more the price paid, far ahead of profit growth, and looked too high by their own standards almost from the start; treating it as a "family business," they and many fund holders kept large stakes and became millionaires. An SEC technicality forced the fund to distribute the GEICO shares; 100 fund shares at the start of 1948 ($11,413) held with the distribution were worth $1.66 million by 1972. Buffett made a huge GEICO bet of his own in 1976 when it was near bankruptcy.
- The profit from this one decision far exceeded the sum of all others in 20 years of wide-ranging, laboriously researched operations.
- The morals: there are several ways to make and keep money in Wall Street; one lucky break or one supremely shrewd decision (they are hard to tell apart) may count for more than a lifetime of journeyman effort; behind it there must be preparation and disciplined capacity, enough standing that opportunities come to your door, and the means, judgment and courage to seize them.
- The modern corrective: successful investing is managing risk, not avoiding it, but most investors must diversify; GEICO was itself called a lucky break, a signal most investors cannot count on finding one. Uncertainty is the permanent condition of investing; to invest at all you must believe in a better tomorrow.

## 15. Translating to today

- The classic 9%-vs-4% example and the 8.33%-vs-7.5% problem are 1965 and 1972 levels. The method is timeless: earnings yield on normalized (5+ year) earnings minus the current high-grade bond yield, accumulated over 10 years, should be a large fraction (about half) of the price. In low-rate eras a positive spread can coexist with high P/Es; check that earnings are normalized (cyclically adjusted) and compare with market-level.md before relying on it.
- When high-grade yields exceed the market's normalized earnings yield, the classic verdict is that ordinary stocks carry no margin; respond through allocation (portfolio-policy.md) rather than market timing.
- The railroad 5x pretax coverage figure is one classic example; use the industry table in security-analysis.md.
- The bond-like-margin test for an all-equity company should use current bond yields and include lease and pension obligations as debt.
- Diversification numbers (20+ stocks; the classic partnership's 100+) are ratios to the margin, not fixed counts: the thinner the margin per holding, the more holdings you need.
