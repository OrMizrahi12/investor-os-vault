## 1. Rules for acting on price moves

- [ ] Treat a quotation as an offer you may accept or ignore, never as an instruction. Why: the investor is almost never forced to sell, so the quote matters only when the price is favorable enough to justify acting.
- [ ] Never buy a stock because it has gone up or sell one because it has gone down. Simplified form: never buy right after a substantial rise, never sell right after a substantial drop. Why: the move itself carries no information about value.
- [ ] After a sharp fall in a holding, re-examine the business for a miscalculation. If the study reassures you, ignore the decline as temporary, or buy more if you have the cash and the nerve (the A&P case). Why: you lose nothing beyond any real shrinkage in intrinsic value.
- [ ] After a big rise, ask three questions and resist the fourth: Has it risen too high to keep? Should I regret not buying more? Is it time to sell some? And never: should I join the crowd and make larger commitments?. Why: enthusiasm, overconfidence and greed are contagious even to sensible investors.
- [ ] Prefer pricing to timing: buy when quoted below fair value, sell when well above it; at minimum, never overpay. The defensive investor needs only the minimum form.
- [ ] Do not wait for a bear market before buying. Buy whenever you have money for stocks, except when the general level is far above well-established value standards (how to judge the level: market-level.md). Why: waiting costs dividend income and may miss opportunities; bargains in individual issues exist at any time.
- [ ] Use a mechanical rule to vary the stock/bond mix (sell stocks into bonds as the market rises, reverse as it falls) mainly for its behavioral effect: it gives you something to do and makes you act opposite to the crowd. Modern update (2003): today's form is periodic rebalancing (mechanics in portfolio-policy.md §3).
- [ ] Do not try to pick the stocks or industries that will outperform over the next few months. Why: thousands of traders and first-class analysts compete there, and their work is self-neutralizing.
- [ ] Give each holding a good, hard look from time to time, not hawk-like watching. Why: businesses change character over the years, more often for the worse.
- [ ] Keep any speculation knowing it will probably lose in the end, limited in size and in a separate account (rules in core-principles.md §2).

Response patterns (map the user's words to the rule; each ends with a business question, not a price forecast):

| User says | Respond with |
|---|---|
| "My stock is down 30%, should I sell?" | §2 procedure: what changed in earning power and balance sheet? If nothing, the drop is Mr. Market's offer, not news; expect such moves (§4). |
| "It doubled; should I buy more before it runs further?" | Never buy because it rose. Re-value it. If the price is far above value, trimming is pricing; adding is speculation. |
| "I'm holding cash until the crash." | Waiting pays only if you later buy enough lower to beat the lost income. Classic rule: invest when you have money unless the market is far above value standards (market-level.md); otherwise dollar-cost average. |

| "I check my portfolio several times a day." | Andreassen's finding and the house test (§9); set a review schedule (e.g., twice a year at rebalancing). |

## 2. Mr. Market: the parable and the operating procedure

**The parable (paraphrased).** You own a small share, costing $1,000, in a private business. One partner, Mr. Market, tells you every day what he thinks your share is worth and offers either to buy you out or to sell you more at that figure. Sometimes his idea of value is plausible given the business's progress. Often his enthusiasm or his fears run away with him, and his price looks close to silly. A sensible owner lets Mr. Market's daily figure set his own view of value only when he agrees with it or wants to trade. He sells gladly when Mr. Market quotes a ridiculously high price and buys gladly when the price is low. The rest of the time he forms his own estimate from the company's reports on operations and financial position.

What the parable implies:
- The listed-stock owner has a double status: a part owner of a business (value tied to profits and assets) and the holder of a paper that can be sold in minutes at a price often far from balance-sheet value. He may choose whichever status suits him at any moment.
- Liquidity means only two things: you get the market's daily appraisal, worth whatever it is worth, and you can add or subtract at that price if you choose. It does not force the quote on someone who takes his idea of value from another source.
- Large price moves deserve attention, because judgment needs something to work on. They can be read as warning signals, but such signals prove misleading at least as often as helpful.
- The basic advantage: the investor who lets himself be stampeded or unduly worried by unjustified declines turns his basic advantage into a basic disadvantage. He would be better off with no quotation at all, spared the anguish of other people's mistakes of judgment. This may be the single most important idea in the framework.
- A missing quote is not safety. In 1931-33, owners of unquoted real-estate first mortgages told themselves nothing had been lost, while owners of better listed bonds felt poorer. The listed holders were actually better off: they could sell, switch into better bargains, or ignore the quotes. It is self-deception to claim no shrinkage just because nothing is quoted.

Modern restatement: most of the time the market prices most stocks about right; sometimes it is very wrong. Mr. Market overpays when stocks rise and dumps them below worth when they fall. Do business with him only when it serves you: his job is to supply prices, yours to decide whether acting on them is to your advantage. Refusing to let him be your master turns him into your servant. Even while he destroys value in one place he creates bargains elsewhere (1999: Wilshire 5000 +23.8%, yet 3,743 of its 7,234 stocks fell).

**Operating procedure when a user reports a big move in a holding:**
1. State the move in percent, not points (see §9).
2. Ask what changed in the business (earnings power, balance sheet, competitive position) since purchase. Get the facts from filings, not from the price.
3. If value fell: re-appraise; sell only if the new value is below the price by a margin you would not accept on a fresh purchase.
4. If value did not fall: holding is the default. Adding is reasonable for an investor with spare cash who would buy the stock fresh at this price (check against the user's allocation band).
5. If the price rose far above value: selling (or trimming via rebalancing) is legitimate pricing, not timing.
6. Never recommend action because of the price trend itself.

## 3. Timing vs pricing; why forecasts and formulas fail

Definitions: **timing** = trying to anticipate the market, buying or holding when the course is expected to be up and selling or abstaining when expected down. **Pricing** = buying below fair value and selling above it.

Why timing fails the investor:
- The general public cannot profit from forecasts: if everyone sells at the same signal, who buys? To profit you must beat countless competitors doing the same thing, and the average investor is himself part of that public.
- Timing matters psychologically to the speculator, who wants quick profits. The investor gains from waiting in cash only if he later buys enough lower to offset the dividends forgone. So timing is worthless to an investor unless it coincides with pricing, i.e., unless it lets him repurchase substantially under his previous selling price.
- This position is the opposite of Wall Street's: brokers and services insist everyone should watch market forecasts; the farther from Wall Street, the more skepticism.

Worked example, the timing break-even (illustrative numbers applying the timing-vs-pricing test):
- Inputs: a user sells an index holding at 100, expecting a decline. The holding yields 3% in dividends; cash earns 1%. He plans to wait about 18 months. Round-trip trading costs are 0.5%.
- Computation:
  - Income forgone: (3% − 1%) × 1.5 years = 3.0% of value.
  - Plus trading costs: 0.5%.
  - Break-even repurchase price: about 100 − 3.5 = 96.5.
  - If the sale realized a taxable gain, the tax is a further immediate cost, which lowers the break-even again.
- Conclusion: the move counts as pricing only if he can buy back substantially below about 96, not merely below 100. If the market instead rises 15% meanwhile, he must either rebuy near 115 or stay out indefinitely, which is the formula planners' fate in §3. A value-based reason to sell (price well above appraised value) is the only defensible version of this trade.

Why trading formulas decay (the general law is in core-principles.md §7):
- **Dow theory.** Buy on an upside "breakthrough" of the averages, sell on a downside one. The calculated (not actual) record showed almost unbroken profits 1897 to the early 1960s, including a sell signal at 306 about a month before the 1929 crash and staying out until 84 in 1933. After 1938, once taken seriously, it mostly took followers out at a good price and put them back higher; for nearly 30 years buy-and-hold on the DJIA did appreciably better. Robert Ross reported that the buy signals of December 1966 and December 1970 were both well below the preceding sell points.
- **Formula investment plans** (popular from 1949-50): except for dollar-cost averaging, all sold stocks automatically as the market rose, some down to zero equity, some keeping a minimum. They looked logical, conservative and great in back-tests, and peaked in popularity just as they stopped working: many planners were out of stocks by the mid-1950s and the market ran away from them. Modern update: many would have sold everything at end-1954 after a 52.6% year (then the second-best on record) and watched stocks double over the next five years.
- **A classic "central value method"** for DJIA buy/sell levels failed the same way.
- Two causes: new conditions the formula does not fit, and popularity itself changing market behavior (a Dow-theory stampede is a danger, not a help, to the public trader). Add regression to the mean and crowds piling in. Spinoza: "All things excellent are as difficult as they are rare."
- Classic buy-low/sell-high on full bear and bull cycles worked tolerably before 1950 but failed after 1949. Conclusion: do not base policy on waiting for demonstrable bear-market levels; instead vary the stock/bond ratio within the 25-75% band by value standards. Institutions call this tactical asset allocation.
- The five classic signs of bull-market tops, and a record of classic market calls, are in market-level.md §3 and §5.

Forecast failures to cite when a user leans on a strategist:
- March 10, 2000, the NASDAQ peak at 5048.62: a leading technical analyst forecast 6000 within 12-18 months. Five weeks later, at 3321.29, another strategist saw only 200-300 points of downside and 2000 of upside. NASDAQ bottomed at 1114.11 on October 9, 2002.
- March 2001: a top strategist forecast S&P 500 at 1,650 and Dow 13,000 for year-end, no recession. Actual: 1148.08 and 10,021.50, 30% and 23% below.
- Timing services: a Duke University study found that following the best tenth of market-timing newsletters earned 12.6% a year from 1991 through 1995, against 16.4% for a plain stock index fund, before subscription fees, extra trading costs and short-term taxes. When a user cites a timing service, note that even the top tenth trailed the index by about 4 points a year.
- "Missing the worst days" studies: claims that skipping the market's few worst days each year would have multiplied returns assume you know those days in advance. Hindsight is perfect; foresight is not. Treat such claims as sleight of hand, and rebalance or dollar-cost average instead.

## 4. How much fluctuation to expect

- **Rule of expectation:** expect, as a probability rather than a possibility, that most of your holdings will at various times in the next five years rise 50% or more from their low and fall the equivalent one-third or more from their high. This applies whatever you own and whether or not the market as a whole rises. Arithmetic: $10 +50% = $15; $15 −33% = $10. If you cannot live with that, you are not yet an investor.
- A 50% decline fully offsets a 100% advance.
- Index vs components: an index fluctuates less than its members because they peak and trough at different times. Second-line (non-S&P 500) companies swing more, but well-established smaller companies need not do worse over long periods.
- Conservative-portfolio proxy: DJIA about 890 average in 1964, high 995 in 1966 (985 in 1968), low 631 in 1970, about 940 in early 1971.
- Cycle history 1897-1949 (ten cycles; advances 44-500%, declines 24-89%): market-level.md §6.
- Bull-market amnesia: October 1990 to January 2000 the Dow never fell more than 20%, dropped 10%+ only three times, and gained 395.7% before dividends. That was the second-longest uninterrupted bull market of the century, after 1949-1961. After about five years many people stop believing bear markets can happen.

## 5. Price vs business value: the quality paradox and the near-book rule

The paradox: successful companies sell well above book value. The bigger the premium, the less certain the basis of value and the more the price depends on market moods. So the better the company, the wider its price swings are likely to be. In a real sense, the higher the quality of a common stock, the more speculative it may be compared with unspectacular middle-grade issues. (This excludes companies that are speculative because the business itself is.) Paying big premiums over assets gives hostages to fortune: you depend on the market to validate your purchase.
- Examples: IBM fell from 607 to 300 in seven months of 1962-63 and, after two splits, from 387 to 219 in 1970. Xerox fell from 171 to 87 in 1962-63 and from 116 to 65 in 1970. Neither drop reflected doubts about growth, only lost confidence in the premium valuation.
- Durand's 1957 "Petersburg paradox" article makes the point: if growth could last forever, any price would be justified. The higher and longer the assumed growth, the costlier a tiny error. (Growth-stock math: security-analysis.md §4.)

**Near-book rule for the conservative stock buyer. All four conditions are required:**
- [ ] Price close to tangible-asset value: not more than about one-third above it (P/TBV ≤ ~1.33). Treat the premium as a fee for listing and marketability.
- [ ] A satisfactory ratio of earnings to price.
- [ ] A sufficiently strong financial position.
- [ ] A prospect that earnings will at least be maintained over the years.
- Why: purchases at such levels have support from the balance sheet independent of market moods. The holder can take a detached view of fluctuations as long as earning power holds, and can sometimes buy low and sell high. The prescription is easy to fill except in dangerously high markets, provided you give up demanding above-average growth prospects.
- A low price to book alone does not make a sound investment.
- 1970-72 evidence: more than half the DJIA issues met the asset test at end-1970; AT&T sold below tangible asset value; most electric utilities were near asset value (details in defensive-selection.md).
- Compute tangible book per share: total shareholders' equity minus goodwill, trademarks and other intangibles, divided by fully diluted shares. Net asset value, book value, balance-sheet value and tangible-asset value are synonyms here. `scripts/value_screen.py` reports price / tangible book as an INFO row against the 1.33x guide (it uses `shareholders_equity`, `intangibles`, `preferred_stock` and `diluted_shares`).

## 6. Judging management by the long-run average price

- Apply to management the same yardstick you apply to your own results: dividends received plus the long-range trend of the average market price.
- Managements are not responsible for fluctuations unrelated to value. But only shareholder inattention lets them escape responsibility for a permanently depressed, unsatisfactory price level. Good managements produce good average market prices, bad ones bad prices.
- Use: if a stock has sat for years at a large discount to the value of its assets or earning power, treat it as a management and governance question (management-and-dividends.md), not as Mr. Market's fault.

## 7. Bond, convertible and preferred price fluctuations

Rules:
- [ ] High-grade bonds of 7 years or less, and redeemable savings bonds, need no attention to price; longer bonds and all stocks will fluctuate.
- [ ] Choose long vs short maturity mainly by personal preference. Bond-price prediction is completely impossible, even harder than stock prediction. The modern intermediate-maturity and ladder advice is in portfolio-policy.md §5.
- [ ] If you take a long bond's higher yield, accept its price swings. Consider valuing high-grade bonds mentally at amortized cost, as banks and insurers do, and ignoring the quotes.
- [ ] Do not expect convertibles to combine high-grade safety with stock upside. Their prices move with three things: the common's price, the issuer's credit standing and general interest rates. Many issuers are rated well below the best (full treatment in convertibles-warrants.md).
- [ ] Nonconvertible preferreds: the safe ones suit corporate holders, which get a tax advantage. Poorer ones swing about as widely as common stocks (Dec 1968-Dec 1970: lower-grade preferreds averaged −17% vs −11.3% for the S&P composite).

Mechanics:
- Price moves inversely to yield, but less than proportionately, because the fixed repayment at 100 moderates it. For very long maturities, price and yield change at nearly the same rate.
- Safety of principal and interest does not prevent large price swings.

- The Northern Pacific 3s fell in 1940 on safety doubts, reached an all-time high a few years later, then lost two-thirds of their price mainly because rates rose.
- Prime municipals: yield 3.2% (January 1965) → 7% (June 1970); price index 110.8 → 67.5. Mid-1970 long high-grade yields were the highest in nearly 200 years of U.S. history. About 25 years earlier, long municipals had yielded as little as 1% and industrials 2.40%, against 4.5-5% once considered "normal". Moral: nothing important on Wall Street happens exactly as before. Modern update: in 2002 long Treasury yields hit their lowest since 1963, so investors were keenest to buy bonds just when future returns were almost guaranteed to be low.
- Rating scale: the top three grades are Aaa, Aa, A (Moody's) and AAA, AA, A (S&P), going down to D.
- The classic proposed "bond of the future": interest floating with an index of going rates, so principal stays near 100, or a contract guaranteeing minimums for both interest and principal over about 20 years. 1971 precedents: floating-rate notes of the Italian state electricity concern, and Toronto-Dominion Bank 7%-8% debentures due 1991 with a holder put in 1976. Inflation-protected Treasuries (TIPS) are the improved modern version.

## 8. Why not to play the professionals' game; control the controllable

Professional handicaps. These are why an individual cannot beat the pros at their own game, and why the pros cannot win it either:
- Size forces funds into the biggest stocks, so many end up owning the same overpriced giants.
- Money flows in as markets rise and gets spent on more of the same stocks, pushing prices higher.
- Redemptions in declines force sales at low prices.
- Bonuses tied to beating benchmarks drive compulsive buying of index additions. Missing a winner is a career risk; owning a popular loser is not.
- Style-box mandates force sales when a stock becomes too big, too small, too cheap or too dear.

What you can control instead:
- [ ] Brokerage costs: trade rarely, patiently, cheaply.
- [ ] Ownership costs: refuse funds with excessive expenses (thresholds in funds-and-advisers.md).
- [ ] Expectations: forecast returns with realism (market-level.md §4).
- [ ] Risk: choose how much of total assets is exposed to stocks, diversify, rebalance.
- [ ] Taxes: hold at least one year, ideally at least five.
- [ ] Above all, your own behavior.

Default for a long horizon (25-30+ years), modern update: buy every month automatically, plus whenever spare cash appears, into a total-market index fund; sell only when you need the money. Measure success by whether your plan and discipline will get you where you need to go, not by beating the market or other people. One retiree put it this way: he did not care whether he beat the market, only that his investments earned enough to retire where he wanted.

## 9. Behavioral findings and guardrails

Findings to cite:
- **Prediction addiction.** People told a sequence is random still try to predict it. After an event repeats just two or three times, brain regions (anterior cingulate, nucleus accumbens) expect it to recur, and a confirmed repeat releases dopamine. Rising stocks literally feel good and breed addiction to one's own forecasts.
- **Fear circuitry.** Losses activate the amygdala's fight-or-flight response; fear in a crash is involuntary, so plan for it rather than hoping to feel calm.
- **Loss aversion** (Kahneman and Tversky): a loss hurts more than twice as much as an equal gain pleases. This drives selling near bottoms and refusing to buy more.
- **News frequency hurts returns.** Paul Andreassen's late-1980s experiments: investors given frequent news updates earned about half the returns of those given none.
- **Imitating Mr. Market.** U.S. 401(k) contributions averaged 8.6% of pay in 1999 and fell by nearly a quarter to 7% by 2002, so people bought less as stocks got cheaper.
- Classic observation (1972): the main cause of individual investors' failure is paying too much attention to what the market is doing now.
- Damasio: you gain control by admitting how little control you have. So pre-commit: dollar-cost average, rebalance, sign a contract, and put much of the portfolio on autopilot.

Guardrails to apply in conversation:
- [ ] **Percent framing.** A 100-point drop at Dow 8,000 is 1.2%, the same as a temperature falling from 81 to 80 degrees. Restate every move as a percentage of value.
- [ ] **House test.** Would you call an agent for your home's price at 1:24 and again at 1:37, and sell if it moved? Does not knowing the price stop the house appreciating? Treat a long-term portfolio the same way.
- [ ] **Ten-year no-quote test.** Be comfortable owning the stock or fund even if prices stopped being published for 10 years.
- [ ] **Bear-market reframe.** For anyone still accumulating, falling prices are good news: the longer and further prices fall while you keep buying, the more you end up with, provided you hold on. Picture a news network that reports every decline as a sale.
- [ ] **Breadth reminder.** Even in a euphoric index year most stocks may be falling; cheap, unglamorous companies are where the next winners often hide (data in §12).

## 10. Tax-loss harvesting (2003 U.S. law; verify current rules)

The one legitimate reason to sell into a bear market is a tax benefit.
- Rule as of early 2003: realized capital losses offset realized gains and up to $3,000 a year of ordinary income, with the excess carried forward. Rebuying the same security within 30 days disallows the loss (wash sale), so wait at least 31 days.
- Worked example (assuming no other realized gains in 2002 and no reinvested dividends):
  - Input: 200 Coca-Cola shares bought January 2000 at $60 = $12,000; worth $44 at year-end 2002 = $8,800.
  - Action: sell before year-end to realize a $3,200 loss; wait 31 days; rebuy 200 shares.
  - Result: deduct $3,000 from 2002 income, carry $200 to 2003, and still own the same company at a cost basis almost one-third lower.
- Cautions: easy to mishandle; read the tax authority's guidance on investment income and wash sales and consult a tax adviser. Thresholds, holding periods and wash-sale rules change and differ by country. Tax placement and holding-period rules are in portfolio-policy.md §10.

## 11. The investment owner's contract

The pre-commitment device: a short signed statement, written before temptation arrives, that binds the investor to a plan. Use the reworded version in assets/investment_policy_template.md §7 rather than drafting from scratch. Its elements:
- [ ] A statement of purpose: building wealth over many years, and an admission that rising prices will tempt buying and falling prices will tempt selling.
- [ ] A promise not to trade on either temptation, or on what the crowd is doing.
- [ ] A fixed automatic contribution (amount, frequency, vehicle), with more added only from money the investor can afford to see fall for a while.
- [ ] A minimum holding date, ten or more years out.
- [ ] Named exceptions only: an emergency (health, job loss) or a planned expense (home, tuition).
- [ ] A rule to reread it before any off-plan trade, a signature and a witness, and a place where it is easy to find.

## 12. Cases, grouped by lesson

**The market can misprice a sound business at both extremes; the alert investor can use the errors.**
- A&P. Listed in 1929, high 494; 1932 low 104 with earnings nearly intact; 1936 range 111-131; 1938 low 36. At 36 the preferred and common together were worth $126 million against $85 million of cash alone and $134 million of working capital: America's largest retailer, a consistent large earner, valued below liquidation value. Causes: a feared chain-store tax (groundless), one year of lower profits, a depressed market. A 1937 buyer at about 80 (12 times five-year average earnings) should have re-checked the business, then ignored the drop or bought more. In 1939 it reached 117 1/2, three times the 1938 low. In 1961 the split stock hit 70 1/2 (equal to 705 on the old shares), 30 times earnings against the DJIA's 23, a price unsupported by the record. A year later it had more than halved to 34, and this time it was not a bargain. It reached 21 1/2 in 1970 and 18 in 1972 after the first quarterly loss in its history; 1968 profit was below 1958's, and it paid confusing small stock dividends not warranted by retained earnings. Modern update: year-end prices $27.875 (1999), $7.00 (2000), $23.78 (2001), $8.06 (2002) for a stable grocery business. Lessons: the market often goes far wrong, and businesses change in quality over time, so re-check them.
- Inktomi. Peaked at $231.625 on March 17, 2000, up about 1,900% since its June 1998 IPO and nearly tripled since December 1999. Quarterly revenue was $36 million, more than all of 1998; extrapolating the growth gave $5 billion a month within five years. Yet it had lost $6 million that quarter and $24 million in each of the two prior years, and had never made a profit. Market value: $25 billion, 250 times revenue. On September 30, 2002 it was $0.25, a value under $40 million, or 0.35 times revenue of $113 million. On December 23, 2002 Yahoo! agreed to pay $1.65 a share, nearly seven times that price. Lesson: Mr. Market's depression is as unjustified as his mania; extreme cheapness invites takeovers. Classic 1932 articles described Depression stocks priced below their cash, worth more liquidated than operating.

**Losers and winners trade places; breadth matters more than the index.**
- CMGI vs Berkshire Hathaway. In 1999 CMGI +939.9%, Berkshire −24.9%. Then CMGI −96% (2000), −70.9% (2001), −39.8% (2002), cumulative −99.3%; Berkshire +26.6%, +6.5%, −3.8%, cumulative +30%. On the NASDAQ peak day a well-known pundit said he had been tempted to short Berkshire, and a strategist asked whether to own Norfolk Southern or Cisco: over the next year Norfolk Southern rose 35% and Cisco fell 70%.

## 13. Translating to today

- Timeless: Mr. Market, pricing over timing, the 50%-up/one-third-down expectation, judging management by long-run average price, the inverse bond-price mechanics, the behavioral guardrails.
- The near-book rule (P/TBV ≤ ~1.33) was workable when half the DJIA sold near tangible book (1970). Today's large companies carry heavy intangibles, so few pass.
