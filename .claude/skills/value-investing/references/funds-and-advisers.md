# Funds and Advisers: Selecting, Supervising, Selling

Use this file when evaluating a mutual fund, ETF, closed-end fund or index fund, deciding whether to sell a fund, or choosing, interviewing and supervising a broker, planner, investment counselor or other adviser. The second business principle (do not let anyone run your business unless you can supervise them) is in core-principles.md §9.

## 1. Default decisions

- **Defensive investor:** a low-cost, broadly diversified total-market index fund as the core, with bonds or bond funds per portfolio-policy.md. Modern update (2003): it beats most funds over the long run and suits anyone holding 20+ years and adding monthly. Buffett's 1996 letter called index funds the best choice for most individuals too.
- **Active funds:** acceptable only after the §2 procedure. Expect at best slightly better than average from large, soundly run funds.
- **Closed-end funds:** buy only at a discount to net asset value (NAV), never at a premium.
- **Advisers:** if you rely mainly on others, either limit everyone to standard, conservative, even unimaginative investments, or have unusually intimate and favorable knowledge of the person directing your money elsewhere. Accept unconventional advice only to the extent that you can judge it yourself; at that point you have become an enterprising investor.
- **Expectation rule:** if you get advice without paying specifically for it, you are not entitled to, and should not expect, better-than-average results. Paid counsel should deliver results like those of an average well-informed, cautious investor, not exceptional ones.

Why funds anyway: in aggregate they have built saving habits, protected people from costly mistakes, and delivered returns in line with common stocks. The average all-fund investor of 1961-70 probably beat the average direct stock buyer, even though funds did no better than the market and cost more. The real alternative for most people was not a self-built balanced portfolio. It was a fund salesman at the door versus wilier peddlers of second- and third-rate new issues; a brokerage account pulls toward speculation.

## 2. Fund selection procedure (executable)

The modern order reverses the typical buyer's (performance, then manager reputation, then risk, then costs if ever). Run the steps in this order and stop at the first failure.

**Step 1: Costs (the most predictable factor).**
- [ ] Annual operating expenses no higher than these ceilings (modern update, 2003):

| Category | Max expense ratio |
|---|---|
| Taxable and municipal bonds | 0.75% |
| U.S. large and mid-cap stocks | 1.00% |
| High-yield (junk) bonds | 1.00% |
| U.S. small stocks | 1.25% |
| Foreign stocks | 1.50% |

- [ ] Trading costs: high turnover lowers returns. The typical 2003 fund held stocks only about 11 months. Trading costs ran about 1% a year for a $100 million fund and at least 2% for a $10 billion fund. With about 1.5% operating expenses plus about 2% trading costs, the average fund had to beat the market by about 3.5 percentage points before costs just to match it after costs.
- [ ] Sales loads: quoting the load as a percentage of the offering price, which includes the load, is a sales gimmick. Compute it on the net amount invested. Example: a 5.75% load on $10,000 = $575, leaving $9,425 invested, so the load is really 6.1%. (In the early 1970s: about 9% on small purchases.)
- [ ] Tax drag: frequent large taxable distributions from heavy trading count as a cost.
- [ ] Distribution timing: in a taxable account, check the fund's estimated capital-gain distribution and record dates before a large purchase late in the year (distributions are usually paid in the fourth quarter). Buy after the record date, or you owe tax on gains earned before you owned the fund.

**Step 2: Risk.**
- [ ] Find the worst calendar-quarter loss shown in the prospectus. If you could not bear losing at least that much in three months, go elsewhere.
- [ ] Highly volatile funds tend to stay volatile.

**Step 3: Manager and firm traits (the funds that do beat the index share them).**
- [ ] Managers are among the biggest shareholders; check whether filings show managers owning at least 1% of fund shares. Firms that require employees to invest only in their own funds align interests.
- [ ] The fund is cheap. Higher fees mean lower returns over time; high returns are temporary, high fees are close to permanent.
- [ ] It dares to be different: compare its holdings with the S&P 500 list; if they look like twins, shop elsewhere. (Peter Lynch at Magellan: Treasuries as the largest holding in 1982, then Chrysler when most expected bankruptcy, then about 20% foreign stocks in 1986.)
- [ ] It shuts the door to new money before, not after, it balloons.
- [ ] It does not advertise or hype its returns.

**Step 4: Asset growth (size kills performance).**
- [ ] Rapid inflows after hot returns force bad choices: hold cash, overload existing positions, or spread into names the manager liked less. Example: a $100 million fund putting 2% ($2 million) into a $500 million company owns under 0.5% of it. At $10 billion, 2% is $200 million, nearly half the company and beyond legal limits, so the fund must own far more stocks and spread attention thin.
- [ ] Classic evidence: of the ten best 1967 performers (gains of 84% to 301% that year), none was large; their average size was about $60 million. Smaller size appears necessary for continued outstanding results.

**Step 5: Ratings, then past performance (last, and lightly).**
- [ ] Star ratings look backward; five-star funds have tended to underperform one-star funds afterward. Consult them only after Steps 1-4.
- [ ] Past winners usually fade, but past losers almost never become winners. Avoid funds with consistently poor returns, especially if expenses are above average.
- [ ] Classic condition: comparative performance may be weighed over at least five years, provided that period was not one big rise in the market. In a big rise, spectacular results may only show the managers took undue speculative risk and got away with it for the time being.

**Ignore or distrust:**
- [ ] Last year's or last quarter's ranking; "hot" sector funds. The most popular sector of a year often ranks among the worst the next; sector funds are the investor's nemesis.
- [ ] Star managers likely to be poached or to leave (cases in §10).
- [ ] Incubated funds (run privately with tiny assets, then marketed on their record) and fee-waived funds. Their returns almost always fade once outside money arrives.
- [ ] The argument that higher fees buy better management; decades of research show the opposite.
- [ ] Style-box labels for their own sake.

Research consensus to cite: the average fund does not pick stocks well enough to overcome its research and trading costs; higher expenses mean lower returns; more trading means lower returns; volatile funds stay volatile; high past returns rarely persist.

## 3. Index funds: the cost arithmetic

- Index costs (2003): operating expenses about 0.2% a year plus trading costs about 0.1%. Average active stock fund: about 1.5% expenses plus about 2% trading.
- Worked example: assume stocks return 7% a year for 20 years.
  - Index fund nets just under 6.7%: $10,000 → more than $36,000.
  - Average fund nets about 3.5%: $10,000 → just under $20,000, nearly 50% less.
- Funnel of fund performance (Lipper, to December 31, 2002): share of U.S. stock funds that beat a large S&P 500 index fund:

| Period | Funds beating the index |
|---|---|
| 1 year | 1,186 of 2,423 (48.9%) |
| 3 years | 1,157 of 1,944 (59.5%) |
| 5 years | 768 of 1,494 (51.4%) |
| 10 years | 227 of 728 (31.2%) |
| 15 years | 125 of 445 (28.1%) |
| 20 years | 37 of 248 (14.9%) |

  The figures exclude funds that died (survivorship bias), so the real advantage of indexing is larger.
- The only real flaw is boredom: you can never brag about beating the market, because the index's job is to match it.
- Why funds cannot beat the market in aggregate: professionals collectively own so much of the market that their combined decisions determine the averages. End-1969 figures: insured commercial banks' trust departments held $181 billion of stocks, plus investment-adviser accounts, plus about $56 billion in mutual and similar funds.

## 4. Closed-end vs open-end funds

Rule: one of the few clearly evident rules for fund buyers. If you want funds, buy a group of closed-end funds at a discount of about 10-15% to NAV instead of paying about 9% above NAV for open-end shares. If both groups produce similar dividends and changes in asset value, you get about one-fifth more for your money.

Why the discount says nothing about results: 1961-70 data show the average of ten yearly results was +9.14% for closed-end funds, +9.95% for mutual funds and +9.79% for the S&P composite. Meanwhile the average closed-end discount ranged from about 3% to 14%, with a 7% premium in 1968. The price gap comes from salesmanship (open-end funds are sold by many thousands of salesmen; closed-end shares have no one promoting them), not from management quality.

**Worked example: does a widening discount hurt the closed-end buyer?**
- Inputs: Investor A buys open-end shares at 109% of NAV. Investor B buys closed-end shares at 85% of NAV plus a 1.5% commission. Over about four years both portfolios earn and pay out 30% of asset value and end at their starting NAV.
- A redeems at 100% of NAV: 30% − 9% = 21% on asset value, which is 19% on his money.
- B matches A's 19% only if he sells at 73% of NAV, a 27% discount. So the discount must widen by 12 points (roughly double) before the closed-end buyer does worse. An adverse change that large had rarely, if ever, happened.
- With a low-load or no-load open-end fund the closed-end edge shrinks but remains.

Premium funds:
- [ ] Do not pay a premium for supposedly superior management. In 1961-70, three diversified closed-end funds at premiums returned +115.0% over ten years (+25.4% for 1966-70; −5.2% in 1970) while ten at discounts returned +102.9% (+22.6%; +1.3%), and the premium group sold at 11.4% above NAV against a 9.2% discount for the other.
- [ ] Two old, large funds: General American Investors returned +165.6% over ten years at a 7.6% discount, while Lehman Corp. returned +108.0% at a 13.9% premium. The pricing was the reverse of the records.
- Three of the six premium funds of 1970 invested mainly abroad. By end-1970 one sold at a quarter of its high, another at a third, another under half.

Modern update (2003): diversified closed-end stock funds at a discount still tend to beat both premium closed-end funds and the average open-end fund (research by Donald Cassidy of Lipper). But only about 30 diversified U.S. equity closed-end funds remained, many tiny, illiquid, expensive or oddly specialized, and discounts are not always available. Closed-end municipal bond funds at a discount can amplify yield and are attractive if their expenses meet the §2 ceilings.

## 5. Judging past performance; the performance-fund warning signs

Classic evidence on large funds (the ten largest stock funds at end-1970, one per management group, with over $15 billion between them):
- Over 1961-70 the ten funds' combined results were about the same as the S&P 500 (about +105% over ten years against about +105%), slightly worse in 1961-65 (an average of about +72% against +77% for the S&P) and slightly better in 1966-70. They clearly beat the DJIA (+83%).
- Individual results ranged widely: from +44% to +152% over ten years.
- Growth funds did no better: the 45 growth funds with ten-year records gained an average 108% over 1961-70, against 105% for the S&P composite and 83% for the DJIA, and in 1969-70 most growth funds did worse than both indexes. Diversified growth investing paid no special reward even to specialists.
- The top five of 1961-65 mostly stayed ahead in 1966-70, though two slipped behind two of the other five.
- Conclusion: large, soundly managed funds produce at best slightly better than average results; unsoundly managed ones produce spectacular, largely illusory profits for a while, then calamitous losses. Funds that beat the market for ten years or more were scarce exceptions: specialized, with self-imposed limits on capital, and not actively sold to the public. Modern equivalent: funds closed to new investors.

Performance-fund warning signs:
- [ ] Young managers (in their 30s and 40s) whose whole experience is one long bull market (1948-68 in the classic warning; the 1990s in the modern one).
- [ ] A working definition of a sound investment as a stock likely to rise in the next few months.
- [ ] Big positions in newer ventures at prices out of all proportion to assets or earnings, justified by naive hope plus skill at exploiting public enthusiasm.
- [ ] Holdings at high multiples of current earnings, paying no or tiny dividends, with large speculative followings and spectacular price moves.
- [ ] Valuation tricks: restricted "letter stock" bought below market and immediately carried at full market value, booking an illusory profit.
- [ ] Manager enrichment out of line with investors' results (see the Manhattan Fund case in §10).
- [ ] Spectacular returns in a rising market (such results may only reflect undue risk).
- Moral: bright, energetic, usually young people have always promised miracles with other people's money, appeared to deliver for a while, and in the end brought their public losses. The specific abuses of the 1920s were outlawed, but new gadgets produced similar results in the 1960s. The urge to speculate, and its exploitation, will never be abolished.
- Modern timing heuristic: manias recur roughly every 35 years (late 1920s, late 1960s, late 1990s), perhaps as the investors who remember lose influence. On that reasoning, be especially vigilant around 2030.

## 6. When to sell a fund

Do not sell merely for underperforming for one, two or three years:
- Sequoia Fund trailed the S&P 500 in 12 of its 29 years from 1970 to 1999 (over 41% of the time) yet gained more than 12,500% against 4,900% for the index. It had been closed to new investors since 1982.
- Most funds lag because their style is temporarily out of favor. Firing a manager for doing what he promised locks in the loss and misses the recovery. Fund investors underperformed their own funds by 4.7 percentage points a year from 1998 to 2001, simply by buying high and selling low.
- Charles Ellis's rule: do not buy a fund you are not prepared to hold through at least three lean years.

Sell on these red flags:
- [ ] A sharp, unexpected change in strategy (e.g., a value fund loading up on technology in 1999, a growth fund buying piles of insurers in 2002).
- [ ] Rising expenses (managers lining their own pockets).
- [ ] Large, frequent tax bills from excessive trading.
- [ ] Suddenly erratic returns: a formerly conservative fund posts a big loss, or a giant gain.
- [ ] A switch into performance-fund behavior (§5).

## 7. Other fund types: balanced, bond, sector, ETFs, dual-purpose

- **Balanced funds**: in 1970 the 23 balanced funds in the Wiesenberger survey held 25%-59% in bonds and preferreds (average 40%) and paid only 3.9% on NAV, about 3.6% on offering price. Make the bond part of the portfolio directly instead: government savings bonds, corporates rated A or better, or tax-exempts. Modern update: for small bond amounts, a low-cost bond fund (portfolio-policy.md §5).
- **Sector and specialty funds:** treat as speculation (§2).
- **Exchange-traded index funds** (modern update, 2003): low cost; useful for narrow markets (a single country, one industry) or broad exposure. Then a drawback for regular small purchases because each purchase and reinvested dividend incurred a commission; check current brokerage terms.
- **Dual-purpose funds**: two classes, one getting all the income and the other all the capital gains. Modern update: popular in the late 1980s, essentially gone by 2003.
- **Hedge funds:** under 2003 U.S. rules, generally limited to investors with $200,000+ income or $1 million+ net worth. Apply the same cost-first and supervision tests.
- Taxation: most funds are pass-through "regulated investment companies" that distribute essentially all income and realized gains. A classic value partnership's 1948 in-kind distribution of GEICO shares to its holders was a rare exception.

## 8. Advisers: the classic framework

Basic thesis: investing is unique in relying on advice, and asking others to tell you how to make money is somewhat naive. Businessmen take advice on parts of their business, never on how to make a profit. Advisers legitimately protect clients from mistakes and secure the normal return their money is entitled to. Trouble starts when the client demands more than average or the adviser promises it.

Sources of advice, with the classic verdicts:

| Source | What to expect | How to use |
|---|---|---|
| Investment-counsel firms and bank trust departments (fee-based) | Modest promises; standard income-producing securities; rarely more than about 10% outside leading companies and government (including state and municipal) bonds; no serious market-swing trading. Aim: conserve principal and earn conservatively acceptable income. Chief value: shielding clients from costly mistakes. | As much as a defensive investor has a right to expect from any adviser serving the public. Modern update (2003): many now require $1 million+ (some $5 million+); independent financial planners do similar work, with funds in place of blue chips and diversification in place of quality ratings. |
| Financial services (subscription bulletins, statistical services) | Valuable data and business forecasts that help make prices fairly rational. Market forecasts are hedged in Delphic phrasing and have no real significance. Pervasive flaw: buy when near-term prospects look good and sell when they look bad, regardless of price. | Use for information and suggestions only, never as the sole basis for buying or selling. Ignore purely technical services; they do not concern investors. |
| Brokerage firms (customers' brokers, also called registered representatives or account executives) | Generally honest and knowledgeable, but paid by commissions and therefore speculation-minded. Wall Street thrived on speculation, at which speculators as a class lose, so it cannot be fully professional. A business has customers; a profession has clients. | State explicitly, in word and deed, that you are an investor and want no tips; a good broker will then cooperate. Modern update: the classic view was not cynical enough about Wall Street's ethics; the 1990s analyst scandals and online brokers that goaded overtrading proved it. |
| Security (financial) analysts | Detailed studies, comparisons, opinions of safety and value. Hampered by being expected to be market analysts too (asking whether a stock is sound often means asking whether it will rise soon). | Ask the right questions and show you are value-minded, not quotation-minded. A reputable firm's analyst can draw up a list of leading stocks for a defensive investor and certify whether their price level is conservative by past experience. Modern update: most sell-side analysts guess at prices rather than analyze businesses, and individuals rarely reach them. |
| Investment bankers (underwriters of new issues) | Constructive role in raising capital, but toward individuals they are salesmen facing inexperienced buyers who secretly want quick profits; protection rests on the seller's ethics. 1960-61 and 1968-69 saw floods of the lowest-quality issues at absurd prices, some backed by major houses. | Listen to reputable houses, but always apply independent judgment, your own if competent or another adviser's. It is imprudent to trust the seller's judgment. The prospectus discloses everything but is too long for most buyers to read. |
| Local commercial banker | Not an expert in security values, but experienced and conservative. | Useful as a steadying influence for the unskilled investor tempted away from a defensive policy; of little use to a bargain hunter. Modern update: the role has largely passed to accountants, lawyers and planners. |
| Relatives and friends | Almost as hard to choose well as securities; much bad advice is given free. | Be critical; do not assume superior knowledge. |

Summary rules by investor type:
- [ ] Defensive: be explicit, even repetitiously so, about what you will buy: high-grade bonds and leading companies' common stocks at prices not high by experience and analysis. You will not be able to judge recommendations yourself, so restrict their scope.
- [ ] Enterprising: work actively with advisers, demand detailed explanations, and pass your own judgment on every recommendation. Act on advice you do not understand and approve only where the adviser's integrity and competence have been thoroughly demonstrated.
- [ ] Everyone: be wary of anyone, broker or salesman, who promises spectacular income or profits, whether from security selection or from market trading.
- [ ] Custody (1971 advice, now dated): deal only with established exchange-member firms, and have deliveries and payments go through your bank. Brokerage capital had been held in partners' speculative stocks at inflated values, and 1969-70 saw failures and forced rescues; in 1970 seven firms failed. Modern update: investor-protection insurance (SIPC, 1970) and electronic settlement have replaced the bank step. Today, verify the broker's membership in the investor-protection scheme and segregation of client assets.

## 9. Choosing and supervising an adviser (executable)

**Do you need one?** Signals:
- [ ] Big losses: the portfolio lost more than 40% from the start of 2000 to the end of 2002 (worse than the market itself). Today, read it as losses far beyond the market's in a bear market.
- [ ] Busted budget: can't make ends meet, don't know where money goes, can't save regularly, pay bills late.
- [ ] Chaotic portfolio: "diversified" across dozens of similar holdings (39 Internet stocks, seven U.S. growth funds) that all move together. An asset-allocation plan is needed.
- [ ] Major life changes: self-employment retirement plans, aging parents, college costs, complex tax and retirement rules.
- An adviser can also give someone to blame and encouragement that keeps you investing through a bear market. There is no shame in either self-management or hiring help.

**Due diligence (trust, then verify)**
- [ ] Start with referrals from the few people you know best and trust most, or from fee-only planner associations.
- [ ] Search the adviser's and firm's names with terms such as fine, complaint, lawsuit, disciplinary action, suspension.
- [ ] Check the regulator for the adviser's type: securities regulators and the broker-registration database for brokers and insurance agents; state accountancy boards for accountants; the planners' certification board.
- [ ] Registered investment advisers file a disclosure form (Form ADV in the U.S.). Obtain your own complete copy from the regulator; some advisers have removed the disciplinary disclosure pages before handing it over.
- [ ] One complaint is not disqualifying; a persistent pattern is. A disciplinary action by regulators usually means find someone else.
- [ ] Credentials: CFA, CFP and CPA mean rigorous study and can be verified with the issuing body. Most other initials after a name signify little.

**Warning words (Maryland securities commissioner).** If an adviser keeps using these, or pressures you, contact the authorities quickly:
- guaranteed; can't lose; sure thing; no downside; we can cap your downside
- opportunity of a lifetime; you need to hurry; you can't afford not to own it; you'll be sorry if you don't
- offshore; prime bank; exclusive; no one else knows how to do this
- our proprietary computer model; active asset-allocation strategy; options strategy; commodities trading; monthly returns
- focus on performance, not fees; we can beat the market; the smart money is buying it
- this baby's gonna move; the upside is huge; it's a no-brainer; don't you want to be rich?
- trust me; I'm putting my mother in it

**Interview the adviser (questions from leading planners), with automatic disqualifiers:**
- [ ] Why are you in this business? What is your firm's mission? (Looking for real care for clients.)
- [ ] What is your investing philosophy? Stocks or funds? Do you use technical analysis? Market timing? **Yes to either of the last two = do not hire.**
- [ ] Do you cover taxes, estate and retirement planning, budgeting, debt and insurance, or only asset management? What qualifies you for each?
- [ ] What do your clients' needs have in common? How will you help me reach my goals, track progress and report it? Is there an implementation checklist?
- [ ] How do you choose investments, and what evidence of success can you show for clients?
- [ ] What do you do when an investment does poorly for a whole year? **"Sell" = do not hire.**
- [ ] Do you accept any compensation from third parties for recommendations? Why, when, how much?
- [ ] What will I pay in actual dollars in year one, and what would change it? **Fees over 1% of assets a year: probably shop elsewhere.**
- [ ] How many clients, how often do you communicate, who handles my calls, how long do clients stay, what was your worst client experience and how did you resolve it, why did the last client who fired you leave, any formal complaints?
- [ ] May I see a sample statement? **If you can't understand it, or his explanation of it, he is not right for you.**
- [ ] What average annual return is feasible on my portfolio? **Anything over 8-10% is unrealistic** (2003 view; scale to current yields and valuations, see market-level.md §4).
- [ ] Will you give me your résumé, your disclosure form and at least three references? **Refusing the required disclosure form = leave immediately.**
- If you have less than about $100,000 to invest you may not find an adviser who will take you. Use a diversified set of low-cost index funds and follow the behavioral rules until you can afford one.

**A good adviser interviews you too.** Expect questions like: why do you want an adviser; what are your long-term goals; what frustrated you with past advisers, including yourself; do you have a budget and live within your means; what percentage of assets do you spend each year; what must be accomplished in a year for you to be satisfied; how do you handle disagreements; how did you react emotionally to the last bear market; what are your worst fears and greatest hopes; what return is reasonable (base it on the expected-return arithmetic in portfolio-policy.md §4). An adviser who asks none of these is a poor fit.

**Supervision systems, created jointly before a dollar is invested (Robert Veres):**
- [ ] A comprehensive financial plan: how you will earn, save, spend, borrow and invest.
- [ ] An investment policy statement: your fundamental approach (assets/investment_policy_template.md).
- [ ] An asset-allocation plan: how much goes into each category.
- Purpose statement, after Nick Murray: you hire an adviser "not to manage money but to manage you." The adviser's job is to stand between you and your worst impulses.

## 10. Cases, grouped by lesson

**Hot performance attracts money just before it collapses.**
- Manhattan Fund. Organized end-1965; first offering of 27 million shares at $9.25-$10 raised $247 million. It held high-multiple, no-dividend, heavily speculated stocks. Annual results against the S&P composite: 1966 −6% vs −10.1%; 1967 +38.6% vs +23.0%; 1968 −7.3% vs +10.4%; 1969 −13.3% vs −8.3%; 1970 −36.9% vs +3.5%; 1971 +9.6% vs +13.5%. Two of its largest holdings at end-1969 filed for bankruptcy within six months, and a third faced creditors' actions in 1971. The end-1969 portfolio included stocks at roughly 80 to 130 times earnings, and one at about 490 times. The founder sold his management company, which had less than $1 million of assets, for more than $20 million in stock. It was perhaps the greatest gap ever between the results for the manager and for the people whose money he managed. Lesson: performance chasing plus manager enrichment is the classic signature.
- "The Money Managers" (1969 book profiling 19 stars). Results were available for 12: good in 1966, brilliant in 1967, mixed in 1968, all lost in 1969 (only one slightly beat the S&P), and worse in 1970. Lesson: a track record built in one market phase proves little.
- Crash-and-burn club. Ten of the hottest 1999 funds gained 211.6% to 291.2% that year. Three years later $10,000 invested on 1/1/1999 was worth $829 to $7,327 each, against $7,780 in the total-market index (+23.8% in 1999). One leveraged fund went +233.2%, −73.7%, −69.1%, −69.4% (→ $829). Lesson: the hottest sector turns cold suddenly; never buy on past returns alone.
- Jacob Internet Fund. Launched by a 29-year-old after a 216% year at his previous fund; nearly $300 million poured in early in 2000; it lost 79.1% (2000), 56.4% (2001) and 13% (2002), cumulative −92%.
- National Student Marketing. A fraudulent "growth" company held by Manhattan Fund, by elite university endowments and by prestigious bank trust departments. Lesson: sophisticated holders are no protection.

**Star managers leave.** Glen Bickerstaff gained 47.5% in 1997, was hired away in mid-1998, and his old fund lagged in three of the next four years. Erin Sullivan nearly tripled shareholders' money from 1997 and left to start a hedge fund in 2000; her former fund then lost more than three-quarters of its value in three years. Lesson: you buy the fund, not the manager.

**Patience with a sound fund pays.** Sequoia (§6).

**Advice industries have conflicts.** The 1969-70 broker failures, where partners speculated with the capital meant to protect customers; the 1960-61 and 1968-69 new-issue floods; the late-1990s Internet IPOs (the classic new-issue rules would have kept investors out).

## 11. Translating to today

- Expense ceilings are 2003 numbers. Broad index funds now cost a small fraction of the 0.2% cited in 2003, so tighten every ceiling proportionally. Prefer low-cost index funds in each category and require an active fund's net-of-fee record to justify any premium.
- The ~9% load and 1970 fund counts are history. Front loads still exist; always compute them on the amount actually invested.
- Closed-end discounts, the 10-15% buy zone and the "never pay a premium" rule still apply. Check the fund's own leverage and fee drag before treating a discount as a bargain.
- ETF commissions are now often zero at many brokers, which removes the 2003 objection to ETFs for regular investing.
- The adviser fee guide (≤ 1% of assets a year) and the 8-10% return ceiling are 2003 views. Relate return claims to current bond yields and valuations (market-level.md).
- Regulatory names and databases differ by country; the procedure (verify registration, disciplinary history and the full disclosure document) is universal.
- The classic and modern views and Buffett converge on one point: for most individuals the best fund is a broad, very low-cost index fund held for decades with automatic contributions.
