## 1. Ground rules for the enterprising investor

- Start from the defensive base (high-grade bonds plus high-grade stocks at reasonable prices) and depart from it only with a reasoned justification for each departure; enterprising means more work, not more risk.
- Require every better-than-average policy to pass both tests: (1) it is sound by objective, rational standards; (2) it differs from what most investors and speculators do. A sound method that everyone uses cannot give superior results.
- Choose defensive or enterprising for each sleeve; no half-measures (policy detail in portfolio-policy.md).
- Hunting ground: securities that are (a) certainly not overvalued by conservative measures and (b) decidedly more attractive than the average stock because of prospects, record, or both.
- Apply the defensive tests of quality and price (defensive-selection.md) but less rigidly: let a considerable plus in one factor offset a small black mark in another. Example: do not automatically drop a company with one deficit year (such as 1970) if large average earnings and other attributes make it cheap.
- You may favor industries you are optimistic about, but never pay a high price relative to earnings and assets because of that enthusiasm.
- Contrarian cyclical rule: buy important cyclical enterprises (steel was the classic example) when the current situation is unfavorable, near-term prospects poor, and the low price fully reflects the pessimism. The 2003 update applied this to then-depressed technology, telecom and electric utilities: yesterday's losers are often tomorrow's winners.
- Growth stocks: avoid the usual growth commitment where excellent prospects are fully recognized in a current P/E above about 20. Two catches: the good record already carries a high price, and judgments of the future may be wrong because rapid growth cannot last and size slows it. Diversified growth investing paid no extra reward even to professionals: 45 growth funds with ten-year records gained 108% in 1961-70 against 105% for the S&P composite, and most growth funds lagged both the S&P and the DJIA in 1969-70. Don't expect an individual to do better. Full treatment of growth valuation: security-analysis.md; margin-of-safety test for growth: margin-of-safety.md.

## 2. The don'ts (bargain-only exceptions)

The most useful generalizations for the enterprising investor are negative. At "full prices" (near par for bonds and preferreds; about fair business value for common stocks) exclude: foreign bonds, ordinary preferred stocks, and secondary common stocks including new offerings. The defensive investor avoids them at any price; the enterprising investor buys them only at bargain prices, meaning no more than two-thirds of appraised value.

### 2.1 Second-grade bonds and preferreds
- [ ] Leave high-grade preferreds to corporate buyers; they get a tax advantage you lack.
- [ ] Buy inferior bonds and preferreds only at bargain levels: ordinarily at least 30% under par for high-coupon issues, and deeper discounts for low coupons. Why: if you accept a real chance of principal loss you must be paid with a chance of substantial principal gain, not 1-2 points of extra yield. Modern update (2003): "high coupon" today means roughly bonds yielding 8%+ or preferreds 10%+; having to pay such rates is itself a risk signal.
- [ ] Reject the "businessman's investment" (accepting principal risk for 1-2% more income). A second-grade 5.5-6% bond at par is almost always a bad buy; the same bond at 70 may make sense, and patience usually brings 70 in the next weak market.
- [ ] Judge grade by coverage, not yield: the key difference between first- and second-grade bonds is how many times interest is covered by earnings (coverage standards: security-analysis.md).
- Evidence:
  - Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pacific 5% income debentures at 68 yielded 7.35% in 1964, but the road covered total interest only 1.5x pretax in 1963 vs the 5x requirement for a well-protected railroad bond. In 1970 the road ran a large deficit, suspended interest, and the bonds fell to 10.
  - Ten railroad income bonds that sold at 96+ in 1946 (average high 102.5) fell to an average low of 68 the next year (about -1/3) despite better 1947 rail earnings, a bigger fall than DJIA stocks (about -23%), for a yield edge of only 1.75 points (4.25% vs 2.50%).
  - Second-grade seniors almost all suffer sinking spells in bad markets, yet many later work out: Cities Service $6 first preferred, paying nothing, sold at 15 in 1937 and 27 in 1943 with $60 of arrears, then was retired in 1947 for $196.50 of 3% debentures per share and traded up to 186. Lesson: the profit went to those who bought when the issues were friendless, not at offering prices.
  - WorldCom, May 2001: $11.9 billion bond offering at yields up to 8.3%; the prospectus showed pretax income had fallen short of fixed charges by $4.1 billion in 2 of the prior 5 years, and the new bonds added about $900 million a year of interest. Bankrupt July 2002; bonds lost over 80%. Compute fixed-charge coverage from the prospectus; no yield compensates for inadequate coverage.
- Modern update (2003): diversified junk-bond funds remove the single-issuer objection (1978-2003 defaults averaged 4.4% a year, yet junk returned 10.5%/yr vs 8.6% for 10-year Treasuries), but fees are often high and principal erodes; a minor option at most (portfolio-policy.md).

### 2.2 Foreign government bonds
- [ ] Let others buy them even at attractive yields: no legal means to enforce the claim and a long default record since 1914. Cuba 4.5s 117 (1953) to 20 after default (1963); Belgian Congo 5.25s at 36, Greek 7s at 30, Poland as low as 7 (1963); Czechoslovakia 8s went 96.5 (1922), 112 (1928), 67.75 (1932), 106 (1936), 6 (1939), 117 (1946), 35 (1948), 8 (1970).
- Modern update: at most 10% of the bond allocation in a low-cost emerging-market bond fund (expenses <= about 1.25%) for diversification (portfolio-policy.md).

### 2.3 New issues and IPOs
- [ ] Be wary of all new issues, including tempting convertibles and common stocks whose excellent earnings are confined to the recent past; apply unusually severe tests. Why: (1) special salesmanship needs special resistance; (2) "favorable market conditions" for issuing mean favorable to the seller. The effect worsens as quality falls from top bonds to second-grade seniors to common stock flotations.
- [ ] Refuse new common stocks offered in bull markets as an elementary discipline; even if one passes, getting involved is bad policy; for every dollar made this way expect to lose at least two.
- [ ] Watch the cycle: early in a bull market new issues are priced reasonably; then they come more often, quality falls and prices verge on exorbitant. A dependable sign the bull swing is ending: shares of small, nondescript companies offered above the prices of many medium-sized companies with long records. Afterward new issues often lose 75%+ and, at the bottom, sell as far below value as they once sold above; some become bargains years later.
- [ ] Revisit friendless former new issues only years later, at a fraction of worth, using the bargain tests below.
- Modern update (2003 data):
  - Underwriting discount on an IPO is about 7% vs under 4% commission on existing shares, so new stock gets the harder sell; firms issue near market peaks.
  - Buying the average IPO at the first-day close and holding 3 years (1980-2001) trailed the market by more than 23 points a year (Ritter-Welch).
  - The "next Microsoft" lure is the vividness bias: rolling $1,000 through every IPO since 1960 at offer price would theoretically compound to an absurd sum, but you cannot get offer-price allocations or know the rare winners.
  - VA Linux, Dec 9, 1999: offered at $30, opened $299, closed $239.25 (+697.5%), valued at $12.7 billion on cumulative sales of $44 million and cumulative losses of $25 million; $1.19 three years later. Test: would you pay that sum to buy the whole business privately?
  - IPO supply cycle: 850+ IPOs June 1960-May 1962; 781 in 1969; 9 in 1974; about 4,000 in the 1980s; about 5,000 in the 1990s; 88 in 2001. The public gets burned, stays away at least two years, returns.
- Cases: Aetna Maintenance and AAA Enterprises (case-studies.md, superinvestors-and-speculation.md).

### 2.4 Trading itself
- Day trading is financial suicide. A hot small stock costs about 2-4% each way to trade; with short-term gains taxed as ordinary income (38.6% top federal rate in 2003 vs 20% long-term), a trader needs about a 10% gain just to break even.
- Barber and Odean (66,000+ households, 1991-1996): the most active traders (turnover above 20% a month) trailed the market by 6.4 points a year after costs; the most patient slightly beat it.

## 3. Field A: the relatively unpopular large company

- Principle: the market overvalues growth and glamour and therefore relatively undervalues companies out of favor because of unsatisfactory but temporary developments.
- [ ] Concentrate on LARGE companies passing through unpopularity. Why: they have the capital and brain power to come back, and the market responds reasonably fast to their improvement. Small out-of-favor companies risk permanent loss of profitability and protracted neglect even after earnings recover.
- [ ] Check that the low multiplier is low relative to past AVERAGE earnings, not just current earnings. Why: cyclicals sell at low P/Es at earnings peaks and high P/Es at troughs (see Chrysler below).
- [ ] Add other quantitative and qualitative requirements; a low multiplier alone is a starting point (lesson of the failed 1968-71 test).

Evidence, Drexel & Co. study: each year buy the 6 or 10 DJIA stocks at the lowest multipliers of current or prior-year earnings; hold 1-5 years. Unprofitable from 1917 to 1933; highly successful after 1933. In 34 one-year tests 1937-1969 the cheap group was clearly worse than the DJIA 3 times, about the same 6 times, clearly better 25 times.

| Period | 10 low-multiplier | 10 high-multiplier | 30 DJIA |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1937-42 | -2.2% | -10.0% | -6.3% |
| 1943-47 | 17.3% | 8.3% | 14.9% |
| 1948-52 | 16.4% | 4.6% | 9.9% |
| 1953-57 | 20.9% | 10.0% | 13.7% |
| 1958-62 | 10.2% | -3.3% | 3.6% |
| 1963-69 | 8.0% | 4.6% | 4.0% |

(Average annual % gain.) $10,000 invested in 1936 and switched yearly grew to $66,900 by 1962 in the low-multiplier group vs $25,300 high-multiplier and $44,000 for all 30. From end-1968 to mid-1971 the low-multiplier group lost sharply while the high-multiplier group gained: one bad instance does not cancel 30+ good ones, but it carries weight. Modern update: this is today's "Dogs of the Dow" idea.

Later academic evidence points the same way: long-run studies such as Davis, Fama and French's work on U.S. stocks over 1929-1997 find that value (low-multiple) stocks have outperformed growth stocks over long periods. Use it to support the low-multiplier preference, not to promise any particular year's result.

Cyclical warning, Chrysler: 1952 EPS $9.04, high 98, P/E 10.8; 1954 $2.13, low 56, P/E 26.2; 1955 $11.49, high 101.5, P/E 8.8; 1956 $2.29, low 52, P/E 22.9; 1957 $13.75, high 82, P/E 6.7; 1958 deficit $3.88, low 44; 1968 $24.92, high 294 (split-adjusted), P/E 11.8; 1970 deficit, low 65. A company earning next to nothing must sell at a high multiplier. Such roller coasters are doubtful choices for the enterprising investor (profits seen in hindsight are not profits made with real money).

Modern update: get interested in big growth companies when something goes wrong, e.g., Johnson & Johnson fell 16% in a day in July 2002 on an FDA record-keeping probe (P/E 24 to 20); after the 1982 Tylenol poisoning it fell 17.5% in a week and became one of the great investments of the 1980s. "Never buy into a lawsuit" creates bargains (Philip Morris fell 23% in 2 days on a potential $200 billion verdict, then doubled within a year) but liability suits also destroyed Johns Manville, W. R. Grace and USG; for all but the most intrepid the maxim stands.

## 4. Field B: bargain issues

**Definition:** a security that analysis shows is worth considerably more than its price. Threshold: indicated value at least 50% above price, i.e., price <= two-thirds of value. Includes bonds and preferreds well under par, and common stocks.

Two valuation tests:
1. **Appraisal method:** estimate future earnings x an appropriate capitalization factor (factor tables: security-analysis.md). If the result is well above the price and you trust the technique, it is a bargain.
2. **Value to a private owner:** also usually earnings-based, but with more weight on realizable assets, especially net current assets (working capital).

Three causes of undervaluation: (1) currently disappointing results; (2) protracted neglect or unpopularity; (3) the market's failure to see the true earnings picture. The market makes mountains out of molehills; mere lack of interest can produce absurd prices.

Requirements before acting, because causes (1) and (2) alone are unreliable:
- [ ] Reasonable stability of earnings over the past decade or more (no deficit year).
- [ ] Sufficient size and financial strength to survive setbacks.
- [ ] Ideal: a large, prominent company selling well below both its past average price and its past average P/E.
- [ ] Avoid if prospects are definitely bad, however low the price; the bargain field is the majority of companies whose future is neither distinctly promising nor unpromising.

Examples:
- Anaconda Wire & Cable: high 85 in 1956 on big earnings; earnings fell six years; 23.5 in 1962; taken over by its parent in 1963 at the equivalent of only 33. Lesson: disappointing results plus neglect did not make it a bargain without stable earnings.
- General Motors under 30 in 1941 (about 5 in 1971 shares) while earning over $4 and paying $3.50+: general market lows produce bargains; level-headed appraisal of average future conditions justifies courage.
- National Presto: 1968 high 45 (8x $5.61 EPS); EPS rose in 1969-70 but the price fell to 21 (< 4x record earnings, below NCAV); March 1972 at 34 = 5.5x earnings, about equal to the enlarged NCAV.
- Standard Oil of California, early 1972: about 56, the same as 13 years earlier; steady earnings (one small dip 1958-71), book about equal to price, never averaged above 15x, then about 10x.
- Northern Pacific Railway 1946-47: fell from 36 to 13.5 while true 1947 earnings were about $10/share, hidden by a $1 dividend and railroad accounting (cause 3).
- Modern molehills: Pfizer -3.4% in a day (May 1998, Viagra deaths) then up about a third in 2 years; Warner-Lambert -19% in a day (late 1997) then nearly doubled in 6 months.

Bargain bonds: 1970 well-secured low-coupon bonds at deep discounts (AT&T 2 5/8s of 1986 at 51; Deere 4.5s of 1983 at 62; Santa Fe 4s of 1995 at 51; McGraw-Hill 3 7/8s of 1992 at 50.5) gain if rates fall. Defaulted railroad first mortgages of the 1930s-40s offered spectacular opportunities to careful, courageous analysts; modern update: today's distressed debt needs $100,000+ dedicated to diversify.

## 5. Net-current-asset stocks ("net-nets")

**Exact definition:** price below net current assets after deducting ALL prior claims, counting fixed and other assets at zero. Per share: NCAV = (current assets - total liabilities - preferred stock at its claim) / common shares. Note: technically "working capital" is current assets minus current liabilities, but here it means current assets minus all liabilities, including long-term debt and preferred.

Rules:
- [ ] Buy only as a diversified group, never one at a time. A classic net-net partnership (1936-56) bought at two-thirds or less of NCAV and usually held at least 100 issues. Modern update: buy only if you can find a couple dozen at once and hold patiently; single net-nets can still lose money.
- [ ] Optionally drop those with net losses in the last 12 months if enough remain to diversify.
- [ ] Be patient; gains may take years (Burton-Dixie below).
- [ ] Remember NCAV opportunities are cyclical: abundant 1923-1957 (except the real trial of 1930-32), scarce through most of the 1960s, back in the 1970 decline (50+ in a quick early-1971 Stock Guide scan).

Evidence:
- **The 1957 test:** one share each of 85 net-nets bought Dec 31, 1957 (a not-low market), held 2 years.

| Exchange | Companies | Aggregate NCAV | Price Dec 1957 | Price Dec 1959 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NYSE | 35 | $748 | $419 | $838 |
| AMEX | 25 | $495 | $289 | $492 |
| Midwest | 5 | $163 | $87 | $141 |
| OTC | 20 | $425 | $288 | $433 |
| Total | 85 | $1,831 | $1,083 | $1,904 |

Result: +75% vs +50% for the S&P 425 industrials; each group rose to about its NCAV; none showed significant loss, 7 about even, 78 gained appreciably.
- **Prominent companies at or below NCAV in 1970** (1970 low / NCAV per share / book per share / 1970 EPS / dividend / pre-1970 high):

| Company | 1970 price | NCAV | Book | EPS 1970 | Div | Earlier high |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cone Mills | 13 | $18 | $39.3 | $1.51 | $1.00 | 41.5 |
| Jantzen | 11 1/8 | 12 | 16.3 | 1.27 | .60 | 37 |
| National Presto | 21.5 | 27 | 31.7 | 6.15 | 1.00 | 45 |
| Parker Pen | 9.25 | 9.5 | 16.6 | 1.62 | .60 | 31.25 |
| West Point Pepperell | 16.25 | 20.5 | 39.4 | 1.82 | 1.50 | 64 |

Lesson: when the market dislikes a company, famous brands, land, buildings and machinery count for nothing, while no-quality new issues are bid to valuations above IBM's.
- **Burton-Dixie:** March 1964 price 20 vs NCAV 30 and book about 50; August 1967 all holders offered 53.75 (about book) -> +165% in 3.5 years, 47% a year non-compounded. Most bargains paid off faster but less.
- **Comverse Technology (Oct 31, 2002):** current assets $2.4 billion - total liabilities $1.0 billion = $1.4 billion; under 190 million shares at under $8 = market cap just under $1.4 billion: the operating business priced at about zero.

How to screen today:
- [ ] Start from new 52-week-low lists.
- [ ] Pull the latest 10-Q/10-K; compute current assets - total liabilities (including long-term debt and preferred).
- [ ] Compare to market capitalization; target price <= two-thirds of NCAV.
- [ ] Assemble a couple dozen or more; hold patiently.
- [ ] Watch for current assets that may not be realizable at book (inventory, receivables) and cash burn that shrinks NCAV.

## 6. Secondary companies

**Definition:** not a leader in a fairly important industry (a smaller company in its field, or the main unit in an unimportant line). Established growth companies are not "secondary."

Why they are cheap: after 1931-32 devastated second-rank firms, investors developed a lasting preference for leaders, so secondaries usually sell at much lower prices relative to earnings and assets. Believing any price too high for them mirrors 1929's belief that no price was too high for blue chips. The typical middle-sized listed company is large relative to private businesses and can earn a fair return indefinitely.

Rules:
- [ ] Buy only below their value to a private owner, at no more than two-thirds of appraised value. They fluctuate about a central level well below fair value and reach it only in upper bull markets.
- [ ] Never buy them at full price; in hot markets the pendulum swings to overvaluation (end-1938 to 1946: DJIA leaders +40% vs S&P low-priced stocks +280%; similar excesses in 1961 and 1968).
- [ ] Remember a detached minority share of a secondary company is worth substantially less than to a controlling owner, so shareholder-management relations matter more (management-and-dividends.md).
- [ ] Boundary-area companies close to primary status may be bought at a small discount from appraisal.

Six ways profits arise even if undervaluation persists:
1. A relatively high dividend return.
2. Substantial reinvested earnings relative to price, eventually reflected in price (over 5-7 years).
3. Bull markets are most generous to low-priced issues, lifting bargains at least to reasonable levels.
4. Continuous price adjustment even in featureless markets.
5. Correction of specific causes of poor earnings (new conditions, policies or management).
6. Acquisition by a larger company at a generous price.

Examples of secondary bargains: ELTRA and Emhart at 1970 closing prices (security-analysis.md); Whiting Corp. 1969 (case-studies.md, Pair 8).

Modern update: small stocks beat large by 17.6 points a year 1975-83, then lagged by 5 points a year for a decade; 1999's small-stock surge rode .com, optical and wireless buzzwords. Buzzwords become buzz saws.

## 7. Field C: special situations / workouts

Types: acquisitions of smaller firms at prices above market; reorganization securities (defaulted bonds expected to receive new securities worth more than cost); breakups (utility holding companies after 1935: parts worth more than the whole); liquidations; merger arbitrage.

The classic partnership's selection rule for arbitrages and liquidations:
- [ ] Calculated annual return of **20% or more**, AND
- [ ] Judged probability of success of **at least 4 out of 5**.
- [ ] Prefer deals most likely to close and those with the smallest loss if they fail; diversify to spread risk.
- [ ] Price in failure: rising shares of announced mergers fail (antitrust, shareholder opposition, market conditions, adverse findings, disagreement on terms).

Worked examples, early 1971:
- **Kayser-Roth / Borden:** offer of 1 1/3 Borden shares per Kayser-Roth share; next day Borden 26, Kayser-Roth 28. Buy 300 K-R, short 400 Borden: value received = 400 x 26 = $10,400 vs cost 300 x 28 = $8,400 -> about 24% gross; if completed in 6 months, about 40% a year. Outcome: K-R directors rejected the bid; immediate unwind lost about 12% including commissions. Late 1971 K-R fell below 20 while Borden at 25 implied 33 for K-R under the old terms.
- **Aurora Plastics / National Biscuit:** $11 cash offer vs price 8.5-9 -> about 25% gross, subject to nonconsummation and time. Outcome: price cut to 10.5 after a bad 1970; paid end of May; realized about 25% annualized.
- **Universal-Marion (liquidation):** book about $28.50, largely liquid; price 21.5 -> 30%+ potential. Outcome: initial distribution of about $7 (net cost about 14.5), but the stock later fell to 13, putting the result in doubt.
- Context: about 5,000 merger announcements in 1970 (6,000+ in 1969). Verdict: if these three are representative, workouts entered at random are unattractive; it has become a field for professionals.
- Modern update: merger arbitrage is wholly inappropriate for most individuals.

Related hedge (the classic partnership's method): buy a convertible senior security and short the common it converts into near parity; small maximum loss, profit if the common falls much more than the senior issue; these earned good profits in bear markets (mechanics in convertibles-warrants.md).

## 8. The Stock Guide screen (the classic test)

The procedure, run on the S&P Stock Guide (about 4,500 companies), end-1970 prices:
1. Prima facie cheapness: P/E of 9 or less on latest 12-month earnings.
2. Then require ALL of:
   - [ ] Financial condition: current assets >= 1.5x current liabilities, AND debt <= 110% of net current assets (industrials).
   - [ ] Earnings stability: no deficit in the last 5 years.
   - [ ] Dividend record: some current dividend.
   - [ ] Earnings growth: last year's earnings above those of 5 years earlier (1966 in the end-1970 test).
   - [ ] Price: below 120% of net tangible assets.
3. Optional seventh criterion: S&P earnings-and-dividend ranking of B+ (average) or better.
4. No minimum size: small companies can be safe enough if bought carefully and as a group.
5. Final judgment: from the survivors, pick perhaps 1 in 5 on your own judgment.

Funnel: about 1 in 10 of the first 200 issues had P/E <= 9 (about 450 in the whole Guide). The five extra tests cut the first 20 to 5; the first 450 issues yielded 15 qualifiers; extrapolated, about 150 in the Guide, about 100 if limited to B+ or better. Earnings data ran to Sept 30, 1970, so a bad fourth quarter could be missing.

## 9. Single-criterion evidence and the "goodwill giants" anomaly

- Two single criteria worked consistently in the long past: (a) low-multiplier stocks of important companies (DJIA-type), and (b) a diversified group below NCAV. The first failed in the 1968-71 test; the second has no bad mark, only scarcity.
- Classic tests: 30-stock portfolios bought at 1968 closing prices, revalued mid-1971, each chosen by one criterion: low P/E; high dividend yield; long dividend record; very large shares outstanding; strong financial position; low price per share; low price vs previous high; high S&P quality rank.
- Random benchmark: 90 random Stock Guide issues fell 22% on average (excluding 19 dropped from the Guide, likely worse) while the S&P composite was flat and the DJIA fell about 5%. Small inferior issues are overvalued in bull markets, fall harder and recover late or never. Rule: avoid second-quality issues unless they are demonstrable bargains.
- Only three groups beat the S&P composite: A+ ranked industrials (+9.5% vs S&P industrials -2.4% and DJIA -5.6%; but A+ utilities -18% vs -14% for the utility index; every higher rank beat every lower rank); companies with 50+ million shares (flat vs a small index decline); stocks priced above $100 (+1%).
- Each single favorable factor beat its opposite (low P/E beat high P/E; long dividend payers beat non-payers), supporting a COMBINATION of quantitative criteria.
- Anomaly: 30 "goodwill giants" (each with goodwill, i.e., market price minus book, over $1 billion and over half the price; aggregate over $120 billion) rose 15%, best of 20-odd lists. The likely cause was momentum attached to size, record, expected growth and long strong market action, which may carry such stocks along almost without limit, though IBM itself slipped from 315 to 304. They can suit a diversified portfolio, but prefer stocks with asset values of at least two-thirds of price.
- Cap-weighting matters: the S&P beat the equal-weighted lists because giant companies were more stable.

## 10. A classic value partnership's operating categories (1926-1956)

| Category | What | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Arbitrages | Buy a security, sell what it will be exchanged into under a reorganization/merger plan | >= 20%/yr, >= 4/5 odds |
| Liquidations | Buy shares due to receive cash liquidating payments | >= 20%/yr, >= 4/5 odds |
| Related hedges | Long convertible, short its common, near parity | small max loss; profits in bear markets |
| Net-current-asset bargains | Buy below NCAV, typically <= 2/3 of it | 100+ issues |
| Control acquisitions | Occasional | not for readers |

Dropped after 10+ years of tracking results by category, as not dependable or "headache proof": (1) apparently attractive stocks not obtainable below working-capital value; (2) unrelated hedges (long one company, short another). From 1939 they confined themselves to the categories above. Lesson: track results by method and abandon methods that do not deliver. Few enterprising investors have the temperament to restrict themselves so narrowly.

## 11. Modern craftsman methods (2003)

First, the gate:
- [ ] For most investors picking stocks is unnecessary or inadvisable; use an index fund.
- [ ] Practice first (classic 1976 advice): run a paper portfolio for one year, recording every pick.
- [ ] After a year compare with an S&P 500 index fund. If you did not enjoy it or did poorly, stop and index.
- [ ] If you enjoyed it and did well, build a basket gradually, capped at **10% of the total portfolio**, the rest indexed; stop if interest or results fade.

Idea sources:
- [ ] Scan the daily list of new 52-week lows; many top value managers become interested when the price falls, not rises.
- [ ] Use stock screeners with the defensive filters (defensive-selection.md) or the enterprising screen above.
- [ ] See which respected value managers own your stocks; read their reports and other holdings.

ROIC (Christopher Davis definition): **ROIC = Owner earnings / Invested capital.**
- Owner earnings = operating profit + depreciation + amortization of goodwill - federal income tax (at the company's average rate) - cost of stock options - maintenance (essential) capital expenditures - any income from unsustainable pension-return assumptions (2003: anything above 6.5%).
- Invested capital = total assets - cash and short-term investments - non-interest-bearing current liabilities + past accounting charges that reduced invested capital.
- Thresholds: >= 10% attractive; 6-7% can tempt with strong brands, focused management, or a temporary cloud. Prefer rising ROIC.

Private-market (sum-of-the-parts) value:
- [ ] Read the business-segment footnote (and MD&A) for each segment's revenue and earnings.
- [ ] Find recent acquisitions of comparable businesses; from their filings compute purchase price / earnings.
- [ ] Apply that multiple to each segment, sum, subtract net debt, compare with market value.
- [ ] Buy "60-cent dollars": price <= 60% of appraised value (O. Mason Hawkins), the classic margin of safety.
- [ ] **Stub arithmetic** when a company owns a listed stake in another: value the stake per parent share (shares of the subsidiary per parent share × subsidiary price). If the parent sells below that, after net debt and other large liabilities, the market is pricing the rest of the business below zero. That points either to a bargain in the parent or to an overpriced subsidiary; check which. Case: in March 2000 each 3Com share carried 1.525 Palm shares worth $144.97 at Palm's $95.06 close, yet 3Com traded at $81.81, implying its other businesses were worth minus $63.16 a share (about minus $22 billion). By end-2002, $10,000 in Palm was worth $83; in 3Com, $2,576.

Management tests:
- [ ] Do managers think like owners? Are statements understandable or obfuscated?
- [ ] Do "nonrecurring" charges recur?
- [ ] Candor about problems, clear plans for current and future cash flow, sizable ownership bought with cash rather than via options.
- [ ] Pass if management talks more about the stock price than the business.
- [ ] Option issuance limited to about 3% of shares outstanding.
- [ ] Compare what the company said one year with what happened the next (honest with holders and with themselves?).
- [ ] Division-head turnover: heavy in a new CEO's first 1-2 years is healthy; persistent turnover signals turmoil.
- Deeper governance and option checks: management-and-dividends.md.

Other roads: very low multiples of assets, earnings or cash flow; undervalued small companies; Buffett's franchise approach (strong brands, understandable business, robust finances, near-monopoly, bought when bad news passes over like a storm, managers who meet realistic goals, grow from within, allocate capital well, and avoid huge option grants). Buffett's proverb, paraphrased: when a management famed for brilliance takes on a business famed for bad economics, it is the business's reputation that survives. So judge the business's economics first, and never buy a structurally poor business for its management. Successful professionals share two traits: unwavering discipline in their approach, and little attention to what the market is doing.

## 12. Why beating the market is hard (classic rationale)

- Funds with top analysts and expenses of about 0.5% a year still failed over long periods to match the S&P 500 (against roughly 15% annual stock returns 1951-60 and 6% in 1961-70). Friend-Blume-Crockett (1960-68, 100+ funds vs random equal-weighted NYSE portfolios): random portfolios beat funds of the same risk class by 3.7%/yr (low risk), 2.5% (medium), 0.2% (high). Modern update: funds trail by roughly their costs; also cash drag, and a 120-stock fund trails if any of the other 380 index stocks soars.
- Two explanations: (1) prices may already reflect all known facts and reasonable expectations, so later moves stem from the unforeseeable, and armies of analysts sharing ideas level results (duplicate-bridge analogy); (2) analysts' flawed approach of buying the best-prospect companies at any price and shunning the rest at any price, which is valid only if growth were certain and endless or losers were doomed. Few companies grow uninterrupted for long; few large ones die.
- Therefore the enterprising investor's edge lies in segments the market neglects or discriminates against, using methods not generally accepted on Wall Street. Modern update: the problem is not stupid analysts but too many smart ones; the market usually prices most stocks about right, and only occasionally goes wildly wrong.

## 13. Translating to today

- **P/E <= 9 cut-off.** The cut-off was set at end-1970, when high-grade corporate bonds yielded roughly 7.5-8%; P/E 9 = an earnings yield of about 11%, roughly 1.4-1.5x the bond yield. The classic linkage of stock earnings yields to bond yields is in margin-of-safety.md. Translation (an inference, not a stated classic rule): set the cut-off so the earnings yield, preferably on multiyear average earnings, sits well above the current AAA corporate or 10-year government yield, and never above the defensive ceiling of 15x average earnings (defensive-selection.md). In low-rate eras this permits a higher P/E but not a high one; it is not a license to buy at 25x.
- **"Last year's earnings above 1966"** = earnings higher than 5 years earlier.
- **Price < 120% of net tangible assets and NCAV tests** are timeless but use tangible book: exclude goodwill and intangibles; adjust for buybacks that shrink book; check off-balance-sheet obligations, leases and pension deficits as liabilities.
- **S&P B+ ranking** measures only the stability and growth of earnings and dividends over about the past 8 years; price plays no part, and B+ means average (A = high). It maps to any objective earnings-and-dividend score built the same way. To stand in for it, score the last 8 years of earnings and dividends for stability and growth only, leave price out, and require about average or better; otherwise count deficit years and dividend continuity yourself.
- **Low-price warning.** The classic $10/share caution became about $1 (the delisting level) in the 2003 view: trading costs are high, monitoring is laborious, and such companies often fail; a diversified basket of dozens may still suit an enterprising investor.
- **Bargain thresholds** (price <= 2/3 of value; bonds >= 30% below par; the classic partnership's 20%/yr and 4-in-5) are ratios and need no inflation adjustment. The modern ROIC pension cut-off (6.5%) and tax rates are 2003 levels; use current figures.
- **Net-nets** are rare in large caps in most markets; expect to find them mainly in micro-caps and in markets after crashes. Treat their scarcity as information about the market level (market-level.md).
- Special situations, related hedges and distressed debt now face professional competition (the classic verdict in 1971); individuals should treat them as exceptional.
