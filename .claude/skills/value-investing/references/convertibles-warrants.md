## 1. Decision rules for convertibles

- [ ] Treat every new convertible with mistrust; look more than twice before buying. Why: the conversion privilege is paid for by giving up quality, yield, or both; the issuer saves interest only by handing part of the common's future gains to the convertible holder. No clever device makes a deal much better for both sides.
- [ ] Judge the issue on its own facts: the convertible form guarantees neither attractiveness nor unattractiveness.
- [ ] Be especially wary of convertibles issued late in a bull market (that is when most are issued): a broad market decline makes the conversion feature far less valuable and often calls the issue's safety into question. Modern update: they "come out of the woodwork" near bull-market ends because even weak companies then have stock prices that make conversion look attractive.
- [ ] Do not buy a convertible as a substitute for a common you would not buy on its own at its price. The convertible is safer than that common, but if the common is not a sound purchase, the substitution rarely improves things enough.
- [ ] Ideal convertible: (a) strongly secured as a senior security, (b) convertible into a common that is itself attractive, (c) at a conversion price only slightly above the current market. Look for it among OLDER issues that have drifted into a favorable position; a genuinely strong new issue is unlikely to carry a good conversion privilege.
- [ ] Never convert a convertible (old Wall Street maxim). Why: converting gives up the combination of a prior claim to interest plus a profit chance, and usually turns an investor into a speculator at a bad time (after a big rise in the common).
- [ ] Test soundness in declining markets, not rising ones: in 1950-1968 convertibles looked good only because almost all commons rose.
- [ ] Check seniority: most convertibles are subordinated (junior to other long-term debt and bank loans), so they have no prior claim in bankruptcy.

## 2. Convertible arithmetic

Definitions:
- Conversion ratio = shares received per bond (or per preferred share).
- Conversion price = bond face value / conversion ratio.
- Conversion value (parity) = stock price x conversion ratio.
- Conversion premium = (convertible price - conversion value) / conversion value.
- Payback (break-even) period = (convertible price - conversion value) / (annual convertible income - income from the same number of common shares). If the common pays no dividend, divide by the annual interest.
- Stock price needed for conversion to make sense = convertible price / conversion ratio (compare with the time left to maturity).

Worked example 1 (classic): 6% bond bought at 100, convertible at $25 = 40 shares per $1,000 bond.
- Stock rises to 30 -> conversion value = 40 x $30 = $1,200 -> bond worth at least 120; it trades at 125.
- At 125 the holder is effectively a common shareholder: if the stock falls, the bond falls with it.
- Selling at 125 = 25% profit.

Worked example 2 (modern, DoubleClick 4.75% convertible subordinated notes of 1999, due 2006, year-end 2002):
- Interest $47.50 a year; conversion ratio 24.24.
- Stock $5.66 -> conversion value = 5.66 x 24.24 = $137.20.
- Bond price $881.30 -> premium = (881.30 - 137.20) / 137.20 = about 542%.
- Excess paid over conversion value about $750 -> payback = 750 / 47.50 = nearly 16 years.
- Stock price needed for conversion to be practical: above $36 (881.30 / 24.24), from $5.66, before 2006: borders on the miraculous.
- Conclusion: you are buying a mediocre-yield subordinated bond with a nearly worthless option; the cash yield is inadequate for the low conversion probability.

Worked example 3 (classic; Ford Motor Finance, Nov 1971): a 20-year straight bond yielded 7.5%; a 25-year bond subordinated to it yielded 4.5% but was convertible into Ford stock (then 68.5). Price of the privilege = giving up 40% of income (3.0 / 7.5) plus accepting a junior claim.

## 3. The "what to do when it rises" problem

- Even a profit creates a dilemma: sell on a small rise, hold for a big one, or, when the issue is called after a big rise in the common, sell or convert and keep the stock?
- Typical sequence: holder sells at 125 (25% gain); the common keeps rising and he feels he sold too soon; next time he waits for 150-200; the issue reaches 140, the market breaks, and it slides to 80. Bad guesses are almost inevitable.
- Arithmetic drawback: a stern, uniform policy of selling at 25-30% profit probably works best across many holdings, but it caps the gain on winners while many issues (weakly secured, bought late in bull markets) never reach 125 yet collapse in the downturn. Net: about as many substantial (at least temporary) losses as gains of similar size; the "spectacular opportunities" prove illusory.
- Eversharp case (a classic value partnership participated): private placement of 4.5% convertible debentures at par, convertible at $40. The stock rose to 65.5, then (after a 3-for-2 split) the equivalent of 88, making the debentures worth at least 220. The issues were called at a small premium; nearly all were converted and many fund buyers kept the stock. By March 1948 the stock was 7 3/8, equal to 27 for the debentures: a 75% loss instead of a 100%+ gain. Lesson: experienced managers broke the never-convert rule out of enthusiasm and "favorable market action"; prudent principles are forgotten exactly when needed.

## 4. Switching from common into a convertible preferred

Before about 1956 commons usually yielded more than their companies' preferreds; by 1970 the reverse was common, so many convertible preferreds were clearly better than the related common. Rule:
- [ ] If the preferred converts into a package of common worth about the same as (or slightly more than) the preferred's price, AND the preferred's dividend exceeds the dividends on that common package, the common holder has nothing to lose and important advantages to gain by switching: more income and a senior position, while the conversion right ensures the preferred never sells below the package.

Worked example, Studebaker-Worthington (end-1970):
- Common 57; $5 convertible preferred 87.5; each preferred converts into 1.5 common = 1.5 x 57 = 85.5.
- Price disadvantage of the switch: 87.5 - 85.5 = 2 points.
- Income: preferred $5.00 vs 1.5 x $1.20 = $1.80 on the common package -> extra $3.20 a year, so the 2-point premium is recovered in under a year, after which the preferred out-yields the common.
- At the 1968 and 1970 lows the preferred sold 15 points above the 1.5-share package. Late 1971: common as low as 38 while the preferred was about 77, a spread of 20 points vs 2 a year earlier. The market neglects arithmetic.

## 5. Effect of convertibles on the common: dilution checks

Mergers paid with convertibles (and warrants) usually raise pro forma EPS and the stock (also on perceived managerial energy), but the arithmetic dilution of current and future earnings is largely ignored in optimistic markets.
- [ ] Compute fully diluted EPS: assume conversion of all convertible bonds and preferreds (add back after-tax interest and preferred dividends; add the new shares) using recent or assumed earnings.
- [ ] Compare potential new shares with shares outstanding; large ratios are a red flag (conglomerates were the chief practitioners of "convertible legerdemain").
- [ ] Add warrants by market value (section 6), not by the treasury-stock method alone.

## 6. Warrants: the verdict and the adjustment method

Verdict (classic rule): large issues of stock-option warrants are a near fraud, an existing menace and a potential disaster; they create huge "values" out of thin air and should be prohibited or strictly limited to a minor part of capitalization.

Why warrants destroy value:
- The common normally owns the pre-emptive right to buy new shares (alongside dividends, growth participation and votes). A separate warrant carves part of that value off onto another certificate, just as one could sell dividend or voting rights separately.
- Stock + warrant packages sell for more than the stock alone only because buyers are inexpert; reported EPS typically ignores warrants, overstating earnings relative to the total market value of the capitalization.
- The company gains nothing: it cannot force exercise before expiry; when it needs equity it must still offer stock below market, and outstanding warrants complicate this (often forcing their subscription price down).

Rules:
- [ ] **True market price of the common = stock price + (aggregate market value of all warrants / common shares).** Compute P/E on this price and on undiluted EPS.
- [ ] Reject the company's "full dilution" method (assume warrants exercised, proceeds used to retire debt or buy back stock): it ignores the warrant's premium over exercisable value and can perversely raise EPS.
- [ ] A small warrant issue attached to a moderate bond issue is not worth worrying about; a warrant issue large relative to outstanding stock signals top-heavy senior capitalization (the company should be selling common).
- [ ] Note: warrants sell relatively higher than the equivalent conversion feature of a convertible, so there is some logic to bonds-with-warrants over convertibles for equal dilution.
- [ ] Treat any "units" of stock plus warrants offered by a broker as a red flag. Modern update (2003): about 95% certain warrants are involved and about 90% certain the broker is "either a thief or an idiot."

Worked example, National General Corp. (June 1971 data):
- Market value of 3 warrant issues (June 30, 1971): $94,000,000 -> $18.80 per common share.
- Common price 24.50 -> true price = 24.50 + 18.80 = 43.30.

| 1970 earnings basis | EPS | Price used | P/E |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Before special items, no dilution | $2.33 | 24.50 | 10.5 |
| A. Company's diluted calculation | $1.60 | 24.50 | 15.3 |
| A. Market-value method | $2.33 | 43.30 | 18.5 |
| B. After special items, no dilution | $.90 | 24.50 | 27.2 |
| B. Company's diluted calculation | $1.33 | 24.50 | 18.4 |
| B. Market-value method | $.90 | 43.30 | 48.1 |

After special charges the company's method RAISED EPS (from .90 to 1.33) and cut the P/E, which is absurd; the market-value method raises the P/E substantially, as dilution should. National General at end-1968 under the same method: 192 million common + 221 million warrants = $413 million of common-stock equivalents, true price 98 vs quoted 44.25, P/E 69 (case-studies.md, Pair 7).

Warrant history:
- American & Foreign Power warrants: over $1 billion of market value in 1929 while appearing only in a balance-sheet footnote; $8 million by 1932; wiped out in the 1952 recapitalization although the company stayed solvent.
- Tri-Continental perpetual warrants (from 1929): 1/32 of a dollar in the Depression to 75.75 in 1969 (about +242,000%), at times above the share price because of splits.
- LTV warrants, first half 1971: 2.5 to 12.5 and back to 4.
- Mechanics: newer warrants run 5-10 years; older ones were often perpetual. Once issued they trade like any security; shrewd warrant operations exist but are too technical for most investors. A classic analogy: Goethe's paper money in Faust and the French revolutionary assignats (interest cut in 1790, then stopped; declared worthless in 1797).
- A provocative classic exception: a diversified group of warrants at historically low prices could be a defensible enterprising operation if the mathematical odds of a future bull market favor gain over loss (see margin-of-safety.md).

## 7. Evidence

The classic 1946-47 sample: preferred issues offered in 1946, the last year of the bull market before 1949, price change from issue to low through July 1947.

| Decline from issue price | Straight (37 issues) | Convertible & participating (42 issues) |
|---|---|---|
| No decline | 7 | 0 |
| 0-10% | 16 | 2 |
| 10-20% | 11 | 6 |
| 20-40% | 3 | 22 |
| 40% or more | 0 | 12 |
| Average decline | about 9% | about 30% |

The 1946-47 pattern (straights about -9%, convertibles about -30%) fits conditions then: the stock market fell and dragged stock-linked issues down. Lead with the 1968-70 sample and cite the 1946-47 one as support.

The classic 1968-70 sample: random samples of 20 issues each, Dec 1968 to Dec 1970 (S&P 500 -11.3%):

| | Straight pfd A or better | Straight pfd below A | Convertible pfd | Listed common | Listed warrants |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Advances | 2 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 1 |
| Declines 0-10% | 3 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 0 |
| 10-20% | 14 | 10 | 2 | 1 | 0 |
| 20-40% | 1 | 5 | 5 | 6 | 1 |
| 40%+ | 0 | 0 | 9 | 7 | 18 |
| Average decline | 10% | 17% | 29% | 33% | 65% |

Reading: convertibles as a group were poor-quality senior issues tied to commons that do worse than the market except in speculative upsurges; only 1 of 20 convertible preferreds rose and 14 fell badly. Convertible preferreds also fell more than commons as a whole from Dec 1967 to Dec 1970 (commons -5%). Some strong companies did use convertibles in 1968-69 to avoid very high first-grade bond rates.

Other cases:
- AT&T sold at least nine convertible bond issues 1913-1957, mostly through rights; they reached institutions barred from stocks; bond interest was generally under half the stock's dividend yield; the $9 dividend held for 40 years (1919-1959) so nearly all converted. Holders did well, but not as well as buying the stock outright. Proves AT&T's soundness, not the merit of convertibles: you would need cases where the convertible worked although the common disappointed, and those are hard to find.
- NVF/Sharon Steel 1969: nearly $100 million of 5% convertible bonds plus warrants used to buy a company seven times NVF's size (case-studies.md; tax trick in superinvestors-and-speculation.md).
- Market size then: more than half of preferreds in the Stock Guide were convertible; at least 60 warrant series on the AMEX; in 1970 the NYSE first listed long-term warrants (31,400,000 AT&T shares at $52).

## 8. The 2003 view of convertibles

- Convertibles behave like stocks and work like options: about 83% correlated with the S&P 500 and about 30% with Treasury bonds. 1957-2002 (Ibbotson index) they returned 8.3% a year, about 2 points below stocks with steadier prices, but that index is hypothetical; real-world returns after fees and trading costs are about 2 points lower.
- 1998-2002: convertibles +4.8%/yr vs U.S. stocks -0.6%, medium-term corporates +7.5%, long-term corporates +8.3%. 2002: convertible index -8.6% vs S&P 500 -22.1% and NASDAQ -31.3%. Recently they beat stocks in down markets but lag other bonds, which weakens but does not erase the classic critique.
- Structural changes since 1972: convertible preferreds fell from about half to about one-eighth of the market; maturities shortened to about 7-10 years; many issues have call protection; more than half are investment grade. Issuance swelled late in the 1990s bull market ($15 billion a year mid-1990s; $39 billion in 1999; $58 billion in 2000; $105 billion in 2001), just as the classic analysis predicted. Hedge funds hold much of the market and raise volatility.
- Use: for a conservative investor holding mostly bonds, a diversified convertible holding can add stock-like returns without buying stocks directly ("stocks for chickens").
- [ ] Do not buy individual convertibles in small lots; diversification needs well over $100,000 dedicated to the sector. Use a low-cost convertible fund instead.
- [ ] Never buy a convertible fund with annual expenses above 1.0%.

## 9. Option-like products: structured notes and covered calls

- [ ] Avoid exotic structured convertibles (LYONS, ELKS, EYES, PERCS, MIPS, CHIPS, YEELDS and successors): they floor losses but cap gains and often force conversion on a fixed date; generally more trouble than they are worth. Protect against loss by diversifying across cash, bonds, domestic and foreign stocks instead.
- Covered calls, worked example: buy 100 shares of a stock at $95; sell a call with a $100 strike for a $10/share premium = $1,000 income. If the stock stays below $100 you keep stock and premium; if it jumps to $110 the shares are called at $100 and you forfeit everything above $100. Buying back the call means a loss plus high option trading costs.
- [ ] Reject covered-call writing for individuals: gains are unlimited but losses cannot exceed 100%, so capping the upside to cushion the downside mainly enriches the broker.

## 10. Full evaluation procedure (run in order)

Step 1, the senior-security test (would you buy it with no conversion feature?):
- [ ] Seniority: senior, subordinated, or preferred? Which debt ranks ahead?
- [ ] Coverage: fixed charges (or fixed charges plus preferred dividends) covered by earnings over a period of years, including a weak year, at the bond-grade minimums in security-analysis.md.
- [ ] Enterprise value vs total debt: a large cushion of stock market value above all debt (margin-of-safety.md).
- [ ] Yield given up vs a comparable straight issue of the same company (Ford example: 40% of income).

Step 2, the common-stock test (would you buy the common at today's price?):
- [ ] Apply defensive-selection.md or enterprising-selection.md criteria to the common at its current price.
- [ ] If the common fails, the convertible is acceptable only if Step 1 alone makes it a sound senior investment at its price.

Step 3, the option arithmetic (section 2):
- [ ] Conversion value, premium, payback years, stock price required for conversion and time left.
- [ ] Call terms and call protection: a call forces a decision and can cut off the premium.
- [ ] A high premium plus a low cash yield (DoubleClick) = a poor bond with a remote option: reject.

Step 4, context:
- [ ] When was it issued? Late-bull-market issues as a group do poorly (section 7).
- [ ] Is it a new issue (sold, not bought) or an older issue that has moved into a favorable position (preferred)?
- [ ] Holder's plan: set a sell discipline in advance (e.g., 25-30% gain across a diversified group), and do not convert unless forced; if called, compare selling the bond with converting and immediately judging the common on its merits.

Step 5, the issuer's side:
- [ ] Fully diluted EPS and potential share count (section 5); add warrants and options by market value (section 6).
- [ ] Use of proceeds: an acquisition paid with convertibles inflates pro forma EPS while diluting the common.

## 11. Related hedges (classic partnership mechanics)

- Buy a convertible bond or preferred and simultaneously short the common it converts into, in the number of shares it converts into, set up close to parity (convertible price about equal to conversion value).
- Maximum loss: small, the premium paid over parity plus costs, if the senior issue must actually be converted and the position closed that way.
- Profit: if the common falls considerably more than the senior issue (the bond's investment value supports it), close both in the market. Income: interest on the convertible minus dividends owed on the short.
- Record: from 1939 on the classic partnership kept related hedges as a core category; they earned good profits in bear markets, when the net-current-asset bargains did poorly. Unrelated hedges (long one company, short another) were dropped as undependable.
- Today such convertible arbitrage is dominated by hedge funds (they hold much of the convertible market); for individuals it is a professional's operation.

## 12. Translating to today

- The classic convertible rules are timeless; the 1946 and 1968-70 tables describe convertibles issued near bull-market tops. Check where the market is (market-level.md) before trusting any new-issue convertible.
- **Executive and employee stock options** are today's main warrant equivalent. Apply the classic logic: value the outstanding options (e.g., at a fair-value estimate) and add that value to the market capitalization, or deduct option cost from owner earnings (enterprising-selection.md, ROIC); check the overhang as a % of shares (about 3% is the modern ceiling for new grants; management-and-dividends.md).
- **SPAC and other listed warrants, and stock-plus-warrant units** are the modern "packages": add the warrants' market value to the common's to get the true price; treat units sold to retail investors with deep suspicion.
- **Contingent convertibles, mandatory convertibles and buffered/structured notes** fall under the structured-product rule above: identify the cap, floor, forced-conversion date and seniority before anything else.
- The 1967-71 warrant boom and the "only seven NYSE warrant issues at end-2002" are historical; the arithmetic (true price, premium, payback, dilution) applies to any option-bearing security.
