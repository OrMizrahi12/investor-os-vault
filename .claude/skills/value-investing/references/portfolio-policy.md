## 1. First decision: defensive or enterprising

- Classify the investor by the **intelligent effort they are willing and able to supply**, not by risk appetite. The return sought should depend on effort; the passive investor gets the minimum, the alert, skillful enterprising investor the maximum.
- Defensive (passive) investor: primary aim is avoiding serious mistakes or losses; secondary aim is freedom from effort, annoyance and frequent decisions.
- Enterprising (active) investor: willing to devote time and care to choosing securities that are both sound and more attractive than average; must treat investing as a business enterprise.
- Allow no muddled middle: "half a businessman" earning half a business return is a compromise that usually disappoints. Most people should be defensive. An investor may run a mostly-passive core with a small active sleeve, but each part must follow its own rules.
- Self-test (after Charles Ellis): spare time, competitive, enjoys complex intellectual challenge, thinks like a sports fan -> active approach is possible. Always rushed, wants simplicity, dislikes thinking about money -> passive. The active approach is physically and intellectually taxing; the passive approach is emotionally taxing (you must sit still).
- What decides security choice is the investor's **financial equipment** (knowledge, experience, temperament), not the size of their capital.

## 2. Stock/bond allocation rules

Core rule: hold high-grade bonds and high-grade common stocks; **never less than 25% nor more than 75% in stocks**, with bonds the inverse 75-25%.

- Default: **50-50**. It is simple, aims in the right direction, gives the feeling of responding to the market, and above all keeps the investor from being drawn deeper into stocks as the market rises to dangerous heights. In a rise the investor is content with gains on half the portfolio; in a severe fall they are consoled by being better off than bolder friends.
- Move toward 75% stocks only after a protracted bear market has created bargain prices; move toward 25% when the market is judged dangerously high (judge with market-level.md). These maxims are easy to state and hard to follow because they cut against the human nature that creates booms and busts.
- Do not exceed 50% stocks unless you have strong confidence in your stock position **and** are sure you could watch a 1969-70-type decline (about -35%) with equanimity. Do not go far below 50% unless you are genuinely uneasy about the market and willing to limit participation in any further rise (to about 25% of funds).
- Cold-blooded variant (classic rule, early 1972, when bonds yielded far more than stocks): hold only 25% in stocks until the stock-index dividend yield rises to about **two-thirds of the high-grade bond yield**, then return to 50-50. Example: DJIA 900 paying $36 (4%) with bonds at 7.5% needed bonds to fall to about 5.5%, or the DJIA to fall to about 660, or a mix. The hard part is sticking to it; it may prove too conservative.
- Always keep a significant part in both stocks and bonds. Even when bonds look clearly preferable (as in late 1971) a 100%-bond portfolio is ruled out by possible accelerating inflation, unexpected business profitability, or another speculative boom. Even when stocks look attractive, the bond floor stays because the future is unpredictable.
- Age: the classic framework never ties allocation to age. Reject "100 minus age" and "95% stocks under 30" rules; set the split from life circumstances (section 9). A wealthy 89-year-old with a pension can hold plenty of stocks; a 25-year-old saving for a house down payment should not.
- Modern update: the 25% bond minimum exists because few people hold stocks through a crash; the cushion supplies the courage to keep the rest in stocks. People are bad at predicting how they will feel in a crash.
- **When is 100% stocks acceptable?** Only if ALL are true: at least one year of family living expenses in cash; investing steadily for 20+ more years; lived through a major bear market without selling; bought more during it; has absorbed the Mr. Market lesson and implemented a formal plan to control their own behavior. Anyone who panicked in the last bear market will panic in the next.
- Change target percentages only when life circumstances change. Never raise stocks because the market went up or cut them because it went down; the heart of the approach is replacing guesswork with discipline.
- **Foreign stocks**: your job, salary and home are already a bet on your home economy. Put up to about one-third of the stock money in low-cost funds holding foreign stocks, including emerging markets, as insurance. Why: a Japanese investor who kept everything at home at end-1989, after a decade of 21.2% a year, lost about two-thirds over the next ten years.

## 3. Rebalancing mechanics

- The classic 50-50 band: when market moves push stocks to about 55%, sell **one-eleventh** of the stock holdings and move the proceeds to bonds; when stocks fall to 45%, use one-eleventh of the bond holdings to buy stocks. (1/11 of 55 = 5, restoring 50/50.) In other words, act once the mix drifts about 5 percentage points from target; smaller moves are left alone.
- The modern calendar rule: rebalance **every six months, no more and no less**, on fixed easy dates (e.g., Jan 1 and Jul 1). The only question each time: "Do I now own more of this asset than my plan calls for?" Example: target 70/30; stocks rise 25% with bonds flat -> about 75/25; sell stock funds back to 70/30.
- Rebalancing inside a tax-sheltered account avoids realizing taxable gains; outside, direct new contributions to the underweight asset first.
- This mechanical stock/bond variation is about the best automatic formula for all investors. Its main benefit is behavioral: it makes the investor act against the crowd (see market-fluctuations.md).
- Cautionary history: Yale's famous formula (35% normal equity) was abandoned in the 1950s bull market; by 1969 Yale held 61% equities. Great bull markets kill formula discipline; that is exactly when it matters.

## 4. Expected returns (the classic arithmetic)

- Stock return ≈ **dividend yield + growth from reinvested earnings** (the Gordon equation). Classic estimate, late 1971: 3.5% dividend + about 4% annual appreciation = about 7.5% pre-tax, about 5.3% after tax; a 50-50 portfolio about 7.8% pre-tax.
- Check forecasts in real terms: stocks returned 6.5% a year from 1972 through 1981, close to the classic 7.5% estimate, but inflation ran 8.6% a year, so the real return was negative. A nominal forecast that ignores inflation can look right and still lose purchasing power.
- Modern version: expected nominal return = real EPS growth (about 1.5-2%/yr long-run) + inflation + dividend yield, plus or minus "speculative growth" (changes in the public's appetite for stocks). Early 2003: 1.5-2% + 2.4% + 1.9% = about 6% nominal, 4% real. See market-level.md for valuation-based checks.
- Inflation adds to stock values only if it raises the **rate of earnings on capital**; historically it did not. Do not add an inflation premium on top of the expected return.
- Market prices that outrun earnings and dividends (DJIA 1949-1969: price up more than 5x while earnings and dividends roughly doubled) are a "bootstrap operation" driven by attitude change and imply poorer future returns.
- Measure results after inflation, taxes and costs ("money illusion" makes nominal gains feel better than equal real outcomes; 11% CDs in 1980 lost money after inflation while 2% CDs in 2003 kept pace).

## 5. The bond and cash component

Choose by two questions: taxable or tax-free? short or long maturity? A modern third: individual bonds or bond funds?

### Taxable vs tax-free
- It is arithmetic: compare the yield sacrifice with your marginal tax rate. January 1972 example: 20-year Aa corporates about 7.5% vs prime municipals 5.3%, a 30% income sacrifice -> munis win if your bracket exceeds 30%.
- Compute the taxable-equivalent yield = muni yield / (1 - marginal rate) and compare with Treasury and corporate yields; account for state taxes and any alternative minimum tax. Munis also fluctuate more and yield less than most taxable bonds.
- Placement: unless in the lowest bracket, hold munis in taxable accounts and taxable bonds inside retirement accounts; munis waste their advantage inside a sheltered account.

### Maturity
- Long bonds fall more when rates rise and rise more when they fall; short ones barely move. Bond-price and interest-rate prediction is impossible, so maturity choice is personal preference.
- Warning from history: after 1964 rising rates cut 20-year bond prices by up to about 38% while short-term "cash equivalents" lost nothing and yielded more than stocks. Long bonds are not riskless.
- Modern update: intermediate maturities (5-10 years) split the difference and take you out of the rate-guessing game; laddering maturities is another hedge.
- Bonds of 7 years or less and savings bonds need not be monitored for price changes.

### Individual bonds vs funds
- Individual bonds come in about $10,000 lots and you need at least 10 issuers to diversify default risk, so individual corporate/muni bonds make sense only with $100,000+ for bonds. Exception: Treasuries (no default risk; buy direct from the Treasury, no fees).
- Otherwise use low-cost bond funds: diversification, monthly income, commission-free reinvestment.

### Bond quality rules
- Defensive investors: high-grade only. For municipals require one of the **three highest ratings (AAA/Aaa, AA/Aa, A) from both** major agencies. Revenue bonds depend on specific receipts (tolls, leases) and need scrutiny.
- Avoid lower-grade (high-yield) bonds bought for yield: whatever their aggregate long-run return, they expose the holder to too many individual risks, from sharp price falls to default. Bargains in low-grade bonds exist but need special study (see enterprising-selection.md for the bargain thresholds).
- Coverage, not ratings alone, is the real test of safety; see security-analysis.md for the times-interest-earned minimums.
- Call protection: callable bonds are "heads I win, tails you lose" (American Gas & Electric 5s of 2028: sold at 101 in 1928, fell to 62.5 in 1932, would have reached about 160 in 1946 but were called at 106). In long-term bonds, give up a little yield for non-callability of 20-25 years, or buy low-coupon bonds at a deep discount (they carry built-in call protection).
- Government-guaranteed agency debt sometimes yields more than direct Treasuries of the same maturity with equivalent backing; check whether the guarantee is the full faith and credit of the government (the 1972 examples no longer exist).

### Cash and near-cash
- Bank savings deposits and CDs are a suitable substitute for short-term bonds when they pay as much.
- Everyone needs some cash: job loss, divorce, disability can strike at any age.
- Savings bonds: absolute safety, redeemable, tax-deferred; best simple choice for small bond amounts (classic rule: up to $10,000 in 1972 dollars). Today they are not marketable and carry an early-redemption interest penalty; use them as set-aside money for known future needs. Inflation-indexed savings bonds also exist.
- Modern bond menu (end-2002 yields, to show the trade-offs): T-bills (default risk extremely low, rate risk very low); T-notes (rate risk moderate); long T-bonds (rate risk high); savings bonds; CDs (insured); money-market funds; mortgage-backed securities (moderate risk; buy only through funds, never an individual CMO from a broker); municipals; preferred stock (high risk both ways); high-yield bonds (high default risk); emerging-market debt (high default risk). Treasuries are exempt from state tax; munis usually only from the issuing state's.

### Stocks for income
- Never buy a stock for its dividend alone. The business must be sound and the price reasonable; run the defensive stock tests (defensive-selection.md) first.
- With that condition met, adding a few solid dividend-paying stocks to an all-bond portfolio can raise its income and its long-run return. In early 2003, 115 S&P 500 stocks yielded 3% or more.
- Remember that stocks will not supply dependable income the way bonds do (§9); dividends can be cut.

## 6. Securities to avoid or restrict

- **Straight preferred stock**: an inherently bad form. It lacks the bondholder's legal claim and the common holder's profit share; its safety depends on the company's ability and will to keep paying common dividends; its weakness shows in depressions. Buy only when unduly depressed by temporary adversity, as an enterprising bargain ("bargain basis or not at all"). Investment-grade preferreds belong with corporations, which get a dividend-received tax deduction. Modern update: issuers call preferreds when rates fall or credit improves, capping upside; ask why a healthy company pays a fat non-deductible preferred dividend instead of issuing deductible bonds.
- **Income bonds** (interest payable only if earned): a better form than preferred stock for issuers (deductible interest), and investors should demand an unconditional right to interest when earned plus remedies short of bankruptcy.
- **Second-grade bonds and preferreds at full price**: avoid. Accepting a real chance of principal loss for 1-2% more yield is bad business (the "businessman's investment" fallacy). If you accept risk, demand the chance of a substantial principal gain, which means buying well below par. Enterprising threshold: at least 30% below par for high-coupon issues. A second-grade 5.5-6% bond at par is almost always a bad buy; the same bond at 70 may make sense, and patience usually brings 70 in the next weak market.
- **Foreign government bonds**: avoid. No legal means of enforcing a claim; long record of defaults (Cuba 4.5s 117 in 1953 to 20 in 1963; Czechoslovakia 8s swung from 117 in 1946 to 8 in 1970). Modern update: a small emerging-market bond fund allocation (no more than 10% of bonds, expenses no higher than about 1.25%) can diversify for investors who tolerate risk.
- **Junk-bond funds**: diversification solves the classic single-issuer objection, but fees are often high and principal erodes. A minor option, e.g., for a retiree wanting extra income who can bear temporary drops, or a financial-industry employee hedging rising rates.
- **Annuities**: good ones are bought, not sold. High expenses and surrender charges usually overwhelm the tax deferral. Consider only very low-cost providers bought directly; variable annuities rarely suit investors under 50 who expect a high retirement bracket or who have not maxed out retirement accounts.
- **New issues of any kind**: see enterprising-selection.md and red-flags.md.

## 7. Inflation and inflation hedges

- Neither "stocks always beat bonds" nor "bonds are always safer" holds; even high-quality stocks are not a better buy than bonds regardless of price and yield.
- Inflation and stock prices have **no close time connection** (1966-1970: cost of living +22%, stock earnings and prices fell). Since 1926 stocks beat inflation in 50 of 64 rolling five-year periods (78%), not always. In years of inflation above 6% U.S. stocks lost money 8 times out of 14 and averaged 2.6%; in deflation years they did terribly. Stocks are half a hedge.
- Do not let an inflation thesis justify buying stocks at any price: the inflation believer reads every bull-market rise as vindication and keeps buying regardless of level or yield, which "way lies sorrow".
- Inflation risk is real and recurrent (U.S. 1973-1982 prices more than doubled; since 1960, 69% of market economies had at least one year above 25% inflation, destroying on average 53% of purchasing power). Keep some bonds as insurance against deflation too.
- Classic spending rule: with about 3% inflation, spending only about half of after-tax interest income keeps principal's purchasing power intact.
  - The 3% assumption was a serious misjudgment: inflation hit 8.7% in 1973, and the cost of living more than doubled from 1973 through 1982. Do not apply "half" mechanically. Recompute the spendable share from current inflation (reinvest at least the part of the yield that inflation eats) and judge results in real terms.
- Hedges:
  - Stock component: more protection than bonds, not certain protection.
  - **Inflation-indexed government bonds (TIPS)**: principal rises with CPI, no default risk. The inflation adjustment is taxed yearly as income, so hold them in tax-deferred accounts. Use them as the substitute for retirement cash; hold permanently, do not trade; hold at least 10% of retirement assets in them (modern update).
  - **REIT index funds**: decent long-run defense; weaker case if you already own your home.
  - Gold and collectibles: the classic view treats them as poor hedges (gold went from $35 to $48 in 1935-1972 with no income and storage costs; collectible prices seem artificial). Modern dissenters allow at most about 2% of assets (perhaps 5% over age 65) in a diversified precious-metals-stock fund with expenses below 1%, never physical gold.
  - Real estate: some inflation protection, but wide swings, location and price errors, salesmen's wiles, and no diversification for moderate means; "be sure it's yours" before entering.

## 8. Dollar-cost averaging and autopilot investing

- DCA = invest the same dollar amount at regular intervals, buying more shares when prices are low. It is a formula plan that works without forecasting.
- Evidence: Lucile Tomlinson's test of 23 ten-year DCA periods in DJIA stocks (ending 1929 through 1952): every one showed a profit at the end of the buying period or within five years after; average gain 21.5% excluding dividends. Raskob's 1929 plan, begun at a market peak, still compounded at more than 8% over 1929-1948. Later evidence: $12,000 lump sum in the S&P 500 in Sept 1929 was $7,223 ten years later; $100 a month from the same date grew to $15,571. End-1999 to end-2002: $3,000 plus $100/month lost 30.2% vs the index's -41.3%.
- Existing DCA plans may continue through high markets, but do **not start a new DCA plan at a dangerously high market level** because early losses can break the investor's stamina. DCA begun at a reasonable level needs no attention to market levels.
- Modern autopilot portfolio: automatic monthly transfers into index funds; e.g., $500/month = $300 total U.S. stock market index + $100 international stock index + $100 U.S. bond index. Then answer every forecast question with "I don't know and I don't care."
- Behavioral thresholds for a DIY defensive portfolio: if you trade more than twice a year or spend more than an hour or two a month on it, something has gone wrong.
- Formalize it with an investment owner's contract (see market-fluctuations.md).

## 9. Personal situations

Three classic cases (multiply 1972 dollar figures by about 5 for 2003, roughly 7-8 for the mid-2020s):
- **Widow with $200,000 supporting herself and children**: conservatism is paramount; about 50-50 high-grade bonds and first-grade stocks (stocks up to 75% only if psychologically prepared and nearly certain the market is not too high). She must not speculate to "make some extra income": drawing about 1% a year from principal ($2,000) beats risking half the fund in poorly grounded ventures. She may still choose to be enterprising if equipped.
- **Successful doctor with $100,000 and $10,000 a year of savings**: same choices; invest new savings in the same proportions. Professionals with ample confidence in their intelligence but little time are notoriously poor at security dealings; success requires sustained attention and a professional approach to value.
- **Young saver putting away $1,000 a year**: default to the defensive program (part automatically into savings bonds). If interested in finance, start the education early and test judgment of price versus value with the smallest possible sums; do not try to beat the market.

Risk-capacity questions (modern update) — use them to place the investor between 25% and 75% in bonds and cash:
- [ ] Single or married? What does the partner earn, and how secure is it?
- [ ] Children? When do tuition bills arrive?
- [ ] Inheritance expected, or responsibility for aging parents?
- [ ] Career risks correlated with markets (bank or homebuilder job vs rising rates; chemical maker vs oil prices)? Self-employed, and how long do similar businesses survive?
- [ ] Is investment income needed to supplement earned income (bonds provide it; stocks will not)?
- [ ] Given salary and spending, how much can be lost without changing life?
- Higher capacity -> near the 25% bond/cash minimum; lower -> toward the 75% maximum.

Other rules:
- An investor in advanced retirement may be unable to outlast a long bear market; still, do not sell merely because prices fell (it turns paper losses into real ones and costs heirs a stepped-up tax basis).
- Employees should keep only a small percentage of assets in their employer's stock; "knowing" the company is often an illusion (Enron, WorldCom 401(k)s).
- Keep speculation, if any, in a separate account, the smaller the better; never add to it because the market rose and profits rolled in (that is the time to take money out); never mingle it with investments (classic rule). The modern update adds a hard cap on this "mad money": never more than 10% of total wealth.

## 10. Taxes

- Hold winners at least one year (long-term rates) and, when possible, at least five years. Trading converts long-term gains into ordinary income and, with costs, means a trader needs a gain of roughly 10% just to break even (2003 rates).
- Tax-loss harvesting is the one sound reason to sell into a bear market: realize a loss, wait out the wash-sale period, rebuy (see market-fluctuations.md for the worked example). Verify current rules.
- Place tax-inefficient assets (taxable bonds, TIPS, REITs) in tax-deferred accounts and munis in taxable accounts.
- In a taxable account, avoid large new fund purchases just before a fund's year-end capital-gain distribution (usually paid in the fourth quarter): you would owe tax on gains earned before you owned the fund. Check the estimated distribution and record dates, and buy after them.
- The classic and modern tax notes list 1972 and 2003 U.S. tax rules (capital-gains rates, dividend taxation, fund distributions, wash sales). They are dated; always check the investor's current jurisdiction and law.

## 11. Translating to today

- The 25-75% band, 50-50 default, rebalancing, high-grade bond floor and DCA are timeless.
- The classic yield levels (7.5% corporates, 5.3% munis, 5% savings bonds) and the "dividend yield = two-thirds of bond yield" trigger come from 1972. Since about 1959 U.S. stock dividend yields have stayed below bond yields, so apply the trigger as a relative-valuation idea (compare stocks' earnings yield, cyclically adjusted, against high-grade bond yields; see market-level.md), not as a literal dividend test.
- Inflation-adjust dollar thresholds using current CPI data (1972 dollars ×≈7-8 in the mid-2020s; ×5 for 2003).
- Products the classic framework praised that no longer exist (Series E/H bonds, New Housing and New Community debentures) map to today's Treasuries, inflation-indexed bonds and government-guaranteed agency debt.
