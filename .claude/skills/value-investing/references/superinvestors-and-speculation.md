## 1. Buffett's argument against "it's all luck" (Buffett's 1984 talk, 1984 Columbia talk)

Use this reasoning to evaluate any claim that a manager's record proves (or disproves) skill.
- **Coin-flip contest:** 225 million people each bet $1 on a daily coin flip, losers drop out, winners roll their stakes. After 10 days about 220,000 remain with a little over $1,000 each; after 20 days, 215 have turned $1 into about $1 million. $225 million lost, $225 million won. They will write books and lecture skeptics; 225 million orangutans would produce the same 215.
- **Concentration test:** if 40 of the 215 winning orangutans came from one Omaha zoo, you would investigate that zoo, as you would a town producing 400 of the country's 1,500 annual cases of a rare cancer. Clusters of success point to causes. Buffett's cluster is intellectual: one shared value-investing school.
- **Independence test:** the cluster means nothing if winners copy one leader (100 followers = 1 case) or vote as family units (215 winners from 21.5 families). The school's members share the theory but made independent, very different choices with almost no portfolio overlap.
- **Pre-identification:** the investors were identified as superior before their records were known (the latest more than 15 years before the talk), records were audited, and partners' checks matched the records. Selecting winners after the fact from thousands of records would prove nothing.

Checklist for judging a track record:
- [ ] Was the manager identified by process before the results?
- [ ] Is the record audited, after fees, and over 10+ years including bad markets?
- [ ] Is the process independent of other winners (low overlap) yet based on a common, explainable logic?
- [ ] Did the manager take less risk than average (look at weak-market years)?

Common theme: all search for discrepancies between the value of a business and the price of small pieces of it in the market; they focus on price and value only, ignoring day-of-week or January effects, beta, the capital asset pricing model and covariance. Buffett: businessmen buying a whole business do not care whether it is Monday or January, so why should buyers of small pieces? Academics study price and volume because computers made the data plentiful, not because the studies are useful; to a man with a hammer, everything looks like a nail.

Other points:
- The market contains much inefficiency: prices are set at the margin by the most emotional, greediest or most depressed participant, so they are frequently nonsensical.
- The value idea is like an inoculation: it takes immediately or never; Buffett never saw a gradual convert over ten years, and IQ or academic training does not decide it.
- Size is the anchor of performance: outperformance is possible as assets grow, but the margin shrinks; at the size of the whole market you cannot beat the average.
- Why publish: the secret has been out for 50 years with no trend toward value investing; academia retreated from teaching it; people seem perversely drawn to making easy things difficult; price-value gaps will persist.

- Different styles (dozens of holdings vs a handful) all worked; the common element is buying business value at a discount.
- Concentration raises volatility sharply (Munger, Guerin) without changing the method; match style to temperament.
- Expect multi-year stretches of underperformance (Sequoia 1970-74).

## 3. Risk, reward and beta (Buffett's 1984 talk)

- Sometimes risk and reward rise together (Buffett's revolver: $1 million for one pull with one cartridge, $5 million for two). In value investing they move oppositely: buying a dollar for 60 cents is riskier than buying it for 40 cents, yet the 40-cent purchase also offers more reward.
- Worked example, Washington Post Co. 1973: market value $80 million; its assets (the Post, Newsweek, several major-market TV stations) could have been sold to any of ten buyers for at least $400 million; worth about $2 billion by 1984. Had the price fallen to $40 million, its beta would have risen and beta-users would call it riskier, which Buffett calls absurd. Buying $400 million of properties for $80 million carries essentially no risk, especially if done as ten $40 million piles for $8 million each.
- Requirements: honest and reasonably competent managers (not hard to find) and enough knowledge to estimate business value very generally.
- Do not cut it close: never try to buy a business worth $83 million for $80 million. Engineer it like a bridge rated for 30,000 pounds that only 10,000-pound trucks cross. Full margin-of-safety tests: margin-of-safety.md.

## 4. The new speculation in common stocks (a classic May 1958 address to financial analysts)

Thesis: speculation used to be **endogenous**, inside the company (uncertain, fluctuating or weak industries and capital structures). That kind has diminished: finances are firmer, capital structures conservative, management more expert and honest, disclosure complete. The new major speculation is **exogenous**, coming from buyers and analysts themselves: primary emphasis on future expectations.

Three paradoxes of valuing stocks on expected future performance:
1. It erases much of the old investment/speculation distinction ("speculate" comes from specula, a lookout); the investor now shares the speculator's lookout.
2. The companies with the best credit attract the most speculative interest in their shares, because everyone assumes a brilliant future.
3. Growth projections invite higher-mathematics present-value formulas; precise formulas with imprecise assumptions can justify almost any value, which also means no value or narrow range can hold, so at times the market may value the growth component strikingly low.

Evidence:
- **Old speculative vs investment stock, 1911-13:** Pennsylvania RR ranged 53-65 (12.2-15x average earnings) with steady profits, a reliable $3 dividend and well over its $50 par in tangible assets. American Can ranged 9-47, earned 7 cents to $8.86, sold at 1.9-10x three-year average earnings, paid nothing, and its $100-par common was pure "water" (the preferred exceeded tangible assets). Yet American Can had the far better future, unsuspected and considered irrelevant then.
- **IBM (C-T-R):** early 1916 the stock was in the mid-40s (105,000 shares), had earned $6.50 in 1915, book $130 (including intangibles), had started a $3 dividend; a senior partner of the classic analyst's firm dismissed it as all water because its 6% bonds sold in the low 80s. In 1926 it first disclosed $13.6 million of goodwill (the partner was right), but net had risen from $691,000 to $3.7 million (over 5x) under T. J. Watson Sr.; after a 3.6-for-1 split it earned $6.39 and paid $3; the 1926 range was 31-59; at the 45 average it sold at the same 7x earnings and 6.7% yield as in 1915, and at 31 not far above tangible book. Then: 1936 net 2x 1926, average multiplier 7 to 17.5; 1946 net 2.5x 1936, multiplier still 17.5; 1956 net nearly 4x 1946, multiplier 32.5; 1957 multiplier about 42. The old balance-sheet water was squeezed out by disclosure and write-offs, but investors put new water into the price: IBM at 7x book is practically as if it had no book value; the small book portion is like a minor preferred component and the rest is the same bet on earning power the old speculator made on Woolworth or U.S. Steel.
- **The three M's of old speculation** were mystery, manipulation and thin margins, now nearly gone (a classic analyst once dug the hidden earnings of Consolidated Gas's New York Edison subsidiary out of public-utility commission filings; a friend warned that Wall Street prefers the mystery). The new 3M's: Minnesota Mining & Manufacturing at 101 in 1957 sold at 44x 1956 earnings (no real 1957 increase); enterprise value $1.7 billion, of which $200 million net assets and about $1.5 billion market-assigned goodwill; months later the market cut that goodwill by about $450 million (about 30%). Law: the more important the goodwill or future-earnings factor, the more uncertain the true value and the more inherently speculative the stock.
- **Reversal in valuing intangibles:** a generation earlier a good industrial needed to earn 6-8% on tangible assets (typically represented by bonds and preferred), and earnings above that, or the intangibles they implied, were capitalized at about 15% (Woolworth's 1911 offering). By 1958 the reverse: a company had to earn about 10% on equity to sell at book, and earnings above 10% were capitalized more generously. Worked example: 15% on equity, selling at 13.5x earnings = 2x book -> the first 10% of return is valued at 10x, the next 5% at 20x. Reason: high returns on capital go with growth records, so buyers pay for assumed superior future growth, not old-style goodwill.
- **GE:** pre-1947 growth was modest and irregular (1946 adjusted EPS 52 cents vs 40 cents in 1902, never doubled in between), yet P/E rose from 9 (1910, 1916) to 29 (1936, 1946); in 1947, a record earnings year, the low of 32 (pre 3-for-1 split) was only 9x earnings and the year averaged about 10x. Whether prominent, promising companies will always sell at high P/Es is doubtful and remains unsettled.

Old vs new attitudes:
- Old investors emphasized defensive qualities (dividend maintenance in bad times); superior prospects were something to look for but not to pay for, since stocks of equal strength, record and dividend stability sold at about the same yield. Shortsighted, but simple, sound and highly profitable.
- An old (about 1920) pamphlet line: if a common stock is a good investment it is also a good speculation. True only while ignored: once the public embraced stocks, prices destroyed their margin and drove them out of the investment class; by 1931 an authority declared no common stock could be an investment.
- Capital-gains paradox: the old investor, uninterested in appreciation, virtually guaranteed himself appreciation; today's investor pays heavily in advance for growth, so it may occur and still bring no profit, and a shortfall can mean serious, even permanent, loss.

## 5. Growth-stock mathematics and the U-shaped risk curve (a classic 1958 address)

- **Value ~ earnings squared / book:** if the multiplier rises with return on book, value rises roughly as the square of earnings and inversely with book value, so tangible assets become a drag on market value. Worked example: A earns $4 on $20 book, B earns $4 on $100 book; A sells at about $60, B at about $35: B's extra $80 of assets are in effect responsible for a $25 lower price.
- **The philosopher's stone:** (a) optimistic growth assumptions + (b) a long enough projection + (c) compound interest can justify any desired value for a "good stock." Compare David Durand's comparison of growth-stock valuation with the St. Petersburg paradox.
- **Rule:** in the stock market, the more elaborate the mathematics, the more uncertain and speculative the conclusion. Forty-four years of classic practice turned up no dependable stock valuations or investment policies that went beyond simple arithmetic or elementary algebra; calculus or higher algebra is a warning that theory is replacing experience and speculation is being dressed as investment.
- **U-shaped curve of speculative risk vs credit quality:**
  - Old days: speculative content of the common fell in a straight line as the company's credit rating rose.
  - Now: highly speculative at the low-credit left (as always); also speculative at the highest-credit right, because the market keeps pricing the best companies so high that the price itself carries considerable risk (Shakespeare's sonnet on those who lose all by paying too much rent).
  - Minimum speculation in the middle: strong, established companies growing about with the national economy, buyable at most times (except high in bull markets) at moderate prices vs intrinsic value; because investors chase glamour, these tend to sell somewhat below independently determinable values, so the same prejudice that destroys the margin in glamour stocks supplies it here, with room for analysis, selectivity and diversification.
- Motto (Ovid's advice to Phaethon): you will go safest in the middle course.
- [ ] When valuing a high-quality growth stock, compute what share of the price is goodwill / future growth; the larger it is, classify the purchase as more speculative and demand a wider margin (margin-of-safety.md, growth section).

## 6. Aetna Maintenance: the small-IPO template (the Aetna Maintenance case)

- Nov 1961: 154,000 shares sold at $9; quickly $15. Net assets about $1.20/share before, slightly over $3 after the new money.
- Record before the offering (sales / net / EPS): Dec 1956 $1,003,000 / $2,000 / $.01; Dec 1957 $1,083,000 / $21,000 / $.07; Dec 1958 $1,389,000 / $16,000 / $.06; Dec 1959 $2,215,000 / $48,000 / $.17; six months to June 1960 $1,527,000 / $25,000 / $.09; year to June 1961 $3,615,000 / $187,000 / $.69.
- After: June 1962 $4,234,000 / $149,000 / $.36; June 1963 $4,681,000 / deficit $42,000 / deficit $.11. Price fell to about 2 2/3 in 1962 (other sources give 2 3/8) and 7/8 in 1964; no dividends.
- Verdict: far too small for public participation; sold and bought on one good year after derisory results; a highly competitive business with no assurance of stability; at the post-issue high the public paid more per dollar of earnings and assets than for most large, strong companies; hundreds of lesser cases.
- Sequel 1965-1970: new owners sold the unprofitable building-maintenance business and turned it into an electronic-devices maker (Haydon Switch and Instrument); 1965-69 average earnings 8 cents per old share, best year 34 cents (1967); 2-for-1 split in 1968; price from 7/8 (1964) to the equivalent of 16.5 (1968), above the 1961 high: 52x its only good year and about 200x average earnings, reported a deficit that same year; bid $1 in 1969.
- Questions it raises: did the 1968 buyers know the history, the 5-year record or the tiny asset value, or care? Does anyone on Wall Street bear responsibility for this recurring, brainless, catastrophic speculation?
- Checklist for any small new or re-rated issue:
  - [ ] More than one good year in the record?
  - [ ] Business stable and defensible, not highly competitive?
  - [ ] Price per dollar of earnings and assets no higher than for large, strong companies?
  - [ ] Asset value meaningful relative to price?
  - [ ] Business change, split or name change used to restart a story?

## 7. NVF's tax accounting for Sharon Steel (the NVF tax case)

(The takeover itself: case-studies.md, "minnow swallows whale.")
1. Price paid per Sharon share: $70 of NVF 5% bonds due 1994 (initially quoted about 43% of par = about $30) + warrants for 1.5 NVF shares at $22 (quoted about $10 per NVF share = $15) = about $45, roughly Sharon's 1968 average and closing price. Sharon's book value was $60/share; the gap between book and market on the 1,415,000 shares acquired was about $21 million.
2. The accounting was built to:
   - (a) treat the bond issue as a "sale" at 43, creating a $54 million bond discount amortized as a deduction from income (in effect about 15% annual interest on the "proceeds" of the $99 million debenture issue);
   - (b) offset it with a roughly equal "profit": crediting income each year with one-tenth of the difference between the 45 cost and the 60 book value of the Sharon shares (the mirror image of the required amortization of goodwill when paying above book);
   - (c) save about $900,000 a year (about $1/share) in income taxes, because the discount amortization was tax-deductible while the "excess of equity over cost" credit was not taxable.
3. Because much of the cost was treated as paid with warrants, about $22 million of warrant value was put in the common-stock capital account (disclosed only in a note), a case without known precedent.
- Checks: in any acquisition financed with deep-discount paper, look for paired amortization entries (a deductible discount charge offset by a non-taxable credit), warrants or options carried in equity, and tax savings that depend on the acquirer's own securities trading at distressed prices.

## 8. Technology-name companies, 1968-1971 (the 1968-71 technology-name list)

- Mid-1971 S&P listed about 200 companies whose names began with Compu-, Data, Electro-, Scien- or Techno-; about half in the computer industry; all traded or seeking to sell stock.
- Of 46 in the Sept 1971 Stock Guide: 26 reported deficits, only 6 earned over $1/share, only 5 paid dividends.
- Of 45 such companies in the Dec 1968 Stock Guide, by Sept 1971: 2 rose; 8 fell less than half; 23 fell more than half; 12 were dropped from the Guide (probably worse). Those never listed in the Guide almost certainly did worse still.
- IBM's phenomenal success and a few others spawned a wave of new issues in their fields "for which large losses were virtually guaranteed" (the one quote in this file).
- Lesson: a fashionable industry label plus heavy new-issue supply predicts large group losses; most such companies lack earnings and dividends. (Same pattern: the Red Hat case, VA Linux in enterprising-selection.md.)

## 9. Further data points

Items not obviously covered in other reference files; one line each.
- Real-money record of a classic net-net partnership (1936-56): at least 14.7% a year against 12.2% for the stock market as a whole. The often-quoted figure of about 20% a year probably excludes fees. Quote the net figure when citing the method's real-money evidence.
- "Letter stock" is unregistered stock bought with a letter stating the purchase is for investment (it cannot be freely resold; relevant to 1960s fund valuations).
- Speculation and returns: the classic after-tax return arithmetic assumed a top bracket of 40% on dividends and 20% on capital gains.
- Inflation: the S&P 425 industrials earned about 11.5% on asset value, partly because IBM (not a Dow stock) was included; AT&T's residential phone rates were slightly lower in 1970 than in 1960 (regulated prices lagging inflation).
- Market history: a 1971 Wall Street study projected the DJIA at a median 1520 in 1975 (about 1200 discounted to mid-1971); the Dow did not close above 1520 until Dec 13, 1985.
- Portfolio policy: Industrial Revenue Bonds could offer higher tax-free yields with adequate safety, of interest to enterprising investors (check today's private-activity bond/AMT treatment).
- Defensive stocks: defining risk as volatility (as in Brealey's 1969 text) is more harmful than useful because it overemphasizes market fluctuations; all 30 Dow stocks met one of the general defensive standards in 1971.
- New issues: a Stock Guide sample of 41 issues of that type showed 5 lost 90%+ of their high, 30 lost over half, the group about two-thirds; Hickman's study of corporate bond quality later inspired Michael Milken's junk-bond financing.
- Enterprising methods: a company with an ordinary record is a "promising company," not a growth stock; the classic working definition of a growth stock: per-share earnings growing well above the general rate and expected to continue, at least doubling in 10 years (a little over 7% a year; years to double ≈ 72 ÷ growth rate, so 7.2% doubles in about 10 years); the modern, stricter definition: EPS up at least 15% a year on average for at least five years running (no guarantee for the future); give both; two selling proverbs: no tree grows to heaven, and hogs never make money; two studies of the low-multiplier method: H. G. Schneider (1917-1950) and Drexel Firestone (1933-1969).
- Market fluctuations: Dow-theory authority Robert Ross noted the last two buy signals (Dec 1966, Dec 1970) came well below the preceding sell points; early floating-rate and put-able bonds existed by 1971 (an Italian state utility's floating-rate notes at 8 1/8%; Toronto-Dominion "7%-8%" debentures with a holder's option to redeem in 1976).
- Funds: quoting fund sales charges as a % of the offering price (which includes the charge) makes them look smaller than as a % of net asset value; a sales gimmick; on bubble history, Mackay's 1841 book is not always accurate; Chancellor's and Menschel's histories are better.
- Advisers: the NYSE's capital "haircut" rules failed to prevent the 1969-70 broker failures; SEC prospectuses fully disclose but are so long that few buyers read them, so they rely on the salesman.
- Security analysis: exceptional analysts who can tell in advance which companies merit intensive study may succeed (Philip Fisher's method); Chrysler's 1963 run was helped by two 2-for-1 splits in one year; turnaround managers are hard to identify (Al Dunlap lifted Scott Paper 225% in 18 months, then used improper accounting at Sunbeam); the 8.5 + 2g growth formula approximates fashionable calculations, not "true value."
- Company comparison: Emery Air Freight sold at 64x its 1971 earnings in March 1972.
- Defensive selection: by Dec 1970, 12 of the 29 Dow industrials failed the 2-to-1 current ratio and 12 failed net current assets > debt (only 2 each in 1960); the qualifying list then lagged the DJIA from Dec 1970 to early 1972, showing that the criteria guarantee only getting your money's worth, not superior results; the debt test excludes most gas pipelines (heavily bonded behind purchase contracts, too complex for the defensive investor); the DJIA's actual average share price was about $53 in early 1972.

## 10. Translating to today

- Buffett's records end in 1983-84; they show that disciplined value approaches beat the indexes over decades, not that any current manager will. Apply the pre-identification, independence and audited-record tests to any manager (funds-and-advisers.md).
- Buffett's beta critique remains valid: a lower price for the same business lowers risk. When a tool rates a cheaper stock as riskier only because it fell, prefer the business-value view.
- The classic 1958 6-8%/15% and 10%-on-equity benchmarks reflect 1911-1958 rates; translate them by comparing return on equity and earnings yield with current bond yields. The value ~ earnings-squared effect and the U-shaped curve describe every growth-stock era since (1972 "nifty fifty," 1999-2000 tech, later mega-cap growth episodes).
- The Aetna and technology-name patterns apply to any small-cap IPO wave or fashionable sector name; The NVF tax mechanics apply to acquisitions financed with deeply discounted debt, purchase-price accounting gains and equity-classified warrants.
