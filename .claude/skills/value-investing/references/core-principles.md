## 1. The five pillars

1. A stock is an ownership interest in an actual business whose underlying value does not depend on the share price.
2. The market is a pendulum between unsustainable optimism (stocks too expensive) and unjustified pessimism (too cheap). Buy from pessimists, sell to optimists.
3. The future value of every investment is a function of its present price: the higher the price paid, the lower the return.
4. You can never eliminate the risk of being wrong; insist on a margin of safety (never overpay) to minimize the odds and cost of error.
5. Success depends on your own behavior: think critically, take no Wall Street "fact" on faith, and do not let others' mood swings govern your decisions. How you behave matters more than how your investments behave.

Buffett's summary: success needs a sound intellectual framework for decisions plus the ability to keep emotions from corroding it; this framework supplies the first, you must supply the second. The sillier the market, the bigger the opportunity for the businesslike investor. Of all the ideas, Mr. Market and the margin of safety matter most (Buffett).

## 2. Investment vs speculation

Definition (classic, 1934): **an investment operation is one which, upon thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and an adequate return. Operations not meeting these requirements are speculative.**
- Thorough analysis = study of the facts in the light of established standards of safety and value.
- Safety of principal = protection against loss under all normal or reasonably likely conditions.
- Adequate return = any return, however low, the investor accepts while acting with reasonable intelligence. Aim for adequate, not extraordinary (all three elements are equally required).
- The investor judges the market price by established standards of value; the speculator bases his standards of value on the market price.
- The clearest test is attitude toward price movements: the speculator wants to anticipate and profit from fluctuations; the investor wants to acquire and hold suitable securities at suitable prices.
- The no-quote test: would you be willing to own this business on these terms if there were no market for the shares? Modern extension: be comfortable owning it even if quotes stopped for ten years.

Automatic speculation flags:
- Buying on margin (borrowed money) is speculation by definition. Brains and models do not make borrowed bets safe: in 1998 Long-Term Capital Management, run by mathematicians and two Nobel-winning economists, lost more than $2 billion in a few weeks on a bet that bond markets would return to "normal", and its leverage nearly capsized the financial system. Cite it when a user trusts intelligence or a model over leverage.
- Buying a "hot" new issue or anything bought for a quick profit is speculation or gambling.
- Betting on market direction has no margin of safety in any useful sense, however confident the forecaster.
- Claims of favorable odds based on timing skill, superior selection skill, a trusted adviser or a "system" rest on subjective judgment unsupported by evidence or conclusive reasoning.

Speculation is not illegal or immoral, and some is necessary (it finances new ventures; risk is transferred, never destroyed). The three forms of **unintelligent** speculation:
1. Speculating when you think you are investing.
2. Speculating seriously, rather than as a pastime, without adequate knowledge and skill.
3. Risking more than you can afford to lose.

Rules if someone insists on speculating: a separate account, the smaller the better (classic rule; the modern update caps this "mad money" at 10% of total wealth, never more); never add to it because it has been winning (that is the time to take money out); never mingle speculative and investment money or thinking.

Every common stock contains a speculative component (quotational risk). The investor's job is to keep it within minor limits and be prepared financially and psychologically for adverse results of short or long duration.

## 3. What "risk" means

- Do not equate risk with price fluctuation. A bona fide investor does not lose money merely because the market price falls; a mortgage holder or business owner measures risk by the chance of actual loss, not by forced-sale value.
- **True risk = loss of value that is (a) realized through actual sale, (b) caused by significant deterioration in the company's position, or (c) most often, the result of paying an excessive price relative to intrinsic worth.** Risk is present when the price was clearly too high by value standards, even if a later recovery recoups the decline.
- A properly executed, diversified group of common stocks bought at sensible prices is not "risky" merely because it fluctuates.
- Risk lives less in your investments than in what kind of investor you are; look in the mirror. Stocks become riskier as prices rise and less risky as they fall. "First, don't lose": a 95% loss needs a 1,900% gain to recover; a stock that halves in year one and then earns double the market's 5% still needs over 16 years to catch up; JDS Uniphase from its March 2000 peak needed 84.6 years at 5% a year (or 10.2 years at 50%) to break even.
- Consequences must dominate probabilities (Pascal's wager): you will certainly make mistakes; you can control how much a mistake costs. Diversify permanently and refuse fashionable bets so no error is catastrophic.

## 4. Price vs value: Mr. Market, timing vs pricing

(Full treatment: market-fluctuations.md.)
- **Mr. Market**: imagine you own a share of a private business and an obliging partner quotes you a price every day, offering to buy you out or sell you more. Sometimes his price is reasonable; often enthusiasm or fear drives it to silly levels. Use him only when it suits you: sell to him when his price is absurdly high, buy from him when it is absurdly low, and otherwise form your view of value from the company's reports and results.
- The investor's basic advantage is that he is never forced to sell. Letting unjustified declines stampede or worry him turns that advantage into a disadvantage; he would be better off without any quotation (perhaps the single most important idea in the framework).
- **Timing vs pricing**: timing = forecasting market moves (speculation, speculative results); pricing = buying below fair value and selling above it, at minimum never overpaying. Timing has value to an investor only when it coincides with pricing.
- Never buy a stock because it went up or sell because it went down; never dump a stock purely because its price fell without first asking whether the value of the business changed.
- Most of the time the market prices most stocks about right; occasionally it is wildly wrong. You need only act on the occasions.

## 5. Margin of safety (summary)

(Full treatment and tests: margin-of-safety.md.)
- The three-word secret of sound investment: **margin of safety**. Its function is to make an accurate estimate of the future unnecessary.
- Bonds: earnings well above fixed charges over many years (or enterprise value well above debt).
- Stocks bought at normal prices: expected earning power (earnings yield) considerably above the high-grade bond rate; over ten years the excess should add up to a large share (about half) of the price paid.
- Bargains: the gap between price and appraised value (bargain threshold: price no more than two-thirds of value).
- Growth stocks: only if conservative projections still leave a margin at the price paid; all estimates that depart from the past record should err toward understatement.
- Margin of safety and diversification work together, like insurance underwriting: each commitment has better odds of profit than loss, and many such commitments make an aggregate profit very likely.
- The worst losses come not from overpaying for quality but from buying low-quality securities at fair-weather prices during good business conditions.

## 6. Defensive vs enterprising (summary)

(Policy detail: portfolio-policy.md.)
- Defensive: safety plus freedom from effort; follows simple rules; accepts market-like results. Enterprising: invests time and skill to do better, but must first make sure results are not worse.
- Better-than-average results require policies that are (1) inherently sound and promising **and** (2) not popular on Wall Street. Only worth the effort if it can plausibly add about 5 percentage points a year pre-tax to the stock portion.
- Effort paradox: a creditable result is easy; improving on it is hard, and adding a little cleverness often makes results worse.
- No half-measures for the investor's own role.
- The three usual ways to try to beat the market, and why each fails:
  - Trading the market (buying after advances, selling after declines, chasing the stocks "behaving" best): not investment at all.
  - Short-term selectivity (buying companies expected to report higher earnings soon): this year's results are common knowledge and next year's are already in the price, because everyone else is doing the same.
  - Long-term selectivity (betting that excellent growth or a promising technology will pay off): the error risk is higher, and you must out-forecast the professional analysts at their own game.
  - The two obstacles behind all three: human fallibility (the forecast is wrong) and competition (the price already reflects it). Point a user who wants to trade or chase next year's earnings to the sound-but-unpopular routes instead (enterprising-selection.md).
- Short selling overvalued popular issues tests courage, stamina and pocketbook, and is not recommended. The potential loss is unlimited, so treat shorting as unacceptably speculative for individuals.

## 7. The law of decaying formulas: popular formulas self-destruct

- Any formula that becomes popular loses its reliability, because (1) conditions change in ways the formula did not anticipate and (2) its popularity changes market behavior. Any approach easy enough for many people to follow is too simple to last.
- Classic examples: a 1949 central-value formula for buying/selling the DJIA stopped working after 1949; the Dow theory worked on paper 1897-1933 but not once widely followed; net-current-asset bargains vanished in the 1960s; arbitrage profits were competed away.
- Modern examples: the January effect shrank once publicized and is eaten by trading costs in tiny stocks; "What Works on Wall Street" and the "Foolish Four" failed right after promotion; data mining finds chance patterns (a portfolio of stocks whose names had no repeated letters did about as well as the Foolish Four).
- Test any claimed edge: Does a business reason cause the outperformance? How long and large is the sample (prefer periods of about 50 years; 22 years is too short)? Does it survive costs and taxes? Has publicity already arbitraged it away?
- Stocks do well or poorly in the future because the businesses behind them do well or poorly, nothing more.

## 8. Paradoxes worth remembering

- **Quality paradox**: the better a company's record and prospects, the bigger the premium over book value, the less certain the basis of value, and the more the price depends on market moods. The highest-quality stocks can be more speculative than middle-grade ones.
- **Growth paradox**: obvious prospects for physical growth do not translate into obvious profits for investors; experts cannot reliably pick the winners in the most promising industries (airlines vs IBM). Foreseeing the fastest-growing industry is worth nothing if everyone foresees it: prices get bid up until future returns can only fall. Treat each new consensus "sure thing" industry (health care, energy, real estate, gold, whatever it is today) the same way.
- **Popularity paradox**: stocks are called speculative when they are cheap (1948 surveys: over 90% opposed buying them, just before 18.7% annual returns) and "investments" when dangerously high.
- **Bull/bear paradox**: the sensible investor dreads bull markets (stocks cost more) and welcomes bear markets (stocks on sale), provided enough cash is held for spending needs.
- **Size paradox**: the bigger a company gets, the slower it can grow.
- **Effort paradox**: see section 6.

## 9. The four business principles

Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike. Treat every security as a share in, or claim against, a specific business, and run your investing by business principles:
1. **Know what you are doing — know your business.** Do not try for business-sized profits unless you know as much about security values as you would need to know about goods you manufactured or traded.
2. **Do not let anyone else run your business** unless you can supervise their performance with adequate care and comprehension, or have unusually strong reasons for implicit confidence in their integrity and ability. (This governs every adviser, fund and manager; see funds-and-advisers.md.)
3. **Do not enter an operation unless a reliable calculation shows a fair chance of reasonable profit.** Avoid ventures with little to gain and much to lose; operate on arithmetic, not optimism. When accepting a small fixed return (bonds, preferreds), demand convincing evidence you are not risking a substantial part of principal.
4. **Have the courage of your knowledge and experience.** If your conclusion from the facts is sound, act on it even if others hesitate or differ. You are right not because the crowd agrees or disagrees but because your facts and reasoning are right. After knowledge and tested judgment, courage is the supreme virtue.

A defensive investor who limits ambition to capacity does not need these qualities: satisfactory results are easier to obtain than most people realize; superior results are harder than they look.

## 10. Behavioral discipline and decision checklists

The investor's chief problem, and even his worst enemy, is likely to be himself. The quality needed is character more than brain (patience, discipline, eagerness to learn, emotional control, independent thought). Isaac Newton sold South Sea stock for a 100% profit, rejoined the crowd at higher prices and lost £20,000: intelligence without temperament.

Biases the skill should name when it sees them:
- Extrapolation and recency: expecting recent returns to continue (survey expectations rose with prices in 1998-2000 and fell after the crash).
- Prediction addiction: the brain anticipates repetition after two or three occurrences and rewards "spotting" patterns, even in random data.
- Loss aversion: losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains please; drives selling near bottoms.
- Overconfidence and familiarity ("buy what you know" without the research; home bias; heavy employer-stock holdings).
- Vividness/availability: judging odds by memorable examples ("the next Microsoft" IPO).
- Money illusion: judging by nominal rather than real returns.
- Herding: following the crowd's enthusiasm, confidence and greed.
- Affective forecasting errors: misjudging how you will feel in a crash.

Kahneman's two marks of a good decision:
- **Well-calibrated confidence**
  - [ ] What is my track record with decisions like this?
  - [ ] What is the typical record of others who tried it?
  - [ ] If buying, how likely is it that I know something the seller does not (and vice versa)?
  - [ ] How much must it rise just to break even after taxes and trading costs?
- **Correctly anticipated regret**
  - [ ] If I am wrong, how much could I lose, judging by similar investments' history?
  - [ ] Do I own other assets that would tide me over, or that rise when this falls?
  - [ ] Am I putting too much of my capital at risk?
  - [ ] How do I know my risk tolerance is high? Have I lost a lot before, and did I buy more or bail out?
  - [ ] Am I relying on willpower alone, or have I pre-committed through diversification, an investment contract and dollar-cost averaging?

Control what you can: brokerage costs (trade rarely, cheaply), ownership costs (refuse high-expense funds), expectations (realism), risk (allocation, diversification, rebalancing), taxes (hold at least a year, ideally five), and above all your own behavior. Measure success by whether your plan gets you where you need to go, not by beating the market or other people.
