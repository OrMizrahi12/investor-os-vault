# Red Flags: The Cross-Cutting Warning-Sign Checklist

Value investing is as much a catalogue of what goes wrong as of what works. This file gathers every warning sign from the classic framework and its modern update into one checklist. Run it on every single-stock analysis (Workflow A, step 6), on every screen's survivors (Workflow B), and whenever a security, fund or adviser is being sold to the user.

## 1. How to use this checklist

- **A flag is a reason to dig, not an automatic verdict.** The "antennae words" in footnotes call for investigation, not rejection. Record each hit with the evidence (filing, page or note, date) and what you found when you dug.
- **Count and weigh.** One minor flag in an otherwise strong record is normal. Several flags in the same family (for example aggressive revenue recognition plus capitalized costs plus recurring write-downs) point to a pattern, and patterns are how the classic disasters looked beforehand.
- **Defensive investors need a clean record.** Anything in the "disqualifying" list below removes the stock from a defensive portfolio, whatever the price. Enterprising investors may buy despite a flag only at a bargain price, with the flag priced in and the position sized for diversification.
- **Price does not cure fraud.** Low price can compensate for weak quality (the bargain logic), but it cannot compensate for numbers you cannot trust. If the accounts are unreliable, no valuation is possible.
- **Cite the case.** When a flag matches one of the classic case histories, name it (see case-studies.md). It makes the warning concrete for the user.

## 2. Severity

**Disqualifying (stop and say so):**
- The auditor refused to certify the accounts, or qualified its opinion on a material matter.
- Earnings fail to cover fixed charges, or coverage is far below the coverage-table minimums for the industry (the Penn Central case).
- Operating cash flow is chronically negative while financing inflows keep the company alive ("OPM addict").
- Material related-party dealings between the company and its officers (the Enron proxy).
- Restated revenue, or revenue recognized before the cash is reasonably assured, on a scale that changes the earnings picture.
- Large reported profits for years with little or no income tax paid, and no clear explanation (the Penn Central case).

**Serious (demand a convincing explanation, then normally reject for defensive accounts):**
- "Nonrecurring" charges in two or more of the last five years.
- More than two or three acquisitions a year, or chronic write-offs of past acquisitions.
- Long-term debt including preferred above 50% of total capital. For a defensive industrial, long-term debt above net current assets.
- Option repricing for insiders, or option grants above about 3% of shares outstanding.
- Repeated large insider sales.
- One customer, or a handful, supplying most of revenue.
- Pension income above 5% of net income, or an assumed plan return that is high or rising.

**Context (note, then weigh with everything else):** high institutional ownership, quarterly earnings guidance, hyped stock splits, a P/E far above the market's, heavy promotion, chairman's letters that change story with each fad.

## 3. Price and valuation flags

- **Price far above the earnings record.** A P/E well above the market's without support in the long-term record (A&P traded at about 30 times earnings in 1961 versus the Dow's 23, then collapsed). Popular stocks whose price rises much faster than profits (Texas Instruments, then Cisco and Microsoft in 2000-02).
- **Paying a high multiple of both earnings and assets.** It leaves the investor dependent on the market's continuing enthusiasm, with no asset backing to fall back on.
- **Paying for an industry's glamour.** Enthusiasm about an industry's growth is usually already in the price, and obvious growth does not guarantee profits (airlines).
- **Implied growth above about 8% a year.** Compute (P/E − 8.5)/2. If the price needs growth the classic method would not capitalize, the margin of safety is gone.
- **Extreme multiples:** P/E above 100, or a market value that is a large multiple of revenue (the 1999-2000 examples ran to dozens of times sales and about 30 times book).
- **Giant size plus a giant multiple.** Flag any mega-cap whose P/E is above about 60. Siegel's test: no company as big as Cisco (about $548 billion and 219 times earnings in March 2000) ever grew fast enough to justify a multiple above 60. Size and a very high multiple cannot both be justified (the Cisco case).
- **Cheap only next to something dearer.** Never call a stock cheap because a peer is even more overvalued. Value each company against its own earnings, assets and cash flow, then compare. In 2000 an analyst preferred Commerce One because its rival Ariba sold at about 400 times revenue; Commerce One then lost 99.7% (the Commerce One case).
- **Forward P/E as the selling point.** Consensus forecasts miss often and widely; compute P/E on trailing average earnings yourself.
- **Goodwill giants.** A price that rests mostly on intangible "earning-power value" rather than tangible assets (the Emerson case): fine for some investors, but recognize the cushion is thinner. Goodwill payback: compute (market value − tangible equity) ÷ normal annual earnings, the years of earnings needed just to cover the premium paid over assets. In 1969 American Home Products and American Hospital Supply together carried almost $5 billion of goodwill valuation in their prices; the question to ask is how many years of excellent earnings it would take to realize that as dividends or tangible assets. The longer the payback, the more the price depends on the distant future.
- **Growth from a tiny base.** Very high growth rates achieved from a small starting profit are hard to keep once the company is large (the Emery case).

## 4. Earnings quality and accounting flags

The rule: the investor must know exactly what earnings figure is being capitalized. The late-1990s cases show how the games worked.

- **Special charges that keep coming.** Big charge-offs bunched in a bad year (the 1970 "big bath"), and "nonrecurring" costs that recur every year (one chipmaker wrote down inventory in quarter after quarter). Treat recurring charges as ordinary costs and average them over the years they relate to.
- **Grab-bag special items.** Conglomerates lumping closures, disposals and reorganizations into one charge that equals a large share of income.
- **Pro forma, "adjusted" or EBITDA earnings in place of net income.** Ignore pro forma figures entirely. Companies published pro forma results that left out preferred dividends, payroll taxes on option exercises, investment losses and billions of merger charges.
- **Changes in revenue recognition.** A switch that books revenue earlier is often the tip of an iceberg (Qwest's directory revenue change preceded a multibillion restatement; Informix booked licenses before resale to end users).
- **Capitalizing operating costs.** Turning ordinary expenses into assets inflates profits now and assets on the balance sheet (Global Crossing; WorldCom). Ask what is capitalized and why.
- **Accounting-policy switches that raise EPS.** Depreciation method, inventory method, pension assumptions, useful-life changes (Trane's depreciation switch).
- **Pension income.** "Net pension benefit" above 5% of net income, or a high or rising assumed return on plan assets, inflates earnings with money the business did not earn. Strip it out of owner earnings.
- **Odd intangible assets inflating equity.** Deferred debt expense, bond-discount assets, large goodwill carried as if it were tangible (the NVF case).
- **Dilution ignored.** Reported EPS that ignores warrants, convertibles and options. A company's own "fully diluted" figure that ignores the market value of warrants. Always use fully diluted shares and add warrant market value to the price (scripts/value_screen.py does this when given the fields).
- **Tax carryforwards treated as permanent earnings.** Profits sheltered by past losses are not normal earning power; tax-adjust them.
- **Earnings boosted by temporary business** (war-related contracts) or by investment gains.
- **Taxes paid far below reported profit for years** (the Penn Central case).
- **Vendor financing, loans to customers**, and reserves that move to smooth earnings.
- **Receivables and inventories outrunning sales.** Compare growth in receivables and inventories with growth in sales over 2-3 years. Flag either one rising much faster than sales, any jump in long-term receivables, or SG&A taking a rising share of revenue. They mean products are being shipped or financed faster than they sell (the Nortel case: in 1999 receivables rose about $1 billion and inventories $1.2 billion, long-term receivables went from $519 million to $1.4 billion, and SG&A rose from 17.6% of revenue in 1997 to 18.7% in 1999).
- **Profits from option tax breaks and investment gains.** Strip tax benefits from employee option exercises, and gains on selling investments, out of operating earnings. In early 2000 about one-third of Cisco's earnings over the prior six months came from tax breaks on option exercises, and it had gained $5.8 billion selling investments (the Cisco case). Modern note: under current U.S. GAAP (since 2017) excess tax benefits on stock compensation pass through income-tax expense, so check the effective-tax-rate reconciliation.
- **Acquisition footnote alarms.** Goodwill from buying businesses with no revenue or customers, "hope chest" goodwill (Lucent booked $4.2 billion of goodwill on Chromatis Networks: 150 employees, no customers, no revenue). Write-offs of purchased in-process R&D, which also shrink future goodwill charges (Tyco, about $545 million over 1997-2001). Available-for-sale securities making up a large share of assets (nearly half of AOL's, $4.9 billion, when it agreed to buy Time Warner).
- **A regulator's inquiry into the accounting.** AOL was under SEC investigation of its marketing-cost accounting, the second time in three years, when the Time Warner deal was announced. Treat an open inquiry as serious until it is resolved.
- **Loan-loss reserves lagging loan growth** at banks and lenders. Compare growth in the loan-loss reserve with loan growth and with the credit cycle. Flag reserves left flat late in an expansion, because defaults jump in recessions (the Capital One caveat, 2000).
- **Operating efficiency far worse than peers.** Compare the company's main efficiency ratio (operating or transportation ratio, margin, cost per unit) with its closest peers. A persistent large gap is a warning even when headline earnings look fine (the Penn Central case: a transportation ratio of 47.5% in 1968 against 35.2% for its neighbor Norfolk & Western).
- **Stale or partial trailing earnings.** Before trusting a very low P/E, confirm it uses the latest reported quarter and fully diluted shares. NVF showed a P/E of 2 at end-1970 on earnings for the 12 months to September; the full year, after dilution and a bad fourth quarter, came to $1.80 a share, so the true multiple was about 5 (the NVF case).
- **Footnotes longer than the statements.** ALCOA's footnotes in 1970 ran about twice as long as the basic figures. Complexity is not proof of wrongdoing, but it is a cost of analysis and a hiding place.
- **Excluding the bad hours.** Counting only the good years, segments or quarters (like King Edward VII's sundial, which marked only the sunny hours).

## 5. Balance sheet and financing flags

- **Overbonding.** Debt that average years cannot cover with ample room (the railroad bankruptcies). Test with the coverage table and the earnings-to-debt alternative (security-analysis.md).
- **Debt outrunning earnings.** From 1950 to 1969 U.S. corporate debt rose about fivefold ($140 billion to $693 billion) while pre-tax profits only about doubled ($43 billion to $91 billion), and pre-tax earnings after interest fell from about 30% of debt to 13.2%. Run the earnings-to-debt test on the trend as well as the latest year: a falling ratio is the warning, even before it crosses the minimum.
- **Weakening liquidity at formerly strong companies.** Falling current ratios and debt growing faster than working capital (by 1970 many Dow companies failed tests they had passed in 1960).
- **Expansion financed by bank loans and long-term debt** that produces operating losses (a pattern since 1966); bank loans ballooning.
- **OPM addict.** Operating cash flow consistently negative while financing cash flow is consistently positive: the company consumes more cash than its business produces. The script flags three or more such years out of five.
- **Debt that cannot be paid from earnings** ("ratio of earnings to fixed charges" below 1).
- **Variable-rate or short-term debt** concentrated where rising rates would hurt.
- **Capital structure as a puzzle.** Many kinds of debt, several warrant series, preferreds with odd terms, treasury-stock deductions exceeding stated capital. The more complicated, the less a lay investor can judge it.
- **A tiny acquirer swallowing a much larger target with paper** (the NVF and Sharon Steel case).
- **Buying in its own warrants or making acquisitions with borrowed money while its bonds sell at distressed prices**.
- **Heavily bonded utilities and pipelines** whose payment "guarantees" are too complex for a defensive investor.

## 6. Growth and acquisition flags

- **Serial acquirer:** averaging more than two or three acquisitions a year, then writing many of them off (Lucent, Mattel, Quaker Oats, Tyco). Organic growth hidden under acquisitions.
- **Growth targets of 15% a year or more.** About 10% pretax (6-7% after tax) may be sustainable for a large company; promises of more are delusional, and the fastest growers tend to flame out.
- **Slipping margins or return on capital underneath rapid revenue growth**.
- **Diversification into unrelated ventures,** and rapid growth in total assets matched by proportional debt (Priceline, Amazon, JDS Uniphase and AOL Time Warner writing off ventures and mergers paid for with overvalued stock).
- **Negative unit economics.** Compute the loss per dollar of revenue. If it is not shrinking, treat revenue growth as faster cash burn, not progress (Commerce One lost about $2 for each $1 of sales in 1999-2000; eToys spent about $2 for each $1 of toys sold). Read the risk-factors section and take a "may never be profitable" warning at face value; Commerce One's own report said exactly that.
- **A "merger of equals" that isn't.** Compare each side's share of the combined company with its share of combined assets, revenue and earnings. AOL holders got 55% of AOL Time Warner although Time Warner had about five times AOL's assets and revenue; the combined company later lost $98.7 billion in 2002, mostly on write-downs of the AOL side (the AOL case).
- **Customer concentration:** one or a few customers supply most revenue (a 1999 IPO valued in the billions on one customer, which later went bankrupt).
- **No R&D at all** where competitors spend on renewal: at least as dangerous as overspending.
- **Earnings that reverse from profit to large loss within a quarter** of a stock offering (the AAA Enterprises case).

## 7. Management, incentives and governance flags

- **Say-do gap.** Compare past annual-report promises with results. Blaming "the economy" or "uncertainty", or a chairman's letter that adopts each year's fad, are warning signs.
- **Pay out of proportion to results.** Huge pay without extraordinary performance; perks granted "without compensation".
- **Option repricing** for insiders (a disgrace at an established company); option grants above about 3% of shares; plans not tied to beating peers over five or more years.
- **Option overhang.** Read the options footnote and count potential new shares (AOL Time Warner had options on about 15% more shares). Value per fully diluted share.
- **Repeated large insider sales** (Form 4). Occasional sales have personal reasons; a steady stream while you buy means management is not your partner.
- **Promoters, not managers.** Managers who always call the stock undervalued, issue quarterly earnings guidance, or flood the wire with hype. Management should try to keep the price from going too high as well as too low.
- **Obscure statements** and executives who say all is well while the business sputters; executive turnover that persists after a new CEO's first year or two.
- **Related-party transactions** in the proxy, especially officers on both sides of a deal (Enron's CFO ran partnerships that bought assets from Enron). Take your money elsewhere.
- **Low payout without results.** Retaining most earnings without a demonstrated, satisfactory rise in per-share earnings. The burden of proof is on the company.
- **Buybacks at high prices** that mainly offset option dilution (one software company issued shares to insiders at a few dollars and bought back shares at several times that). Buybacks are good only when the stock is cheap.
- **Hyped, repeated stock splits**; small stock dividends not backed by reinvested earnings (the A&P case).
- **Results poor on all three classic counts:** unsatisfactory in themselves, worse than comparable companies, and leading to a depressed price for a long time. Shareholders are then entitled to question management (management-and-dividends.md).

## 8. Security-type flags

- **New issues and IPOs.** Sold when conditions favor the seller, with roughly double the selling commission of existing shares and the hardest sell. New issues of small companies in bull markets later fall far below their offering prices. The classic warning about 1960-61 and 1968-69 applied word for word to the 1999-2000 Internet IPOs. Defensive investors avoid them; enterprising ones consider them only years later at bargain prices.
- **IPO proceeds and share reserves.** Read the use-of-proceeds section and the share-reserve note. Flag proceeds spent on loss-making acquisitions, and shares reserved for management well above a few percent of the total (compare the ~3% ceiling on option grants in §7). eToys used IPO money to buy BabyCenter, which lost $4.5 million on $4.8 million of sales, for $205 million, and its 40.6 million reserved shares meant nearly one-third dilution (the eToys case).
- **IPO terms that favor insiders:** a price at a large multiple of earnings and book, insiders selling most of the shares offered (the AAA Enterprises case).
- **Second-grade bonds and preferreds at full price.** Taking a real risk of principal loss for an extra point or two of yield (the "businessman's investment" fallacy) is bad business. Buy them only well below par, at least about 30% under par for high-coupon issues.
- **Straight preferreds.** A weak form: no creditor's claim and capped upside. The question to ask: if the issuer is healthy, why pay a fat preferred dividend instead of issuing tax-deductible bonds?
- **Callable bonds at full price.** The issuer keeps the upside. Prefer call protection for 20-25 years on long issues, or low-coupon bonds bought at a discount.
- **Convertibles issued late in a bull market.** They appear in quantity near market tops, when even poor companies' stocks make conversion look attractive.
- **Large warrant issues and warrant-heavy capitalizations.** When the warrants are worth more than the common stock itself (one conglomerate's three warrant issues outvalued its common), the capital structure is a device, not an investment.
- **Stock-plus-warrant "units."** A broker offering units is close to proof that the deal and the broker should be avoided.
- **Very low-priced stocks** (today around $1 a share and below): costly to trade, laborious to follow, prone to failure. Defensive investors stay out; enterprising ones only as a diversified basket.
- **Foreign bonds and emerging-market bonds at full price** for a defensive investor.
- **Merger arbitrage and special situations** for an individual without the skill and diversification.
- **Letter (restricted) stock** valued at market in a fund's NAV, creating illusory profits.

## 9. Market-environment flags

Use market-level.md for the full tests. Signals that should raise caution on any purchase:
- The five classic signs of a bull-market top: historically high prices, high P/E ratios, low dividend yields against bond yields, much margin speculation, and many new offerings of poor quality (market-level.md §3). The most dependable of them: new stocks of small, unknown companies offered at prices above those of established medium-sized companies.
- A flood of IPOs and new convertibles; the public pouring into small issues.
- Popular formulas and trading systems with spectacular back-tested records: they peak in fashion just as they stop working (core-principles.md: popular mechanical formulas self-destruct).
- Pundits mocking proven value investors at the top, and "this time it's different" explanations for record valuations.
- Companies with soaring prices and no profits.

## 10. Seller, adviser and fund flags

- **Promises.** Spectacular returns, or returns above about 8-10% a year promised in advance.
- **Sales language.** Regulators publish lists of warning phrases. Paraphrased, they fall into these families: certainty ("guaranteed", "can't lose", "no downside"), urgency ("you need to hurry", "you'll be sorry"), exclusivity and secrecy (a proprietary model nobody else has, offshore or "prime bank" schemes), greed appeals ("don't you want to be rich?"), social proof (the smart money is buying, the salesman's own mother owns it), and a plea to focus on performance rather than fees. Repeated use, or pressure, means walk away and consider reporting it.
- **Market-timing or technical-analysis advisers,** and advisers who say "sell" after one bad year.
- **Fees above about 1% a year,** refusal to provide the regulatory disclosure form (Form ADV in the U.S.), or missing disclosure pages.
- **Performance funds.** Managers whose experience covers only one bull market, who define a sound investment as one likely to rise in the next few months, with heavy positions in new ventures at prices far above assets or earnings.
- **Star managers** likely to leave after a hot year; returns that fade as soon as new money arrives; incubated funds and temporary fee waivers; heavy advertising; asset bloat; closet indexing at active fees.
- **Fund sponsors enriching themselves** at investors' expense (a founder sold his management company for many times the fund's assets).
- **Brokers whose capital is in their own speculations** (the 1969-70 back-office and capital crisis).

## 11. The investor's own behavior flags

Flag these when the user describes them; see market-fluctuations.md and core-principles.md.
- Buying on margin, or borrowing to invest: speculation by definition, and it can force a sale at the worst time (margin traders' savings were wiped out this way in the 1907 panic).
- Buying because the price went up, selling because it went down.
- Checking prices many times a day; day trading.
- Mixing speculative and investment money, or topping up the speculative account from investments.
- Acting on tips, forecasts, price targets or "what everyone is buying".
- Concentration in one stock, especially the employer's in a retirement plan (Enron, Global Crossing and WorldCom employees; portfolio-policy.md).
- Buying because others will be forced to buy: index-inclusion runs and small-float squeezes. The buyer is betting on other buyers, not on the business (the Yahoo case: after its addition to the S&P 500 was announced in late 1999, with about 90% of its shares locked up, the stock more than doubled in a month, then lost about 92% over 2000-02).
- Confusing a rising market with personal skill.

## 12. Procedure: reading a report for flags

1. **Gather five years** of annual reports and at least the latest year of quarterly reports, plus the latest proxy statement. Use the regulator's filing system (SEC EDGAR in the U.S.) or the company's investor-relations site.
2. **Read backwards.** Start with the notes at the back; what a company least wants noticed tends to be there.
3. **Read the accounting-policies note** first: revenue recognition, inventories, contract and installment sales, capitalized costs, marketing costs, pensions, stock compensation.
4. **Search for the antennae words:** capitalized, deferred, restructuring, and change words such as began, change, however. Each hit is a question to answer.
5. **Reconcile earnings with cash.** Compare net income with operating cash flow over five years, and both with taxes actually paid. Compute owner earnings (scripts/value_screen.py quality section).
6. **Read the debt and options notes:** maturities, fixed vs floating, covenants; options outstanding, grants per year, repricing.
7. **Read the proxy:** pay, insider ownership, related-party transactions, auditor fees and changes, board independence.
8. **Check insider transactions** (Form 4 in the U.S.) and the shareholder list (institutional ownership above about 60% is a context flag).
9. **Compare footnotes with at least one close competitor** to judge how aggressive the accounting is.
10. **Record flags in the analysis** with source and severity, using the output template's "Risks and red flags" section.

If the user (or you, for lack of data) cannot do steps 1-7, say so: the test is that an investor unwilling to read the proxy and five years of reports should own an index fund rather than individual stocks.

## 14. Translating to today

- **Stock options are now expensed** under U.S. GAAP (since FAS 123R, effective 2006) and IFRS 2. The 2003 complaint that options were "free" in the income statement is dated. The current version of the flag: large stock-based compensation that companies add back in "adjusted" earnings, and buybacks that merely offset it. Subtract stock-based compensation from owner earnings.
- **Pro forma lives on as "adjusted EBITDA", "non-GAAP EPS" and "core earnings."** Regulators now require reconciliation to GAAP; read the reconciliation and treat recurring add-backs (restructuring every year, stock compensation, "acquisition-related" costs at a serial acquirer) as ordinary costs.
- **Revenue recognition** now follows ASC 606 / IFRS 15. The questions are the same: when is revenue booked relative to cash, and did the company change its practice?
- **Pension assumption check:** the 6.5% ceiling was a 2003 judgment. Recalibrate to current high-grade bond yields plus a modest equity premium, and flag assumptions well above that or rising while rates fall.
- **Units and warrants** today appear mainly in special-purpose acquisition companies (SPACs). Apply the classic dilution logic: add warrant value, count sponsor shares, and treat the structure as speculation for a defensive investor.
- **Penny-stock cutoff:** around $1 a share, where exchanges begin delisting procedures. Over-the-counter and pink-sheet markets carry the same cautions.
- **Data sources:** SEC EDGAR full-text search finds antennae words across filings; Form 4 shows insider trades; the proxy is form DEF 14A. Outside the U.S., use the local regulator's filing system and the annual report's notes.
