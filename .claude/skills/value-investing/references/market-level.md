# Judging the General Market Level

Use this file when the question is "Is the stock market cheap, fair or dangerously high?", when deciding whether to tilt toward 25% or 75% stocks, or when someone extrapolates past returns into the future.

## 1. What a market-level judgment is for

- Its only legitimate uses: (a) deciding where to sit inside the 25-75% stock band (portfolio-policy.md); (b) deciding whether to start a new dollar-cost-averaging plan or lump-sum purchase now; (c) tempering expectations. It is never a reason to forecast short-term moves.
- Verdict on such calls: they can be defensible but are less useful than plain counsels — a consistent, controlled common-stock policy and refusing to try to beat the market or pick winners.
- Aristotle's rule: expect only the exactness the subject allows. A financial analyst sits between a mathematician and an orator. Express conclusions as ranges and probabilities.
- If in doubt which course to follow, choose caution. Each investor must weigh the arguments and own the decision.

## 2. The tests, step by step

Run all of them; no single indicator decides.

1. **Price/earnings on multi-year average earnings.** The classic test used the S&P composite's price over 3-year average earnings and over last-year earnings, compared with the same ratios at earlier market dates. The modern equivalent is Shiller's CAPE: price / 10-year average inflation-adjusted earnings. Historically, CAPE well above 20 -> usually poor returns over the next decade; well below 10 -> usually handsome returns. CAPE was 44.2 in December 1999 and 22.8 in early 2003 (still in the danger zone). A rough modern gauge for a P/E: below 10 is low, 10-20 moderate, above 20 expensive. Use it only on normalized (multi-year average) earnings, and always read it against bond yields (test 2): a moderate P/E can still be unattractive when high-grade bonds yield about as much as stocks earn, or more.
2. **Stock earnings yield vs high-grade bond yield.** Earnings yield = E/P (the inverse of P/E). Track the ratio (stock earnings yield / AAA bond yield): about 3.96 in 1948, 3.20 in 1953, 1.41 in 1958, 1.10 in 1963, 0.80 in 1968, 0.72 in 1971. A falling ratio means stocks offer less and less compensation over bonds. In January 1972 this adverse change "fully offset" the better P/E, so the classic verdict judged the market (DJIA about 800-950) unattractive for conservative investment.
3. **Dividend yield vs bond yield.** Ratio fell from about 2.1 (1948: stocks yielded twice bonds) to about 0.41 (1971: bonds yielded more than twice stocks). The classic buying trigger for the cautious investor: stocks' dividend yield back to about two-thirds of the bond yield. Since about 1959 U.S. stocks have yielded less than bonds almost continuously, so today use the dividend comparison as one input, not a literal trigger.
4. **Return on book value and price/book.** The 1971 DJIA: about 10-11% earned on net tangible assets; market 900 vs book 560, so earnings yield on price only about 6.25% (P/E about 18). Rising price/book without rising returns on capital = attitude change, not value.
5. **Price change vs earnings/dividend change over long periods.** DJIA 1949-1969: price up more than five-fold while earnings and dividends roughly doubled = a "bootstrap" rise built on sentiment, implying weaker returns ahead. S&P P/E went from 6.3 (June 1949) to 22.9 (March 1961) while its dividend yield fell from 7% to 3% and bond yields rose 2.60% -> 4.50%: the most remarkable turnabout in market history.
6. **Speculative and new-issue activity.** A flood of low-quality IPOs, small nondescript companies priced above established medium-sized ones, heavy margin buying: see section 3.
7. **Public mood and extrapolation.** Survey expectations rising with prices (Gallup: investors expected 13% a year in mid-1998, over 18% in early 2000, then 7% in 2001-02 when prices were about 50% lower) and institutions raising return assumptions (S&P 500 pension plans assumed a record 9.2% in 2002) are contrary signals.

Interpretation rule: the more the indicators agree that stocks are expensive relative to earnings and to bonds, the closer to the 25% floor a cautious defensive investor may go, and the less sense it makes to start new lump-sum purchases. But never forecast timing: markets can go much higher first, and "heedlessness" can be followed either by a quick replay of a recent decline or by a further boom and a worse collapse.

## 3. Classic danger signals of bull-market tops

Five characteristics common to bull-market tops 1897-1949:
- [ ] Historically high price level
- [ ] High price/earnings ratios
- [ ] Low dividend yields relative to bond yields
- [ ] Much speculation on margin
- [ ] Many offerings of new common-stock issues of poor quality

These old signals did not time the market reliably after 1949, so use them to set caution, not to sell everything. One "fairly dependable sign of the approaching end of a bull swing": new stocks of small, nondescript companies offered at prices higher than many medium-sized companies with long market records. The absence of a new-issue frenzy weakens the case for an imminent top.

Other warning patterns:
- Bull-market amnesia: after about five years of rising prices many investors no longer believe bear markets are possible. Investors who were waiting to buy low feel left behind and capitulate.
- "This time is different" arguments: new-era books (Dow 36,000 etc., 1999), claims that stocks are riskless over long holding periods, so price does not matter. Stocks become **riskier, not safer, as prices rise**.
- The market's disregard of harrowing losses suffered less than a year before (early 1971) is itself a disquieting sign.
- Rule of opposites: at the end of a long poor period (1949) the public has no enthusiasm, and the ground is ripe for a great advance; after a huge rise the extent of the rise may itself show it was overdone.

## 4. Expected-return arithmetic

- Classic rule: stock return ≈ current dividend yield + growth from retained earnings. Late 1971: 3.5% + 4% ≈ 7.5-8% for DJIA-type stocks; no inflation bonus should be added.
- Modern update: stock return ≈ real earnings growth (long-run 1.5-2%/yr) + inflation + dividend yield ± change in valuation ("speculative growth or decline"). Early 2003: about 6% nominal.
- Valuation cross-check from history: starting years with CAPE about 19-24 (1898-1992) produced 10-year nominal returns from 0.1% (1929, CAPE 22.0) to 11.1% (1955, CAPE 18.9), averaging 6.0% from an average CAPE of 20.8. Selected: 1964 CAPE 22.8 -> 1.2%/yr; 1965 23.7 -> 3.3%; 1968 22.3 -> 3.2%; 1972 18.6 -> 6.7%; 1992 20.4 -> 9.3%. Conservative practice: split the difference between the best and worst historical outcomes from similar valuations.
- Do the same exercise with current data before stating expectations: current dividend yield, a modest real growth assumption, current inflation, and a valuation-change scenario. Present a range.
- Forecast humility: the only certainty in forecasting returns is that you will probably be wrong; markets most brutally surprise the most certain. Lower expectations without losing heart: the worse the future looks, the better it usually turns out.

## 5. A record of classic market calls

Use when someone is overconfident about their market view. Even a careful value analyst's calls were mixed:
- 1948, DJIA 180: "not too high" — right.
- 1953, DJIA 275: value favorable but cautious — too cautious (market doubled in five years).
- 1959, DJIA 584: "dangerous" — partly right: 685 in 1961, then 566, then 735, then a 27% drop to 536 by May 1962 (IBM 607 -> 300; hot issues -90%).
- 1964, DJIA 892: "too high"; possibilities ranged from +50% (1350) to -50% (450). Vindicated: 995 in 1966, 632 in 1970; DJIA closed 1970 below its level six years earlier (first time since 1944).
- 1971-72, DJIA 900: unattractive; prepare for difficult times. The 1973-74 bear market followed (-37% including dividends, -47.8% without).
- The classic 1964 policy advice, re-endorsed for 1972, in order of urgency: (1) no borrowing to buy or hold securities; (2) no increase in the stock proportion; (3) reduce stocks where needed to a maximum of 50% of the portfolio, paying capital-gains tax with good grace and putting proceeds in first-quality bonds or savings deposits; (4) existing bona fide DCA plans may continue or pause; do not start a new DCA plan at such levels.

## 6. History: swings, eras, survivorship bias

- Major swings 1871-1971 (S&P composite / DJIA declines): 1929-32 -86% / -89%; 1937-38 -55% / -50%; 1939-42 -44% / -41%; 1946-49 -30% / -24%; 1952-53 -15% / -13%; 1956-57 -24% / -20%; 1961-62 -29% / -27%; 1966-68 high to 1970 low -36% / -37%.
- Market cycles 1897-1949: ten complete cycles; six took 4 years or less, four ran 6-7 years, the 1921-1932 "new era" cycle 11 years. Advances ran 44% to 500% (mostly 50-100%); declines 24% to 89% (mostly 40-50%). Remember that a 50% fall wipes out a 100% gain.
- Three eras of the 20th century: 1900-1924 three-to-five-year cycles, about 3% a year; 1924-1949 about 1.5% a year (boom, collapse, irregular recovery); 1949-1968 the greatest bull market (DJIA 162 -> 995 by 1966, about 11% a year plus 3.5% dividends). 1960s: S&P +5.25% a year, DJIA about 3%.
- Long recoveries: DJIA's September 1929 high (381) was not regained until November 1954; GE and the DJIA took 25 years to recover. With reinvested dividends (over 5.6% a year) returns were positive over that span. Dividends dominate long-run results: $1 in U.S. stocks in 1900 -> $198 by 2000 spending dividends vs $16,797 reinvesting them (Dimson, Marsh, Staunton).
- Decade data 1871-1970: only two decades (1891-1900, 1931-1940) showed lower earnings and average prices; no decade after 1900 showed lower average dividends; growth rates varied widely. History encourages a consistent stock policy but cannot tell you the next decade's gain.
- Survivorship bias: pre-1871 indexes contained as few as seven stocks while about 300 companies existed by 1800 and most failed. Siegel's 1802-1870 real returns (stocks 7.0%, bonds 4.8%, cash 5.1%) overstate stocks by at least 2 points a year per Dimson et al., so stocks then did no better than bonds or cash. Even 1871-1920s indexes are overstated by 1-2 points. Never claim history proves stocks always beat bonds.
- Since profits are finite, the justified price for stocks is finite; a stock's or market's future return is always a function of the price paid (paying a star athlete 10 or 1,000 times his worth is not justified by his greatness).
