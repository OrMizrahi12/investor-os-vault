# Case Studies: The Four Extremes, the Eight Pairs, and Their Modern Equivalents

Use this file when you need a concrete precedent for a situation you are analyzing (a giant with thin coverage, a serial acquirer, a tiny company swallowing a big one, a hot IPO, or two similar-looking stocks at very different prices), with the numbers and the transferable lesson. ## 1. How to use a case

- Match the situation to a lesson group below, then run that group's checklist on the live company.
- The moral for all four extreme cases: do the elementary work (coverage, taxes paid, balance-sheet quality, dilution) before studying market movements, forecasts or elaborate mathematics.
- The frame: the four extremes are an overpriced tottering giant, an empire-building conglomerate, a minnow swallowing a whale, and an IPO of a basically worthless company; every bubble produces new examples of all four.

## 2. Inadequate coverage in a giant

### Penn Central: the executable checklist
Run every item; any one failure should have removed the bonds and shares from a professionally supervised account by 1968 at the latest.
- [ ] **Interest coverage vs standard.** Penn Central earned interest 1.91x (1967) and 1.98x (1968). Classic railroad-bond minimum: 5x before income taxes, or 2.9x after taxes at regular rates. FAIL.
- [ ] **Taxes actually paid.** It paid essentially no income tax for 11 years, so the after-tax test is meaningless and pretax coverage (< 2x) applies. A company reporting profits but paying no tax for years: question the earnings. FAIL.
- [ ] **Swap test.** In 1969 Pennsylvania RR 4.5s of 1994 ranged 61-74.5 while Pennsylvania Electric 4 3/8s of 1994 ranged 64.25-72.25; the utility covered interest 4.20x pretax in 1968 vs 1.98x. An exchange at no sacrifice of price or income was available and would have saved the holder: end-1970 the railroad bonds were in default at 18.5; the utility bonds at 66.5. Always check whether a better-secured issue with the same coupon, maturity and price exists.
- [ ] **Valuation vs untaxed earnings.** 1968 EPS $3.80; high 86.5 = 24x earnings that bore no tax. FAIL.
- [ ] **Special charges vs headline earnings.** The newly merged company reported $6.80/share for 1966 (stock later peaked at 86.5, over $2 billion of equity value) while a $275 million ($12/share) charge for merger "costs and losses" was set for 1971. Add charges back against earnings. FAIL.
- [ ] **Operating efficiency vs peers.** Transportation (operating) ratio 47.5% in 1968 vs 35.2% for Norfolk & Western. FAIL. (Modern update: today a railroad ratio of 70% is considered excellent; the ratio's level has changed, the peer comparison has not.)
- [ ] **Odd transactions with peculiar accounting** (see Briloff's 1971 Barron's analysis). FAIL.
- Outcome: stock 86.5 (1968) to 5.5 (1970); bankruptcy June 1970; the shares were expected to be wiped out. Modern update: the stock closed at $11.25 on the Friday before the weekend filing, hardly a going-out-of-business price; Enron and WorldCom also sold relatively high just before bankruptcy.
- Lesson: size and reputation are no substitute for coverage; market price is no signal of solvency.

### Lucent Technologies
- Mid-2000: most widely held U.S. stock, $192.9 billion market value (12th largest).
- Quarter to June 30, 2000 vs 1999 ($ millions): revenue 8,713 vs 7,403; continuing operations (14) vs 622; discontinued (287) vs 141; net income (301) vs 763; cash 710 vs 1,495; receivables 10,101 vs 9,486; goodwill 8,736 vs 3,340 (1999 figure is all other assets incl. goodwill); capitalized software 576 vs 412; total assets 46,340 vs 37,156.
- Footnote flags: Chromatis Networks bought for $4.8 billion, $4.2 billion of it goodwill, for a firm with 150 employees, no customers and no revenue; $1.5 billion lent to customers plus $350 million of guarantees on their borrowings, doubled in a year (customers running out of cash); software development costs capitalized instead of expensed.
- Outcome: Chromatis shut in Aug 2001 (reportedly 2 customers); losses $16.2 billion (FY2001) and $11.9 billion (FY2002), including $3.5 billion of bad-debt and customer-financing provisions, $4.1 billion of goodwill impairment and $362 million of capitalized-software charges. Stock $51.06 to $1.26: about $190 billion of value lost in 2.5 years.
- Lesson: vendor financing, capitalized expenses and acquired "hope chest" goodwill are coverage failures in disguise.

Narrative facts: sales $7 million (1958), 20x by 1960, 20x again to $2.8 billion by 1968; debt from $44 million to $1,653 million (1968) and $1,865 million (1969); bank loans $161 million end-1967 to $414 million a year later plus $1,237 million long-term debt; combined debt about $1,869 million in 1969, perhaps the largest of any industrial except Standard Oil of N.J.; 1970 net loss close to $70 million; the largest bond issue at one point quoted at 15 cents on the dollar; 1969-70 losses exceeded all profits since formation.
Specific tells:
- 1961 "big bath": a small operating deficit year loaded with about $13 million of charges and reserves (more than the prior three years' combined profits), setting up "record earnings" in 1962.
- End-1966 net tangible assets $7.66/share; 1967 price reached 22x that.
- End-1968 balance sheet: $286 million for 3,800,000 common and Class AA shares (about $77/share); deducting preferred at full value and excluding goodwill and a huge "bond-discount asset" left $13 million, about $3/share, later wiped out. (Note: the bond-discount asset treated bonds bought below par as if they could be sold at par; if they can only be sold below par it is really a liability.)
- Late 1967 two top banking firms sold 600,000 shares at $111; under 3 years later 7.125.
- Moral: coverage, current ratio and equity-to-debt failed conservative standards by 1966, yet banks lent about $400 million more in two years for "diversification." Modern update: banks keep funding such expansions (Enron, WorldCom); founder James Ling paid for dozens of companies with LTV stock, the rising stock enabling more deals, until LTV was 14th on the Fortune 500 (1969); LTV (by then a steelmaker) filed for bankruptcy in late 2000.
- Lesson: serial acquirers paying with their own stock and debt nearly always end badly; check coverage trend and tangible equity after removing acquisition intangibles.

### Tyco International
- 1997-2001: over $37 billion spent on acquisitions, mostly in stock; about 200 companies in FY2000 alone. Revenue $7.6 billion to $34 billion; operating income from a $476 million loss to a $6.2 billion profit; market value $114 billion at end-2001.
- Acquisition-related charges ($ millions; the company's labels): "merger/restructuring/other nonrecurring" 1997 918, 1998 0, 1999 1,183, 2000 175, 2001 234, total 2,510; impairment of long-lived assets 148, 0, 335, 99, 120, total 702; write-offs of purchased in-process R&D 361, 0, 0, 0, 184, total 545. (175 for 2000 reconciles to the stated total.)
- "Nonrecurring" charges in 4 of 5 years, about $750 million a year of cleanup; the SEC review in 1999 added $257 million of such charges to 1998 retroactively and recast 1999. Writing off in-process R&D up front shrank goodwill and future amortization.
- Questions to ask: if growth by acquisition is so good, why the constant cleanup? If buying companies is the business, why are its costs "nonrecurring"? Who can forecast earnings?
- Outcome: FY2002 loss $9.4 billion; stock $58.90 to $17.08 (-71% in a year); former CEO charged in 2002 with tax fraud and diversion of company assets.
- Lesson: treat recurring "one-time" charges as operating costs; average them into earnings.

## 4. Minnow swallows whale

### NVF takeover of Sharon Steel
- End-1968 sizes: NVF long-term debt $4.6 million, stock capital $17.4 million, sales $31 million, net $502,000 (before a $374,000 special credit), "vulcanized fiber and plastics." Sharon: LTD $43 million, stock capital $101 million, sales $219 million, net $2,929,000: seven times NVF.
- Offer (early 1969) per Sharon share: $70 face of NVF junior 5% bonds due 1994 + warrants for 1.5 NVF shares at $22. NVF got 88% despite Sharon's resistance, issuing $102 million of bonds and warrants for 2,197,000 shares.
- Pro forma 1968 at 100%: debt $163 million, tangible stock capital only $2.2 million, sales $250 million; a loss of 50 cents/share before an extraordinary credit, 3 cents profit after.
- The new bonds never sold above 42 in the year of issue (grave doubt about safety), yet management used that low price to save about $1 million a year in taxes (mechanics: superinvestors-and-speculation.md, the NVF tax case).
- Balance-sheet adjustment: the 1968 report showed a $58.6 million "deferred debt expense" asset, larger than the $40.2 million stockholders' equity, and kept a $20.7 million "excess of equity over cost of investment in Sharon" outside equity. Drop the first, include the second: real tangible equity $2.2 million. NVF's real equity went from $17.4 million ($23.71/share) to about $3/share on 731,000 shares; holders also gave away rights to buy 3.5 times as many shares at 6 points below the end-1968 price; warrants worth about $12 each (about $30 million) exceeded the market value of all NVF stock.
- Further tells: 1969 interest $7.5 million plus $1.795 million "amortization of deferred debt expense," nearly offset by a $1.65 million credit for "amortization of equity over cost"; $22.129 million of "fair market value of warrants" placed in stock capital; just after year-end NVF bought in 650,000 warrants at $9.38 (about $6 million, evidently borrowed) with only $700,000 cash and $4.4 million of debt due in 1970, while its bonds sold below 40; retired $5.1 million of bonds and 253,000 warrants for common; sold warrants to employees on 5%-down installment terms; Sharon changed pension accounting and lowered depreciation, adding about $1/share to NVF's earnings; end-1970 the Stock Guide showed P/E 2 (8.75 vs $5.38 of 12-month "earnings," really 1.6x) ignoring dilution and a bad fourth quarter; actual 1970 EPS $2.03 undiluted, $1.80 diluted; stock plus warrants worth about $14 million against $135 million of bonds.
- Modern update: in 1972 a federal judge found NVF's chairman Victor Posner had improperly used Sharon's pension assets for affiliates' takeovers; in 1977 the SEC obtained an injunction alleging $1.7 million of personal perks, a $13.9 million overstatement of pretax earnings and shifting of income between years.
- Lesson: when the acquirer is far smaller than the target, check post-deal tangible equity, debt coverage, warrant overhang, and accounting changes at the acquired company.

### AOL / Time Warner
- Announced Jan 10, 2000 at $156 billion. AOL: assets $10.3 billion, revenue $5.7 billion, stock at 164x earnings. Time Warner: assets $51.2 billion, revenue $27.3 billion, about 50x.
- Tells: a "merger of equals" giving AOL holders 55% although Time Warner was about five times bigger; a second SEC inquiry in three years into AOL's marketing-cost accounting; nearly half of AOL's assets ($4.9 billion) were available-for-sale equity securities exposed to tech prices.
- Outcome: closed Jan 11, 2001; losses $4.9 billion (2001) and $98.7 billion (2002), the largest ever, mostly AOL write-downs; holders down about 80% from announcement.
- Lesson: compare relative size with the ownership split; acquirers paying with overvalued stock transfer the overvaluation into write-offs.

## 5. Hot IPO of a near-worthless business

### AAA Enterprises
- 1965 incorporation: $5,800,000 of mobile-home sales, $61,000 pretax. By 1968 franchising mobile-home dealerships and "Mr. Tax of America" tax-preparation franchises run from trailers; shares multiplied to 2,710,000.
- March 1969: a major exchange house and others sold 500,000 shares at $13 (300,000 for the founder, 200,000 for the company, raising $2,400,000). Stock doubled to 28: $84 million for a company with book value about $4,200,000 and maximum earnings $690,000 = 115x its best earnings. The founder's shares had $180,000 of book value; he netted about $3,600,000 (20x). Underwriters split about $500,000.
- Public paid about 10x book after its own money lifted equity per share from 59 cents to $1.35; pre-1968 earnings had peaked at 7 cents/share.
- 1969: carpet stores and a mobile-home plant added; 9 months showed 22 cents vs 14 cents; then a $4,365,000 loss ($1.49/share) wiped out pre-IPO capital, the $2,400,000 raised and two-thirds of reported 9-month earnings, leaving $242,000 (8 cents/share) seven months after the offering. Year-end bid still 8.125 (over $25 million). Earning $686,000 Jan-Sept and then losing $4,365,000 in three months implies the September report was badly wrong; houses tied to a company should see monthly figures.
- 1970: another $1 million loss in the first half, capital deficit, founder's loans up to $2,500,000; bankruptcy petition Jan 1971 with the stock still bid at 50 cents ($1,500,000 for the issue).
- Questions it raises: should reputable houses refuse such deals (perhaps 9 of 10 foredoomed)? Should the SEC have powers beyond disclosure, publish a box score of offerings by type, or require a warranty that the offering price is not far out of line with comparable seasoned issues? Reform is hard because the abuses come from the public's own heedlessness and greed. Modern update: the lessons on speculation, Mr. Market and the margin of safety are the only real defense; investment banks cannot take credit for first-day pops without blame for later collapses (many 1999-2000 IPOs rose 1,000%+, most then lost 95%+; many were deliberately underpriced to manufacture first-day gains).
- Lesson: an offering priced at many times book on one good year, with the insider selling most of the shares, is speculation regardless of who underwrites it.

### eToys
- May 20, 1999: 8% of the company (8,320,000 shares at $20, $166.4 million) underwritten by four top banks; first-day close $76.5625 (+282.8%); 102 million shares = $7.8 billion.
- Business: 20 months of sales $30.6 million vs net loss $30.8 million ($2 spent per $1 of toys sold); proceeds partly to buy BabyCenter (lost $4.5 million on $4.8 million sales) for $205 million; 40.6 million shares reserved for management (102 to 143 million shares, nearly one-third dilution).
- Rival comparison ($ millions): eToys FY to 3/31/1999 vs Toys "R" Us quarter to 5/1/1999: sales 30 vs 2,166; net income (29) vs 27; cash 20 vs 289; total assets 31 vs 8,067; market value 7,780 vs 5,650. Toys "R" Us sold over 70x more in a quarter than eToys in a year.
- Outcome: bankruptcy March 7, 2001 after $398 million+ of losses; peak $86 (Oct 1999), last trade a penny.
- Lesson: compare the newcomer's market value with an established rival's sales, profits and assets; the key to a good IPO for investors is earnings, not branding.

### Others: VA Linux (enterprising-selection.md, new issues); Aetna Maintenance (superinvestors-and-speculation.md, the Aetna Maintenance case); InfoSpace: opened $31.25 (split-adjusted) in Dec 1998, peaked $1,305.32 in March 2000, ended 2002 at $8.45; ZZZZ Best (late 1980s, about $200 million market value for a phone and a rented office; founder jailed).

## 6. Pair comparisons: the grid and the eight classic pairs

The grid (use it for any comparison): price; shares (assume preferred converted); market value; warrants' market value; debt; preferred; total capitalization at market; book value/share; sales; net income; EPS now, 5 and 10 years earlier; dividend rate; dividends paid since; P/E; price/book; dividend yield; net/sales (return on sales); earnings/book (return on capital); current ratio; working capital/debt; interest coverage; EPS growth over 5 and 10 years.

### Pair 1: Real Estate Investment Trust vs Realty Equities Corp. of N.Y.
Trust: New England trust, three trustees, nearly a century old, dividends since 1889, moderate growth, manageable debt. Realty Equities: assets $6.2 million to $154 million in 8 years with debt in proportion; racetracks, 74 movie theaters, literary agencies, a PR firm, hotels, supermarkets, 26% of a cosmetics firm that went bankrupt in 1970; a $7-dividend preferred with $1 par carried as a $1 liability; treasury-stock deduction ($5.5 million for 209,000 shares) exceeding stated common capital ($2.5 million); 3 warrant series for 1,578,000 shares; 6+ kinds of debt over $100 million.

Sequel: Equities lost $13.2 million ($5.17/share, incl. an $8.8 million reserve) in the year to March 1970, still declared an extra 5-cent dividend, auditors refused to certify, AMEX suspended it, bid under $2; later fraud suit against its accountants. The Trust: 1970 low 16.5, about 26.6 in early 1971, EPS $1.50, slightly above book of $21.60; honestly and well served. Verdict: the market ignores stable prudence and chases gadget-laden expansion.

Ten-year record (EPS, price range): Whiting (FY to April 30) 1961 $.42 (12.5-7.75) rising to 1968 $3.63 (43.125-28.25), 1969 $2.63 (37-17.75), 1970 $1.81 (22.5-16.25). Willcox & Gibbs EPS 3-47 cents throughout (1970 $.34), price swinging 3.75-20.625. Whiting (1896) stuck to materials handling; W&G (1866, industrial sewing machines) diversified into 24+ subsidiaries, "small potatoes." Whiting's EPS growth from 41 cents (1960) to $3.63 (1968) carried no guarantee; EPS fell to $1.77 (12 months to Jan 1971) and the stock fell about 60% from its 1968 high. Verdict: Whiting a sound, attractive secondary investment for the enterprising investor as part of a group. Sequel: W&G small 1970 loss, low 4.5, rebound to 9.5 (statistically unjustifiable); Whiting fell only to 16.75 (about its current assets), EPS held at $1.85, rose to 24.5 in early 1971: reasonable, no longer a bargain.

Lessons: almost every stock is a bargain at one time and expensive at another; investors price companies by emotional imagery (the affect heuristic); a company can be a giant or deserve a giant P/E, not both (Siegel: no company that large ever grew fast enough to justify a P/E above 60); in the short run the market is a voting machine, in the long run a weighing machine.

## 8. What the pairs teach (general observations)

- Higher growth and profitability logically earn higher multipliers; whether a specific P/E gap is justified usually cannot be known with confidence.
- Confident judgments ARE possible in two zones: (1) heavy market activity in companies of questionable soundness (speculative and plainly overvalued); (2) "underspeculation," i.e., neglect or undue pessimism after an earnings dip, leaving issues worth more than their price.
- Expect large declines: most of the 16 stocks fell sharply in both 1961-62 and 1969-70 (examples: National General 60.5 to 9, McGraw-Hill 54.625 to 10.25, Realty Equities 37.75 to 2, Air Reduction 37 to 16, Whiting 43.375 to 16.75; even the REIT 30.25 to 16.375 and Home Products 72 to 51.125).
- McGraw-Hill moved at least 3-to-2 (up or down) from one year to the next in each of 13 years, e.g., 54.5 to 112 (1966-67), 39.5 to 10 (1969-70), 10 to 24.125 (1970-71); National General swung that much both ways in every two-year period.
- The analyst's proper job: not to pick the best future market performers but to find the minority cases where he can confidently judge price to be well below value, with enough skill to get good average results.

## 10. Translating to today

- The specific companies and 1968-71 prices are history; the diagnostic patterns recur in every boom: (a) giants with weak coverage or vendor financing; (b) serial acquirers with recurring charges; (c) small acquirers issuing paper for large targets; (d) IPOs valued at many times sales with insiders selling.
- The classic railroad coverage minimums (5x pretax) and transportation ratios are industry-specific 1970s standards; use the coverage table in security-analysis.md and compare operating ratios with current peers.
- When rebuilding a paired-comparison grid today, add: stock-based compensation and option overhang, goodwill and intangibles as a % of equity, lease and pension liabilities in debt, and multiyear free cash flow.
- The 2000-2002 outcomes illustrate one cycle; do not treat the specific magnitudes as forecasts.
